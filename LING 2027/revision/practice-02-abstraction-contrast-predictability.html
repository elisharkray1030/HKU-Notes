LING 2027 Practice Revision hub

Abstraction, contrast, predictability: practice

Drills on the four moves the course teaches for phonemic analysis — look for minimal pairs, describe the environments, generalise into natural classes, decide the underlying form — run on invented languages, so that English cannot answer for you. Then the three relations that trip people up (contrast, complementary distribution, free variation) and the two complications in §10.3: when no allophone is "basic", and when the data genuinely will not decide. One exercise here has no settled answer, and saying so is the answer.

Background: Abstraction, contrast, predictability.

Exam shape

Topic 2 is the second item on the published midterm scope — "Abstraction, contrast, predictability" (Topic 4, p. 31). The shape of a good answer is the one Tutorial C drills: Step 1, look for minimal pairs; Step 2, describe the phonological conditions for each sound in each word; Step 3, generalise the patterns, "DO NOT be too specific or overgeneralize"; Step 4, determine the underlying representation, "the one with less predictability and wider distribution (elsewhere)" (Tutorial C, pp. 14–15). Every exercise below is built to be answered in those four moves, in that order, with the data quoted at each move. No weighting or mark allocation is published anywhere in the course materials, so none is quoted here.

Quiz

Q1

Language J has pata 'moon', bata 'sun', paka 'star', pat 'cloud', pada 'river'. Which pair shows that [p] and [b] belong to separate phonemes?

A minimal pair is "two words that differ in only a single sound in the same position" (Ch. 10, p. 204) — and the pair has to isolate the sounds you are arguing about. pata/bata differs in one segment, in the same slot, with a meaning difference, so it proves /p/ and /b/ contrast. pata/paka is also a minimal pair, but it isolates [t] against [k], not [p] against [b]: the wrong answer to this question though a true minimal pair. pata/pat differs by a whole segment being absent, and pata/pada isolates [t] against [d]. The lesson: "there is a minimal pair" is not the same claim as "there is a minimal pair for this contrast".

Q2

Language V has [n] in kanaka 'bird', sanati 'water', minito 'fish', and [ɳ] in kaɳata 'stone', saɳaku 'hand', miɳitu 'house'. There is no minimal pair. What follows?

This is the Venda argument, which Zsiga builds precisely to break the habit in option 2. First, the environments overlap: [n] occurs between [a] and [a] (kanaka) and so does [ɳ] (kaɳata), and both also occur between [i] and [i], so there is no complementary distribution to make the choice predictable. Second, "if you can collect a number of near-minimal pairs, each of which differ in a different way, you can still be on firm ground in arguing that a distribution is unpredictable and contrastive" (Ch. 10, p. 211) — here one pair differs in the consonant after the nasal (kanaka/kaɳata), another in the final vowel (minito/miɳitu), and the third in both, so no single context is doing the work. Conclusion: different phonemes, even with no minimal pair. Option 3 misuses free variation (that requires the same context and no meaning difference); option 4 is the pessimism the chapter exists to refute.

Q3

In Language G, [t] and [d] contrast: mata 'kill' and mada 'tie' are different words. But between vowels, when the second is unstressed, both are pronounced [ɾ], so both words surface as [ˈmaɾa]. Which term describes the behaviour of [t] and [d] here?

"[T]he most complicated and confusing distribution may be positional neutralization. In this case, two sounds are contrastive in one position, and not contrastive in another. The two sounds still belong to two different phonemes: if minimal pairs exist, the sounds are capable of bearing contrast. But that contrast may be neutralized in certain positions" (Ch. 10, p. 214). English "kitty"/"kiddy" and "writer"/"rider" is the book's own case. Option 2 is wrong on the data: the sounds do occur in the same environment — that is exactly why the words collide. Option 3 is wrong because free variation is a fact about one word's pronunciation, not about a contrast disappearing in a position. Option 4 gets the surface right and the analysis wrong: the phonemes are still two, which is why "kill" and "tie" are different words everywhere else.

Q4

Consonants of Language B: p t k b d g m n ŋ f s ʃ h l ɾ w j. Vowels: i ɪ e ɛ æ a ɔ o ʊ u. Which of these sets is a natural class?

{p t k} is definable by features — voiceless plosives, [−voice, −continuant] — and every member shares them. {p m s} shares nothing: labial stop, nasal, alveolar fricative. {b l ʃ} shares nothing either: voiced labial stop, voiced lateral, voiceless fricative. {a ɔ o} differs in both height and rounding, so it is a list, not a class. The point is the chapter's: "Phonological distributions depend on natural classes: sets of sounds that have something in common", and where the complement set has no property in common it is "the elsewhere case" (Ch. 10, p. 209). A rule can be written with a list as its environment, but then it is a list and not a generalisation — which is what Step 3 of the procedure is there to prevent.

Q5

English [l] occurs in syllable onsets ("leaf") and [ɫ] in syllable codas ("feel"), with no minimal pairs. Which one is the basic allophone?

Zsiga's own wording: "It is equally simple to say /l/ becomes [ɫ] in the syllable coda, or /[ɫ]/ becomes [l] in the syllable onset." And she names the temptation in option 2 and rejects it: "We might have a tendency to choose [l] with the idea that it's a simpler sound, but our intuition is really more that [l] is a more familiar symbol. In terms of articulation, the two sounds are equally complex" (p. 211). The alternative she offers is to refuse to single one out: "One could just call the 'family' of [l] and [ɫ] the 'lateral' family. One member of the family does not have to be singled out as most important." So the honest answer states the distribution, says the choice is not determined, and then either picks one as a labelled convention or uses the abstract family. Option 4 confuses "no minimal pair" with "no allophony" — the same error as Q2, running the other way.

Pitfall

"I couldn't find a minimal pair, so they're in complementary distribution, so they're allophones" is the three-step fall that loses the most marks on this topic. Each link is a separate claim and each can fail. No minimal pair does not entail complementary distribution: the environments can overlap, as in Language V, and then the sounds are separate phonemes. Complementary distribution does not entail allophony: English [h] and [ŋ] never share a context and are not allophones of one phoneme. Write the four steps out and let each one be answered by data you can point at — that is what makes the answer checkable, and checkable is what gets the marks.

Guided exercises

Exercise 1 skill: naming the relation, then deciding whether allophony follows easy

Six short cases. For each, answer two questions separately: (i) what is the distribution — contrast, complementary distribution, free variation, or positional neutralization? (ii) does the case establish that the two sounds are allophones of one phoneme? Do not let the answer to (i) settle (ii) by itself; that is the whole exercise.

the case(i) distribution(ii) allophones?
ALanguage J: pata 'moon', bata 'sun'.
BEnglish [l] in onsets ("leaf"), [ɫ] in codas ("feel"); no minimal pairs.
CA speaker of Language C says father as [ˈtata] or [ˈtaθa], in the same phrase, on different occasions, with no difference in meaning; other speakers use only one.
DLanguage G: [t] and [d] contrast, but both surface as [ɾ] between vowels when the second is unstressed, making 'kill' and 'tie' homophonous.
EEnglish [h] occurs only in syllable onsets, [ŋ] only in codas; no minimal pairs; no related pair of words ever shows one where the other might be.
FLanguage V: [n] between [a] and [a] and between [i] and [i]; [ɳ] in the same two environments; no minimal pairs.
Step 1: ask whether the two sounds can occur in the same environment

If they can, and the meaning changes, you have contrast. If they can, and the meaning does not change, you have free variation. If they cannot, you have complementary distribution — and only then do you go on to ask about allophony. Positional neutralization is the fourth possibility: both, depending on position.

Step 2: for complementary cases, ask what else supports allophony

Two things can support it: phonetic similarity, and alternations — the same morpheme showing one sound in one form and the other in another. Zsiga is explicit that neither is decisive on its own, but their absence is telling.

Step 3: for the overlapping case, do not settle for "unpredictable"

Say what that means for phoneme status, and back it with the near-minimal pairs rather than with the bare absence of minimal pairs.

Full answer

A — contrast; different phonemes. One minimal pair, one segment apart, different meanings: the definition of contrast (Ch. 10, p. 204). There is nothing left to argue.

B — complementary distribution; allophones, but no basic form. [l] and [ɫ] never share a context, speakers treat them as "two versions of the same sound", so they belong to one phoneme (Ch. 10, p. 211). Which is basic is a separate question, and the answer is that the data do not decide it — that is Q5.

C — free variation; one phoneme, and the choice is not predictable. "[S]ounds that are in free variation occur in the same context, and thus are not predictable, but the difference between the two sounds does not change one word into another" (Ch. 10, p. 213). The course's slide marks the same point with an asterisk: allophones "never appear in the same phonetic context *", and the asterisk says "*except in cases of free variation", citing "data" as /ˈdætə/ or /ˈdeɪtə/ (Tutorial C, p. 6). Both variants are allophones of one phoneme because they count as the same sound to the speaker; what fails is predictability, not phonemehood.

D — positional neutralization; two phonemes still. The contrast exists elsewhere, so /t/ and /d/ are separate; it is neutralized in one position (Ch. 10, p. 214). This is the case where the surface forms are identical and the analysis still says "two phonemes" — and Zsiga adds the caution that the neutralization may not be complete, since the preceding vowel can differ in length (p. 214).

E — complementary distribution; allophony NOT established. This is the pair that breaks the inference. The distribution is as tidy as in B, but "[i]t is true that [h] and [ŋ] are not phonetically similar. But more importantly, there are no sets of related words where one form of the word has [h] and another form has [ŋ]. That is, an allophonic relationship is not supported by alternations" (Ch. 10, p. 213). So the right answer to (ii) is "no, and the distribution alone cannot show it".

F — neither contrast-by-minimal-pair nor complementary; different phonemes. Both occur between [a] and [a] and both between [i] and [i], so the distribution is not complementary and the choice is not predictable; the near-minimal pairs differ in different ways, which closes off the alternatives one at a time (Ch. 10, pp. 210–211).

The comparison the exercise is for. B and E have the same distribution shape and opposite conclusions; C and E both fail predictability and reach different conclusions; D and A both have contrast and differ in whether it survives everywhere. No single distributional fact carries the analysis. What carries it is distribution plus similarity plus alternation plus the presence or absence of minimal pairs.

Reasoning chain: same environment? → meaning changes? → if not, free variation or neutralization depending on whether the position matters → if never the same environment, ask what else supports allophony → state the conclusion with the evidence that supports it and the evidence that is missing.

Step 1: look for minimal pairs

Minimal pairs would make the prefix shapes separate morphemes and end the analysis. Check honestly, and check for repeats in the table while you are counting.

Step 2: describe the environment of each shape

Write the first segment of the root after ___ for each row, then group the rows by the shape the prefix takes. The phonetic quality of the nasal matters; the rest of the root should not, and you should check that it does not.

Step 3: generalise the environments into natural classes

Each group of environments has to be nameable by features. If one group cannot be named, that is a result, not a failure — it is the elsewhere case.

Step 4: determine the underlying representation

Apply the elsewhere test: the shape whose conditioning set is the most heterogeneous, the one left when no rule applies. Frequency is not the test, and neither is which shape is listed first.

Full answer

Step 1. No minimal pairs. The prefix never distinguishes two words: every root takes exactly one shape, and the shape is fixed by the root's first segment. There is also one duplicated row — [nˈtali] "my rope" appears twice, in the third row and the last. That is eight distinct roots, not nine, and the duplicate contributes nothing new to any environment list.

Step 2. Environments.

[m] / ___ p, b · [n] / ___ t, d, s, a · [ŋ] / ___ k, g.

Three shapes, three disjoint sets: no root-initial segment appears under two shapes, and every root in the table is accounted for. Check that nothing else in the root predicts the shape: pata and tali both have [a] as their first vowel, and buka and duma both have [u] as theirs — the first vowel predicts nothing, while the first consonant predicts everything.

Step 3. Generalisation. [m] before labial plosives {p, b}. [ŋ] before velar plosives {k, g}. [n] before alveolars {t, d, s} and before a vowel. The first two are natural classes; the third is not one class at all, and that is the diagnostic. A nasal that agrees in place with a following plosive is the generalisation; before a vowel there is no following plosive to agree with, so nothing happens and the nasal keeps its own place.

Step 4. UR = /n/. {t, d, s, a} — coronals plus a vowel — is the set with nothing in common, so [n] is the elsewhere case. The criterion is the chapter's own: the complement set "does not have any property in common: we can call this the elsewhere case" (Ch. 10, p. 209), which is the same reasoning that makes [o] basic in the Swahili example. {p, b} and {k, g} are both nameable, so both are derived.

The rule. /n/ is realized as [m] before a labial plosive and as [ŋ] before a velar plosive; [n] elsewhere. In the general form the chapter uses: a nasal agrees in place of articulation with a following plosive.

The two traps, named. (i) The duplicated row is a counting trap: an answer that says "nine roots" or that writes t___ twice as though it were two environments has been caught. (ii) ata → [nˈata] is the item a naive version of the rule excludes. If you generalise as "[n] before alveolars" you have described a narrower environment than the data show — {t, d, s} is a class, but not the one [n] is conditioned by — and you have no account of the vowel-initial root — which is precisely the row that makes [n] the elsewhere case rather than one more assimilation. The vowel-initial root is not an oddity to be left out; it is the evidence.

One thing not to claim. The data contain no root beginning with a labiodental or a palatal, so you cannot say what the prefix would do there. Say that the pattern is established for the places that occur and leave the rest open. Overgeneralising here is the error Tutorial C warns against at Step 3.

Reasoning chain: count roots and find the duplicate → no minimal pairs → list the segment after the prefix for every root → group the groups → name the two nameable groups and note that the third is not nameable → pick the unnameable one as the elsewhere case → write the rule in the "realized as … in environment …" form.

Exercise 3 skill: free variation beside complementary distribution, in one language medium

Language F is constructed for this drill. It shows two different relations at once, and the exercise is to keep them apart.

factdata
'dog'[ˈkaɾo] or [ˈkaro], same speaker, same phrase, either is accepted; no difference in meaning
'house'[ˈsoma]
'rain'[ˈsuka]
'stone'[ˈsata]
'bird'[ˈθima]
'road'[ˈθela]

(1) Name the relation between [ɾ] and [r]. (2) Name the relation between [s] and [θ], and list both environments. (3) In each case, is the choice predictable? (4) In each case, are the two sounds allophones of one phoneme? (5) The course's slide defines complementary distribution as "allophones never appear in the same phonetic context", then adds an asterisk. What is the asterisk for, and which case here needs it?

Step 1: separate the two pairs before analysing either

[ɾ]/[r] is one question, [s]/[θ] is another. Do not merge them because they are in the same language; a language can have several relations at once, and each is established separately.

Step 2: ask the same-environment question for each pair

Can both members occur in the identical context? For [ɾ]/[r] the answer is yes and the meaning does not change. For [s]/[θ], check the segments either side of each occurrence.

Step 3: decide what each relation implies about the phoneme

Predictability and phonemehood are two different questions. One of these pairs is unpredictable and still one phoneme; the other is predictable and one phoneme for a different reason.

Full answer

1. Free variation. Same context, both accepted, no meaning difference: the chapter's definition exactly — "sounds that are in free variation occur in the same context, and thus are not predictable, but the difference between the two sounds does not change one word into another" (Ch. 10, p. 213).

2. Complementary distribution. [s] occurs before [o] (soma), [u] (suka) and [a] (sata); [θ] occurs before [i] (θima) and [e] (θela). The frames are disjoint: [s] before back vowels, [θ] before front vowels. Both sets are natural classes — the trigger is a vowel's backness — so the generalisation is statable as a rule: /s/ is realized as [θ] before front vowels. (Which of the two is written as basic is a convention here, not a result: as in Exercise 4, neither conditioning set is more heterogeneous than the other, so the elsewhere test does not choose between /s/ and /θ/.)

3. Predictability. [s]/[θ]: predictable from the following vowel. [ɾ]/[r]: not predictable, since nothing in the context selects one.

4. Phonemehood. Both pairs are allophones of one phoneme, but not for the same reason. [s]/[θ] are one phoneme because the distribution is complementary and the choice is determined. [ɾ]/[r] are one phoneme because the variants count as the same sound to speakers and the meaning is unaffected — the same ground on which the chapter treats [tʃ] and [tˀ] in "forgot your" as two allophones of /t/, while adding the caution that this is a possible case of free variation, since the choice may turn out to be conditioned by dialect or register (p. 214). Note what is not available here: you cannot name a basic form on distributional grounds, because there is no distribution to appeal to. Pick one and label it a convention, or treat the phoneme as the family.

5. The asterisk. It concedes that the definition just given is not universal: "allophones never appear in the same phonetic context *" — "*except in cases of free variation", with "data" /ˈdætə/ or /ˈdeɪtə/ as the example (Tutorial C, p. 6). Case 1 is the one that needs it. The lesson is that complementary distribution is the normal shape of allophony, not part of its definition; if it were part of the definition, free variants could not be allophones at all, and the course's own example would be impossible.

Reasoning chain: split the language into pairs → ask the same-environment question of each → label the relation → then, separately, ask what makes them one phoneme → check whether predictability is available as the reason, and if not, say what is.

Exercise 4 skill: recognising an analysis the data underdetermine hard

Language Q is constructed for this drill, and this is the exercise with no settled answer. Work the four steps, then answer the question the steps do not answer — and be precise about what the data permit and what they forbid.

transcriptionglosstranscriptiongloss
[ˈtapa]sun[ˈtsipa]fish
[ˈtuka]moon[ˈtseka]stone
[ˈtoma]river[ˈtsina]bird
[aˈtsi]hand

(1) Are there minimal pairs? (2) Describe the environments of [t] and [ts] and say whether they are in complementary distribution. (3) Can you decide which of the two is the underlying form? (4) Which analyses do the data permit, and which do they forbid? (5) What kind of further evidence would settle it?

Step 1: run the steps that the data do settle

Minimal pairs first, then environments. Up to the end of Step 3 you have a definite answer, and it is worth writing down before you get to the hard part, because it is where the marks are.

Step 2: before choosing a basic form, compare the two conditioning sets

The elsewhere test asks which set is more heterogeneous. If neither set is more heterogeneous than the other — if both are clean natural classes of the same size — the test returns no answer. That is a result about the data, not a failure on your part.

Step 3: check every generalisation against every row

Two rules that look fine on the word-initial data die elsewhere: a rule about word-initial position, and a rule about [i] alone. Find the rows that kill them.

Full answer

1. No minimal pairs. No two words differ only in [t] versus [ts].

2. Complementary distribution. [t] occurs before [a] (tapa), [u] (tuka) and [o] (toma). [ts] occurs before [i] (tsipa, tsina) and [e] (tseka), and in [aˈtsi], between a vowel and [i]. The sets are disjoint: [t] before back vowels, [ts] before front vowels. As a generalisation the trigger is a natural class — front vowels — and so is the complement, back vowels.

3. No. And the reason is precise: the elsewhere test needs one conditioning set to be more heterogeneous than the other, and here neither is. "Before front vowels" and "before back vowels" are equally specific; each names a natural class, and between them they cover every row and exhaust the vowel inventory. It is equally simple to write /t/ → [ts] / ___ front V or /ts/ → [t] / __ back V. The data do not choose.

4. What the data permit and forbid.

Permitted. (a) /t/ is basic and [ts] is derived before front vowels. (b) /ts/ is basic and [t] is derived before back vowels. (c) A more abstract phoneme — a plosive unspecified for stridency, in the spirit of the chapter's proposal to "avoid identifying the phoneme with any one of its allophones" and treat the set as a family (p. 212). All three fit every row. Say which one you are adopting and label it as a choice.

Forbidden. (i) Claiming that one allophone is the elsewhere case: that requires a heterogeneous conditioning set, and neither set is heterogeneous. (ii) Declaring that [t] and [ts] are separate phonemes. Notice how this differs from (i): the prohibition is not the absence of a minimal pair, it is the strength of the conclusion. A minimal pair would prove contrast; its absence proves nothing on its own, and Zsiga says of complementary distribution only that the two sounds are "more likely allophones of the same phoneme" (p. 204). English [h] and [ŋ] are two phonemes on exactly this profile — no overlap and no minimal pair (p. 213) — so a two-phoneme analysis is not excluded by the shape of the distribution. What rules it out here is the rest of the evidence: the inventory is tiny, [t] and [ts] are phonetically similar, nothing in the data shows contrast, and the distribution is the only argument available. Say "the data point to one phoneme", and give the reason; do not say "there is no minimal pair, therefore two phonemes" — that would be the same error as (i), run in the other direction. (iii) A rule about word-initial position — aˈtsi "hand" has [ts] word-medially, before [i], and a word-initial rule would leave it underived. (iv) A rule mentioning [i] only — tseka "stone" has [ts] before [e], so a rule restricted to [i] fails to derive it. Notice that (iv) is the mirror image of the overgeneralisation trap on the phonetics page: there, "before front vowels" claimed too much; here, "before [i]" claims too little. Both are failures to match the rule to the evidence.

5. What would settle it. An alternation: a morpheme with two shapes, one showing [t] and one showing [ts], in the conditioning environments. That would make one of them derived in the way Exercise 2's [n] is derived. Failing that, evidence from a process that treats one of them as basic — for example another rule that applies to /t/ and not to affricates, or the reverse. Absent such evidence, the correct answer is the one the chapter models for [l]/[ɫ]: state the distribution, state that the choice is not determined, and then either adopt one form as a labelled convention or use an abstract phoneme for the family.

How to write this in an exam. Three sentences, in this order: "The two sounds are in complementary distribution, [t] before back vowels and [ts] before front vowels, so the data point to allophones of one phoneme — 'more likely', in Zsiga's words, not proof. Neither conditioning set is more general than the other, so the elsewhere test does not select a basic form. The data therefore permit either /t/ or /ts/, or an abstract phoneme; they rule out a word-initial rule, because aˈtsi has [ts] word-medially, and a rule restricted to [i], because tseka has [ts] before [e]." That is a complete answer to an incomplete data set, and it is the answer the question is looking for.

Reasoning chain: minimal pairs → environments → generalise → compare the two conditioning sets → if neither is heterogeneous, stop and say so → list what is permitted, list what is ruled out and by which row → name the evidence that would decide.

Last question: a set that is not a natural class can still be written as the environment of a rule. What is the matter with doing that?

Step 1: try to name each set in one phrase

If you can say what all the members share in one phrase that excludes every other sound in the inventory, it is a natural class. If your phrase needs "and", it probably is not.

Step 2: check the exclusion as well as the inclusion

A natural class must not accidentally include a sound outside the set. {f s ʃ h} looks like "voiceless fricatives"; check that no other voiceless fricative exists in the inventory, and that nothing else in the inventory is voiceless and continuant.

Full answer

1. {p t k} — yes. Voiceless plosives, [−voice, −continuant]. Excludes the voiced plosives and excludes [ʔ] only because this inventory has none.

2. {p b m} — yes. Labial: same place, three manners. This is the class a place-assimilation rule needs (Exercise 2's [m]).

3. {m n ŋ} — yes. Nasals, [+nasal].

4. {s ʃ} — yes. Voiceless coronal fricatives: [−voice, +continuant, +coronal]. Both are sibilants, which Zsiga notes are the grooved fricatives with the loud high-pitched noise (Ch. 3, p. 39).

5. {k g ŋ} — yes. Velars: same place, three manners, both voicing values. This is the class Exercise 2's [ŋ] is conditioned by.

6. {f s ʃ h} — yes. Voiceless fricatives, [−voice, +continuant]. It is worth noticing that this class cuts across place entirely: labiodental, alveolar, post-alveolar, glottal. A rule with this environment is a rule about a manner, not a place.

7. {i ɪ u ʊ} — yes. High vowels, [+high], both backnesses and both rounding values.

8. {p m s} — no. A labial plosive, a labial nasal and an alveolar fricative share no feature bundle; any phrase that covers all three also covers most of the inventory.

9. {b l ʃ} — no. Voiced labial plosive, voiced alveolar lateral, voiceless post-alveolar fricative: three different places, three different manners, two voicing values.

10. {a ɔ o} — no. Low back unrounded, open-mid back rounded, close-mid back rounded: the members differ in height and in rounding, so no single feature bundle picks them out.

The last question. A rule can be stated with an arbitrary list — "s becomes ʃ before i, e, a, y" — and it will fit the data. What is wrong with it is that it is a description of those rows rather than a generalisation about the language, and it makes no prediction about a vowel the list happens not to mention. The chapter is blunt about it: "to just give a list of sounds is missing something. Phonological distributions seldom, if ever, depend on random lists of sounds with nothing in common" (p. 209). Step 3 of the procedure exists to force the generalisation; when no generalisation exists, the correct result is "this environment is the elsewhere case", not "this environment is a long list".

Reasoning chain: name each set in one phrase → check the phrase excludes everything outside the set → if you needed "and", it is a list → for the rule question, ask what the list predicts about unseen data.

(1) Describe the distribution of [h] and [ŋ] in Set 1. (2) Are they phonetically similar? (3) What in Set 1 shows they belong to one phoneme, given your answer to (2)? (4) Describe the distribution of [s] and [ʃ] in Set 2, and say what conclusion it supports and how confident you can be. (5) English [h] and [ŋ] are also in complementary distribution. Why does Zsiga refuse to call them allophones, and which set here is the analogue of the English case?

Step 1: describe each distribution before comparing anything

Two separate environment lists, per pair. In Set 1 the environment is the first segment of the root; in Set 2 it is the vowel after the fricative, and one row puts a syllable before it, which is worth noticing.

Step 2: ask the similarity question honestly

Zsiga's point is that similarity is "a good clue, but not a reliable determinant" (p. 212). Say for each pair whether the members share features, and do not let a "yes" or a "no" decide the analysis on its own.

Step 3: look for alternations — the same morpheme in two shapes

This is the step that distinguishes the two sets. A distribution tells you where each sound occurs; an alternation tells you that speakers treat the two as one thing.

Full answer

1. [h] occurs only before a vowel, word-initially ([haˈta], [heˈmi]). [ŋ] occurs only before a consonant, word-initially ([ŋˈkolo], [ŋˈtapa]). The sets are disjoint, and every possessive form falls into one of them. Note that the phonetic characterisation has to be "before a vowel" versus "before a consonant", not "before a back vowel" or anything narrower: there are only two vowel-initial roots in the data.

2. No. [h] is a voiceless glottal fricative; [ŋ] is a voiced velar nasal. They share neither voicing, nor place, nor manner. By the similarity heuristic alone you would bet against allophony — which is why question 3 exists.

3. The alternation. The prefix meaning 'our' has two shapes, and which one appears is fixed by whether the root begins with a vowel or a consonant. That is one morpheme with two allomorphs, which means speakers store one form and derive the other by rule. This is the evidence Zsiga says English lacks for [h]/[ŋ]: "there are no sets of related words where one form of the word has [h] and another form has [ŋ]. That is, an allophonic relationship is not supported by alternations" (p. 213). Language S supplies exactly that support, so the conclusion is allophony despite the phonetic dissimilarity — and the phonetic dissimilarity is the point of the construction, not an objection to it. Zsiga's own list of /t/ allophones runs from [tʰ] through [ʔ] to zero, and "there is no one phonetic feature that every allophone shares" (p. 212).

4. [s] occurs before [i] ([ˈsita], [siˈnaka]) and before [e] ([ˈsela]); [ʃ] occurs before [a] ([ˈʃaka]), [o] ([ˈʃoma]) and [u] ([ˈʃuka]). Disjoint sets, both natural classes: [s] before front vowels, [ʃ] before back vowels. Note that [siˈnaka] puts [s] before [i] word-medially as well, so the environment is the following vowel, not the word's first position — the same check that killed the word-initial analysis in Exercise 4. The distribution supports one phoneme with a predictable alternation; the confidence is lower than in Set 1, because the evidence is distribution plus similarity and there is no alternation. The honest phrasing is "the distribution supports treating them as allophones of one phoneme" and, if you want to be careful, "an alternation would confirm it".

5. English [h]/[ŋ] fails on both counts Zsiga gives: the sounds are "not phonetically similar", and more importantly the relationship "is not supported by alternations" (p. 213). Set 1 is the analogue in its distribution (disjoint, one sound before vowels and the other before consonants) but it supplies the alternation, so it comes out allophonic. Set 2 is the analogue in its lack of alternations, but it has phonetic similarity, so it comes out allophonic on weaker grounds. The English pair is the case where both supports are missing, and that is why Zsiga says English speakers "have no sense that [h] and [ŋ] are two ways of saying the same sound".

Reasoning chain: two environment lists → disjoint? → similar? → same morpheme in two shapes? → count how many of the three supports you have → state the conclusion at the strength the evidence licenses, and name the evidence that is missing.

Discuss

"A minimal pair is proof; a near-minimal pair is only evidence." Test it on Language V in Q2. Then push it: Zsiga says that if you can collect several near-minimal pairs, "each of which differ in a different way, you can still be on firm ground" (p. 211). Is that firm ground, or is it a probabilistic argument dressed as a proof? What would a single near-minimal pair be worth on its own, and what does the number of pairs have to do with the strength of the conclusion? Evidence that would change your mind: a language where a near-minimal pair turned out to be a real minimal pair once a phonetic detail was measured, or a case where two sounds overlapped in environment yet were shown to be allophones by alternation. Say two things and discuss each.

Discuss

Free variation and positional neutralization are both described as complications, and for the same stated reason: they "contradict a simple and fully specified mapping between phonemes and allophones" (p. 215). Which one is the deeper problem for the phoneme? Free variation breaks predictability while keeping the phoneme; neutralization keeps the contrast in the system while losing it in one position. Consider which one you could still describe with a rule, and which one forces you to say that the surface form is ambiguous. Then take the further step Zsiga takes and doubt the labels: she calls the [tʃ]/[tˀ] case "a possible case of free variation", because the choice might be conditioned by dialect or register (p. 214), and warns that a distinction may not be "truly neutralized" because the preceding vowel can still differ (p. 214). What does that caution imply about how confidently you should use either label?

Discuss

Exercise 6 on the phonetics page used a language's consonant inventory to decide that [ʃ] and [tʃ] were allophones, and Language H's inventory simply did not list them. Is that legitimate, or is it circular — the analyst assuming the inventory to prove a point about the inventory? Zsiga's own procedure starts from the inventory: after collecting a few thousand words, "the linguist would begin to have a pretty good idea of the sound inventory", and only then asks "which sounds belong to different phonemes, which are allophones of the same phoneme" (p. 207). But the same page warns that the data are assumed accurate and representative, and that "in a real analysis, larger data sets would be required". Take a position on when an inventory is evidence and when it is a hypothesis, and say what would make you revise one.
