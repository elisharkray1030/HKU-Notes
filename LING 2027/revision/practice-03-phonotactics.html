LING 2027 Practice Revision hub

Phonotactics: practice

Five drills on Zsiga Ch. 11.1: reading a constraint off a lexicon, separating actual from possible from impossible, deciding whether a pattern is absolute or statistical, and using loanword repairs as evidence for a constraint. Every language below is constructed for the drill, so nothing here depends on your intuitions about English.

Background: Phonotactics.

Quiz

Q1

The Kesavi lexicon in Exercise 2 has no word [padu]. A classmate concludes that [padu] is impossible in Kesavi. What is wrong with that conclusion?

Zsiga's three-way distinction turns exactly on this: an actual word is one the speakers happen to have; a possible word is one that breaks no constraint and could take on a meaning; an impossible word is one a constraint rules out (p. 223). Her example is Halle's triplet, and Tutorial D runs it in English: [bɹɪk] is attested, [blɪk] is possible, *[bnɪk] is impossible because English bans initial stop-nasal sequences (Tutorial D, p. 5–6). Absence from a word list of a few dozen items tells you nothing on its own — most possible words are unattested, which is what "gap" means.

Q2

In a 26-word sample of Tave, two words end in [k] ([vidak] "iron", [sinek] "herring") and the rest end in a vowel or [n, l, ɹ]. Tave borrows Orim [merek] as [merek], with the final [k] untouched. How should the pattern be stated?

Zsiga's point in §11.1.2 is that phonotactic knowledge is not always categorical: speakers rate sequences as better or worse rather than possible or impossible, and ratings track how frequent the pieces are (p. 224). Her own case is the near-absence of English words in [vl]/[vɹ] — "do we have a constraint against consonant clusters that begin with [v]?" — which the addition of a real word beginning that way answers in the negative (p. 225). The borrowing is the decisive evidence here: an absolute ban would have forced a repair, and none happened. Option 4 is the trap — it converts a count into a claim about a natural class that the two exceptions already contradict.

Q3

Suvali borrows Aldric [tarak] as [tarakɨ], and [salim] as [salimɨ]. What does the [ɨ] establish?

Read the constraint off the repair, not the other way round: something about a word ending in [k] or [m] is illegal, and the language's fix is to add a vowel after it. The vowel is always [ɨ], whatever the preceding consonant, so its quality is predictable — Zsiga's diagnostic for epenthesis rather than deletion is that "the quality of epenthetic vowels is predictable, and the quality of deleted vowels is not" (p. 243). Options 2 and 4 fail because the same insertion happens after two consonants with nothing in common except that neither is coronal.

Q4

Suvali speakers reject [mbaɹa], [nduka], [ŋgola] and [mpesa], but accept [mloka], [njaɹa], [ploka] and [stena]. Which statement is the constraint?

The accepted set is the control group. [ml] and [nj] are two consonants and begin with a nasal, so options 2 and 3 are both false of the data; [pl] and [st] show that initial clusters in general are fine; [mp] and [ŋg] are rejected although [mp] is voiceless and [mb], [nd], [ŋg] agree in place, so voicing and place agreement are irrelevant. What separates the rejected four from the accepted four is the manner of the second consonant: obstruent versus sonorant. Say it as a prohibition on a sequence (no word-initial nasal + obstruent), not as a description of a repair.

Q5

English bans word-initial [bn]; Swahili allows [mba] but bans [bma]; Greek allows [pneu] but bans [mba]. What follows?

"The sequences ruled out by phonotactic constraints vary from language to language. English allows neither stop-nasal nor nasal-stop sequences in initial position. Swahili allows nasal-stop ([mba]) but not stop-nasal (*[bma]). Greek is the opposite" (p. 223). The same page adds the constraint on variation: "Though phonotactic constraints may vary, they are not random. No language, for example, allows complex sequences like Greek [pneu] without allowing the simple [pe] and [ne]. The constraints rule out more difficult, marked, sequences, while permitting those that are unmarked." Option 4 confuses two different kinds of knowledge: which segments exist, and which sequences of them are allowed.

Step 1: sort the judgements, not the words

Take the accepted column first. [mloka] and [njaɹa] begin with a nasal plus a sonorant. [ploka] and [stena] begin with two obstruents. [sambit] has [mb] in the middle. [tandul] ends in [l], a licensed final, and [veɹindu] ends in a vowel.

Now the rejected column. Four begin with a nasal plus an obstruent: [mb], [nd], [ŋg], [mp]. Three end in a consonant that is not one of [n, l, ɹ, s, t, d].

Step 2: check the rejected set for a false generalisation

The four initial clusters include a voiceless one ([mp]) and three that agree in place ([mb], [nd], [ŋg]). So the ban is neither about voicing nor about place disagreement — the way English's nasal-place rule is. What the four share is that the second consonant is an obstruent, and what the accepted [ml], [nj] share is that the second consonant is a sonorant.

Step 3: read the final consonants off the lexicon

List every word-final consonant in the lexicon: [s] (tamos, fentas), [ɹ] (koltaɹ, mobaɹ), [l] (tɹisol, naɹvil, sambul), [n] (veɹin, zelun, lusen, slibun), [t] (kalat), [d] (mubad). Six consonants, no others. Say what they have in common: all are coronal. The rejected [kalak], [tamok] end in a velar; [mubam] ends in a labial nasal. Voicing cannot be the criterion, because [d] is voiced and licensed.

Full answer

Constraint 1 (word-initial). A word may not begin with a nasal followed by an obstruent. In features: *#[+nasal][−sonorant] is prohibited. Evidence: [mbaɹa], [nduka], [ŋgola], [mpesa] rejected; [mloka], [njaɹa] accepted. Place agreement is not the issue — [mb], [nd] and [ŋg] agree in place and are still rejected — and voicing is not the issue, since [mp] is voiceless and rejected too.

Constraint 2 (word-final). A word may not end in a consonant that is not coronal. In features: *#C[−coronal] is prohibited. Evidence: the licensed finals are [n, l, ɹ, s, t, d], all coronal, and they include sonorants and obstruents, voiced and voiceless sounds — so the class is place, not manner or voice. Rejected: [kalak], [tamok] (velar), [mubam] (labial).

Sorting the seven new forms. [mbanu] impossible — violates constraint 1 (#[mb]). [kristel] possible — [#kɹ] is a licit obstruent cluster and [l] is a licit final. [tandak] impossible — violates constraint 2 (final [k]); the medial [nd] is irrelevant. [njeka] possible — [#nj] is nasal + sonorant, and the word ends in a vowel. [mlek] impossible — violates constraint 2 (final [k]) and nothing else: the [#ml] that looks suspicious is licensed. [sambit] possible — the [mb] is word-internal, where the constraint does not reach, and [t] is a licit final. [mbanuk] impossible on both counts — #[mb] and final [k].

Why the two constraints cannot be merged. A single "no nasal + obstruent" rule would wrongly reject [sambul], [kandu], [tanku] and [pundu], all native words with [mb], [nd], [nk] word-internally. The ban is positional: it holds at the left edge only. That is Tutorial D's distinction between sequential knowledge (which combinations are allowed) and positional knowledge (where a sound may stand) — here both are needed, and the wording these problems use — "describe the phonological environment of each allomorph" — is your cue to say where.

One honesty note. Every licensed final in this lexicon is alveolar, so the data do not distinguish "[+coronal]" from "[+alveolar]". State the wider class and say why you chose it: it covers [ʃ] and [tʃ] as well, and a narrower class would be a coincidence dressed as a constraint. In an exam, that sentence earns the mark you would lose by asserting more than the data show.

Step 1: describe the syllables you see

Every word begins with exactly one consonant and ends in a vowel. Most syllables are CV. Where a consonant closes a syllable, it is [n], [m], [ɹ] or [l] — [kanta], [maɹta], [velta], [bunda], [simla]. No syllable closes with a stop or a fricative.

Step 2: turn the description into three constraints

(i) A coda must be a sonorant. (ii) A word may not begin with a consonant cluster. (iii) A word must end in a vowel. Three separate clauses, each with its own evidence — keep them separate, because an answer that lumps them will mis-assign the test forms.

Step 3: check [padu] against the lexicon before judging it

[padu] is not in the list, but [pa-] and [-du] are both ordinary: [padu] is CVCV with no coda, no cluster and a final vowel. If a form breaks no clause, its absence is an accident of the lexicon — which is what "possible" means.

Full answer

Constraints. (i) A syllable coda must be [+sonorant] — the only codas are [n, m, ɹ, l]. (ii) No word-initial consonant cluster. (iii) Every word ends in a vowel.

Classification. [padu] possible — unattested, violates nothing. [kal] impossible — clause (iii): word-final consonant. [kasta] impossible — clause (i): the coda [s] is an obstruent. Note that [kasta] breaks only that clause; it begins with a single consonant and ends in a vowel, so saying "it has a cluster" or "it ends badly" is wrong. [stuma] impossible — clause (ii): initial [st]. [boka] actual — it is in the lexicon. [manta] possible — [n] is a licit coda, the initial consonant is single, the word ends in a vowel.

Unattested versus impossible. [padu] and [kal] are both absent from the lexicon, and only one of them is ruled out. The difference is not the absence; it is whether a constraint excludes the form. Absence is evidence of nothing on its own — a lexicon of twenty words is a sample, not the language.

What the data cannot decide. Whether a three-syllable word is possible: every word here has two syllables, but no constraint in (i)–(iii) mentions length, and a shorter word list cannot show a length limit exists. Whether [ʒ] is a possible Kesavi segment: it never occurs, and that is a fact about the sample, not about the inventory. Whether [m] can close a word: [m] appears as a coda in [simla] but never word-finally, and clause (iii) forbids all word-final consonants anyway, so the question is already answered — that one is decidable, and it is worth noticing the difference between a question the constraints settle and a question they leave open.

Step 1: count, do not eyeball

Word-initial vowels: none in 26. Word-initial clusters: none in 26. Word-final consonants: [n] (taven, soɹan, pelen, pilan), [ɹ] (kudaɹ), [l] (navel), [k] (vidak, sinek). Word-final [k]: 2 of 26. Everything else ends in a vowel.

Step 2: for each pattern, ask what a repaired borrowing would look like

A categorical ban forces a repair when a borrowed word breaks it. So the loanword column is a test: [oɹan] and [ika] gain a [h] (prothesis, because #V is banned); [stal] gains a vowel between the two initial consonants (epenthesis, because #CC is banned); [merek] keeps its final [k] and [koɹma] keeps its medial [ɹm]. Two repairs, two non-repairs.

Step 3: say what the naive rule would predict

Write "no word ends in [k]" and you have said something false of [vidak] and [sinek], which are native words. Write it as an absolute constraint and you must also predict that a borrowed word in [k] gets repaired, i.e. *[merekɨ]. Neither prediction is a fact about Tave. That is what overgenerating means: the rule manufactures a repair nobody performs and marks two real words ungrammatical.

Full answer

Pattern 1 — no word begins with a vowel (0 of 26): absolute. The loanwords show the repair: Orim [oɹan] → [hoɹan], [ika] → [hika]. A prothetic [h] is added at the left edge. Because the violation is repaired in borrowings, it is a constraint and not an accident of the sample.

Pattern 2 — no word begins with a consonant cluster (0 of 26): absolute. Orim [stal] → [satal]: [a] is inserted to break the initial cluster. Again a repair, so again a constraint. The repair is epenthesis, not deletion — nothing is lost.

Pattern 3 — no word ends in [k] (true of 24 of the 26): statistical. [vidak] "iron" and [sinek] "herring" are native Tave words with final [k]; the generalisation that fits the rest is that a word ends in a vowel or a coronal sonorant. The borrowing [merek] keeps its final [k], so no repair is forced. That is the evidence that the pattern is gradient rather than categorical: 2 of 26 is a strong dispreference, not a ban.

How to word it in an exam. "Final [k] is strongly disfavoured but attested ([vidak], [sinek]); borrowings in final [k] are not repaired ([merek]). The pattern is statistical." Then, if the question asks for the constraint: "Tave has an absolute ban on vowel-initial words and on initial clusters; it has no absolute ban on final [k]."

What the naive rule would get wrong. An absolute *#C[−coronal] constraint — which is exactly the right constraint in Suvali, Exercise 1 — is the wrong analysis here. It would mark [vidak] and [sinek] impossible, and it would predict *[merekɨ] for the borrowing. Two native words and one borrowed word are the cost.

The general lesson. The same formal shape of rule is correct in one language and overgenerating in another, and the difference is decided by the exceptions plus the loanword evidence, not by how tidy the pattern looks. Zsiga's framing: "phonotactic constraints are not absolute. Speakers do not necessarily judge sequences as possible or impossible, but better or worse" (p. 224), and her own test case is whether English has a constraint against word-initial [vl]/[vɹ], settled by the arrival of a real word that begins that way (p. 225).

Step 1: mark what changed in each pair

Three lose their first consonant: [mbaɹa] → [baɹa], [nduka] → [duka], [mpela] → [pela]. Two gain a final vowel: [taɹak] → [taɹakɨ], [salim] → [salimɨ]. Two are unchanged: [kandul], [mlaka].

Step 2: match each change to a constraint from Exercise 1

The three that lose a consonant all began with a nasal + obstruent, which is exactly constraint 1. The two that gain [ɨ] both ended in a non-coronal consonant — [k] and [m] — which is constraint 2. The unchanged two break neither: [kandul] ends in [l], [mlaka] begins with nasal + liquid.

Step 3: name the repair type, and check the vowel's quality

Losing the nasal is deletion. Adding [ɨ] is epenthesis. The inserted vowel is [ɨ] in both words, whatever precedes it, so its quality is predictable — the diagnostic for insertion rather than deletion of a stored vowel (Zsiga p. 243).

Full answer

Initial repairs (constraint 1). [mbaɹa] → [baɹa], [nduka] → [duka], [mpela] → [pela]. In all three the nasal is deleted, leaving a single obstruent at the left edge. The repair is deletion, and it removes the nasal rather than the stop — the data show which segment goes, not why, so say that and stop. That [mp] is repaired as well as [mb] shows again that voicing is not part of the constraint.

Final repairs (constraint 2). [taɹak] → [taɹakɨ], [salim] → [salimɨ]. A vowel [ɨ] is added after the final consonant, which becomes an onset of the new syllable — the word now ends in a vowel. The repair is epenthesis. [salimɨ] is the informative one: [m] is a nasal, so the constraint cannot be "no final nasals" or "no final sonorants"; it is place, and [m] is labial.

The controls. [kandul] is unchanged because [l] is coronal, and [mlaka] is unchanged because [ml] is nasal + sonorant. These two are what make the analysis an argument rather than a coincidence: the same two positions — initial and final — are left alone whenever the segment is of the right class.

Prediction: [mbanuk] → [banukɨ]. Both repairs apply: the nasal deletes because #[mb] is word-initial, and [ɨ] is added because the word ends in [k]. The order of the two repairs does not matter here — neither creates or destroys the environment of the other — and that is worth saying, since in the alternation problems on the next page order often does matter.

What the set does not show. Why deletion is chosen for initial clusters and epenthesis for final consonants. That is a fact about Suvali which the data record but do not explain; if asked to justify it, say that both repairs are available to the language and that which one is used is a property of the constraint being repaired (a cluster at the edge versus a single segment at the edge), then stop. Do not invent a principle the data do not show.

Step 1: state each positional fact

[ŋ] occurs seven times in the sample, every time word-finally. [h] occurs seven times, every time word-initially. Neither segment occurs anywhere else. So: [ŋ] is licensed finally only; [h] is licensed initially only.

Step 2: look for repairs, not for absences

The lexicon shows absence in both cases and cannot distinguish them. The borrowings can: [ŋolo] and [ŋisa] are changed to [kolo] and [kisa], so word-initial [ŋ] is actively ruled out and the language has a repair for it. [baha] and [miha] are unchanged, so medial [h] is not ruled out — it is a gap.

Step 3: ask what the repair chose, and why

The repair for initial [ŋ] is [k], not deletion and not [ŋ] itself. [k] keeps the velar place of [ŋ]; nasality is lost, and with it voicing, since [k] is voiceless and [ŋ] is voiced. And the repair does not touch [m] or [n], which begin [mata], [miɹa], [noka] quite happily — so the ban is on [ŋ] in onset position, not on nasals.

Full answer

The two generalisations. (i) [ŋ] occurs only word-finally; a word may not begin with [ŋ], and [ŋ] does not occur between vowels either. (ii) [h] occurs only word-initially; it does not occur word-internally or finally.

Which absence is a constraint. The ban on initial [ŋ] is a constraint: the borrowings [ŋolo] → [kolo] and [ŋisa] → [kisa] show a repair in action. The absence of medial [h] is a gap: [baha] and [miha] come in unchanged, and a language that banned medial [h] would have had to do something to them — delete it, substitute, or insert a consonant.

Why [k]. The substitute shares place with [ŋ]; what changes is nasality, and with it voicing — [k] is a voiceless oral stop where [ŋ] is a voiced nasal. That is a substitution, not deletion and not epenthesis, and it is the kind of repair that preserves as much of the borrowed word as the constraint allows. It also shows the constraint is about the segment [ŋ] in onset position: initial [m] and [n] are untouched in [mata], [miɹa], [noka].

If a speaker were asked about [ŋata]. She should reject it, or produce [kata] — because the absence of [ŋ] initially is enforced, not accidental. If she accepted it, that would be evidence that the ban is weaker than the borrowings suggest; a single judgement would not overturn the seven-word pattern, but it would be the right kind of evidence to look for.

Why this matters for the exam. "Positional knowledge" is one of the three kinds Tutorial D lists — what positions a sound may appear in (p. 8) — and it is the kind that a lexicon alone underdetermines. When a question asks whether some absence is a constraint, the answer is always: find a repair, or say the data do not decide.

Exam shape

The course's own phonotactics quiz is Quiz 3, and it is a loanword question. It is printed on the Topic 3 deck (PDF p. 40), verbatim: "Consider the following three Japanese loan words: 'bat' [batto] / 'strike' [sutoraiku] / 'baseball' [besubaru]. What adaptations can you observe? What phonotactic constraints of Japanese do they reveal? Discuss at least two adaptations." The grading rubric under it reads "3 points — two adaptations with discussion / 2 points — one adaptation with discussion, one without discussion / 1 point — one alternation with discussion" (the slide writes "alternation"; the rubric means adaptation).

Three things follow for how you write. Two adaptations, both discussed — the marks are for the discussion, not the list. Adaptation then constraint — name what changed, then the constraint it reveals; a bare list of changes is the 1-point answer. And the expected content is the one Zsiga gives for the same three words (p. 224): Japanese allows far fewer consonant clusters, "a common strategy is to insert extra vowels to break up the consonant clusters", with the extra detail that "the preferred vowel to insert is [u], except after [t]. The vowel [o] is inserted after [t], because Japanese has another constraint that rules out sequences of [tu]". So [batto] and [sutoraiku] show one constraint (no clusters) plus a second constraint that picks the vowel — that second one is the "at least two adaptations" the rubric is fishing for.

Notice what the quiz does not ask: no formal rules, no UR, no alternation type. Phonotactics questions are answered in prose about sequences and positions.

Pitfall

Three mistakes account for most lost marks on this material.

  • Reading absence as violation. A sequence missing from a word list may be a gap. Say which constraint rules it out, or say the data do not decide — this page's Exercise 2 and Exercise 5 are both built on the difference.
  • Stating the repair instead of the constraint. "Spanish adds [e] before [s] + consonant" describes the fix. The constraint is "No [s]-consonant sequence at the beginning of a word" (Tutorial D, p. 12). Always invert the repair into a prohibition, and say where it applies.
  • Forgetting that constraints are positional. Suvali bans [mb] at the left edge and allows it in the middle; Roku bans [ŋ] at the left edge and allows it at the right. A constraint statement without a position is usually too strong, and too strong is the error that costs the analysis mark.
