Phonotactics: what your language lets you say

Topic 3 is the week-5 lecture (8 Oct), and the reading is one short section: Zsiga §11.1, book pp. 222–225. It answers a small question with a lot riding on it — what does a speaker know about sound sequences that is not just a list of memorised words? Four things follow in the book's order: actual / possible / impossible, absolute versus statistical generalisations, borrowings as evidence, and how a constraint is written. Tutorial D (5–6 Oct) recapped the same ground, and quiz 3 asked you to read Japanese constraints off three loanwords. The last section hands over to alternations.

You know words you have never heard

Zsiga starts from vocabulary size and takes it away. A proficient adult knows 100,000 words or more (p. 222), all memorised, because the link between sound and meaning is arbitrary. But that is not the whole of the knowledge: "But a speaker knows more than the actual words of his language. He also knows whether a string of sounds could be a possible word." (p. 222) Her example is Swahili [mbwa] 'dog'. You need not have heard it to know it could not be English, and the reason is not meaning: "English does not allow the sequence [mbw] at the beginning of a word." (p. 222)

Morris Halle's 1962 article named the three categories Zsiga uses: an actual word, a possible word, an impossible word. A possible word is not stuck being possible — "Possible words could become actual words, if someone decided to start using them and they caught on." (p. 223) — and the mark on the third category is not about rarity: "We use an asterisk to indicate a sequence that is impossible (that is, ungrammatical)." (p. 223) An asterisk is a claim about the grammar, so it is a claim you must be able to back with a constraint.

The tutorial's diagnosis is the model answer: "In English, stop-nasal sequences in initial positions are banned" (p. 6). Notice how much work "in initial positions" does.

What a constraint is

Zsiga's definition names the term rather than a mechanism: "Constraints on sequences that are allowed or disallowed are termed phonotactic constraints (phono = sound, tacti = touching)." (p. 222) The lecture's version is the one to write in an answer, because it names both dimensions a constraint works on: "Phonotactic constraints (or regularities): restrictions on the types of sounds that are allowed to occur next to each other or in particular positions in a word in a given language" (Topic 3, slide 15). Next to each other is adjacency; in particular positions is position. Most constraints do both, and position is the half candidates forget.

The lecture splits the knowledge three ways (slides 5–12). Structural: the inventory of syllable shapes, from Hawaiian V and CV to English's sixteen combinations of (C)(C)(C)V(C)(C)(C) (slides 6–7). Positional: where a sound may stand — Tutorial D pits Cantonese (CVN, CVS) against Mandarin (CVN, *CVS) (p. 8). Sequential: which sounds may sit beside which, as in English str against *tsr (slide 10). One discrepancy matters later: the deck writes Japanese as (C)V(C) (slide 6), Tutorial D as "V, VN, CV, CVN" (p. 36).

What gets ruled out varies by language: "The sequences ruled out by phonotactic constraints vary from language to language." (p. 223) English allows neither stop-nasal nor nasal-stop initially; Swahili allows [mba] but not *[bma]; Greek is the mirror image; Senufo allows no clusters at all. The variation is not free: "No language, for example, allows complex sequences like Greek [pneu] without allowing the simple [pe] and [ne]." (p. 223) Constraints rule out marked sequences and permit unmarked ones, which is why a real constraint never has the shape "this word is banned".

The course's flagship is the nasal-place constraint, worded almost identically in both sources: "Nasals must agree in place of articulation with a following stop." (p. 223; the lecture has "A nasal will agree in its place of articulation with a following stop consonant", slide 15). The English evidence is camp, hamper, bombard, paint, intelligent, wind, window (p. 223), with think and linguistics for the velar nasal (slides 14, 17). What makes it a constraint rather than a coincidence is the negative evidence: no monomorphemic *blimk beside blink, no *ranp beside ramp. That absence is positional neutralization — "nasals do not contrast in place of articulation when they occur before a stop" — and the generalisation is a static generalization, "often called morpheme structure constraints" (p. 223).

The same constraint obeyed differently is the lecture's comparative point (slide 16). English leaves the nasal alone in camp; Korean changes segments — /sɪnpal/ → [sɪmbal] 'shoes', /kamkul/ → [kaŋkul] 'tangerine' (slide 18). Which repair a language picks is a fact about that language.

Pitfall

Three traps sit inside this one distinction.

  • Unattested is not impossible. [blɪk] is unattested and perfectly possible; *[bnɪk] is impossible. "Not a word, so ungrammatical" collapses the two categories the section exists to separate. The test is which constraint is violated, not whether the form is in a dictionary.
  • The nasal constraint is not "no nasal plus stop". English is full of [mp] and [nt] — camp, hamper, paint. What English bans is nasal-stop and stop-nasal initially; what the nasal constraint requires is agreement in place wherever the two meet. Drop "initially" and your constraint excludes the words it was built to describe.
  • An asterisk needs a source. Starring a form claims a constraint rules it out. If you cannot state that constraint in a sentence, write "unattested" and say what you do not know.
Q1

A classmate marks [blɪk] with an asterisk because no English dictionary contains it. The correction is:

Halle's categories are not "in the dictionary / not in the dictionary": a possible word "does not happen to mean anything, but it does not violate the phonotactic constraints of English" (p. 223). Meaninglessness is what makes a word possible rather than actual; it is never a reason for an asterisk.

Absolute and statistical generalisations

The second constraint is the voicing one: "sequences of obstruents must match in voicing" (p. 223). Evidence is negative again — fist, raft, ask, axe, captive, act, adze, rugby, with no "rugpy" and no "azk". It is a static generalization too, "but it is true across the English lexicon" (p. 223). Then the next page takes "true" apart: "it is almost always true. There are a few exceptions: the word 'obstruent,' in fact, has a [bst] voiced-voiceless sequence." (p. 224)

Treating the exception as informative rather than ignoring it leads to the ratings work: "phonotactic constraints are not absolute. Speakers do not necessarily judge sequences as possible or impossible, but better or worse (or perhaps likely vs. unlikely)." (p. 224) Asked to rate nonsense words for English-likeness on a 0–10 scale, speakers use 8, 5 and 2 rather than only the endpoints, and ratings track how frequent the pieces are. Hence [mrupation], "which Halle would mark as totally ungrammatical", rates well: "The sequence [mru] never occurs within a word, but [ation] is very, very frequent, so on average the word isn't too bad." (p. 224)

Zsiga limits the claim, and so should you: "It is not clear, however, that asking people to think about and rate words gets at the same kind of knowledge that speakers access when they actually use language." (p. 224) What she will defend is modest: "Whether absolute or gradient, however, phonotactic constraints do embody an important kind of phonological knowledge." (p. 224)

A sequence is possible or impossible, full stop. Judgements are categorical, a constraint states what the grammar permits, and the asterisk marks the impossible. Use this picture when a question asks you to sort or mark forms.

Judgements are better or worse, on a scale, and they track the frequency of the parts. Nothing is absolutely impossible — [mrupation] is bad but not zero. Use this when a question asks why a form "feels" English-like.

The lecture's labels are three parallel rows rather than two (slide 12): attested vs. unattested is concrete knowledge of your lexicon; (absolutely) possible vs. (absolutely) impossible is absolute knowledge or a generalisation extracted from it; better vs. worse is gradient knowledge. Slide 13 gives the course's minimal set: #bl against #bw, #bltwm as impossible, and #bw against #bz against #bd.

Compare

The sources label this area differently. Use Zsiga when the question is about the distinction, the lecture when it is about what a speaker knows.

  • Zsiga gives three labels over two categories (actual words are a subset of possible ones), then adds gradience on top as a separate empirical claim (pp. 223–224).
  • Topic 3, slide 12 gives three parallel rows — attested/unattested, possible/impossible, better/worse — each with its kind of knowledge: concrete, absolute, gradient.
  • Tutorial D, p. 4 glosses the last two together — "possible/better", "impossible/worse" — which is quick for sorting but blurs exactly the distinction p. 224 is spent on. Do not use the tutorial's paired gloss when a question asks whether a judgement is categorical or gradient.

Borrowings: the language tells on itself

Zsiga's third section is the one quiz 3 was built on, and her framing is why loanwords count as evidence: "A fun place to look at the effect of phonotactic constraints is in lists of new words that are entering a language: possible words that become actual." (p. 224) A borrowing arrives with the donor language's shape and must be fitted to the recipient's constraints, and each adjustment points at one.

Her case is Japanese baseball vocabulary: "Japanese allows many fewer consonant clusters than English does, and English baseball terms are adapted to the constraints of the Japanese language. A common strategy is to insert extra vowels to break up the consonant clusters. So 'bat' becomes [batto], 'strike' becomes [sutoraiku] and 'baseball' becomes [besubaru]." (p. 224) One detail turns description into analysis: "Note also that the preferred vowel to insert is [u], except after [t]. The vowel [o] is inserted after [t], because Japanese has another constraint that rules out sequences of [tu]!" (p. 224)

Two rows are worth arguing about, because that is where a naive version of the rule fails. Batto has no cluster at all — it is CVC — so "insert a vowel to break the cluster" predicts [bato], not [batto]. The vowel is there because [t] cannot end a Japanese word, and the lengthening closes the syllable; both are inferences from the inventory, not claims the book makes. Besubaru shows substitution, not insertion: the [l] is replaced, and no vowel would have fixed it. The book states the correspondence without calling it a constraint — "the symbol [r] is used for the Japanese alveolar lateral tap, which corresponds to both English [ɹ] and English [l]" (p. 224).

The traffic runs both ways. English borrowed goji from Mandarin in 2009: "English phonotactic constraints do not allow unaspirated [k] in initial position, so we pronounce the word with a [g] instead." (p. 224) And vlog, added the same year, settles a gradience question: "do English speakers have a constraint *vC, to which 'vroom' and 'Vladimir' are exceptions, or is the fact that so few English words begin with [vl] just an accident of history? The addition of [vlog] as a vocabulary word suggests the latter." (p. 225) English has plenty of [fr] and [fl] and almost no [vl] or [vr], so the [vl] gap is statistical, and one borrowing can decide which it is.

Exam shape

Quiz 3 was this, word for word (Topic 3, slide 40): "Consider the following three Japanese loan words: 'bat' [batto], 'strike' [sutoraiku], 'baseball' [besubaru]. What adaptations can you observe? What phonotactic constraints of Japanese do they reveal? Discuss at least two adaptations."

The rubric was printed on the same slide: "3 points — two adaptations with discussion / 2 points — one adaptation with discussion, one without discussion / 1 point — one alternation with discussion". Marks go to adaptations that come with discussion, so two adaptations each carrying a constraint sentence beat four forms with none.

What "discussion" means. Name what changed, state the constraint as a sentence with the environment in it ("Japanese rules out sequences of [tu]" is a constraint; "[o] is inserted" is a description), then say what the form would have been without the repair. The midterm covers this, and the three-part shape works on any loanword data.

Q2

Japanese inserts [o] after [t] in [batto] and [sutoraiku] but [u] elsewhere. The reason is:

Zsiga is explicit: "the preferred vowel to insert is [u], except after [t]. The vowel [o] is inserted after [t], because Japanese has another constraint that rules out sequences of [tu]!" (p. 224). Strike has no [o] to copy, and the direction is the reverse of the third option — [u] is the default.

What a constraint statement looks like

Description is not analysis; a constraint you can write is. Three requirements make a statement usable, and the book's sentences model all three.

Name the environment, not the words. The pattern in camp, hamper, bombard, paint is not four items to memorise but one relation between a nasal and a following stop. A statement that lists items cannot be tested on a new one, which is the only thing a constraint is for.

Use classes and features. Zsiga's sentence names natural classes — nasals, stops, obstruents, place, voicing — never individual segments. In notation that is an environment frame plus a class: [+nasal] → [αplace] / __ C[αplace, −sonorant], with frames written # __ C for word-initial, V __ V for intervocalic, __ # for word-final. Half the content of a constraint lives in the frame.

State it as generally as possible. The book's explicit instruction is at p. 231, in §11.2, and page 4 quotes it; the principle applies here all the same. The test is two-sided: does the statement cover every item you listed, and does it wrongly exclude something the language allows? "English bans nasal plus stop" fails the second test on camp. "Nasals agree in place with a following stop" passes both.

For the sentence itself, the course's model comes from the alternation exercise and is worth copying even here: "Vowels separated by a morpheme boundary must have the same value of [+/- high]" (Tutorial D, p. 35). Subject, modal, property, environment — X must be Y in Z. That is what a marker looks for when the prompt says "state the phonotactic constraint".

The bridge to alternations

A constraint that describes which words exist also predicts what happens when morphemes meet, and that is the whole bridge: "Phonotactic constraints also give rise to alternations." (p. 225) The nasal-place constraint holds inside camp and wind, where it yields a static generalization and no variation, because a monomorphemic word has one form; the same constraint holds across the boundary in in-, where it yields [ɪn], [ɪm] and [ɪŋ] and therefore positional variation. One constraint, two consequences. The lecture's slogan compresses it: "Phonotactic restrictions are the main motivations for alternations. In other words, alternations exist primarily to satisfy the phonotactics of a given language." (slide 38) It keeps a hedge elsewhere — "Many, albeit not all, alternations are motivated by phonotactic restrictions" (slide 20).

Page 4 picks up where this stops: allomorphs, environments, the elsewhere case, the rule. What carries across is the direction of explanation. A constraint says what the language permits; an alternation is what a morpheme does when the constraint would otherwise be violated.

Six things to reproduce from memory

  1. The definition. Restrictions on the types of sounds allowed next to each other, or in particular positions, in a word in a given language.
  2. Three labels and the asterisk. Actual, possible, impossible; * marks the impossible because it violates a constraint; unattested is not impossible.
  3. Halle's contribution. The 1962 triplet, and the point that possible words can become actual while impossible ones cannot.
  4. Absolute versus statistical. Obstruent voicing almost always holds, obstruent [bst] is the exception, and ratings are gradient.
  5. Two flagship constraints. Nasal place agreement (with positional neutralization) and obstruent voicing agreement.
  6. Loanword adaptation. The three Japanese forms, [u] except after [t], the *[tu] constraint, and vlog as evidence that a gap can be an accident of history.
