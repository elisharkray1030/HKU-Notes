Phonetics: how speech is produced, and how to describe it

Topic 1, Phonetics part I, weeks 1–3 (3, 10, 17 Sep). Reading: Ch. 1–2 for the anatomy and the descriptive framework, then Ch. 3–4 for the consonant and vowel charts (Topic 0, slide 33; Topic 1, slide 49). This page does phonetics only: the vocal tract, the five parameters, the IPA chart, and the procedure that turns a symbol into a description and a word into a transcription. The midterm covers phonetics “including non-English, but you will be given an IPA chart with the exam” (Topic 4, slide 31), so the target is using the chart, not reciting it.

The problem: the object of study is invisible

Phonetics is “the study of how speech sounds are articulated and transmitted” (Topic 1, slide 3), and its difficulty is access, not theory: “The main obstacle in studying speech is that the object of study is for the most part invisible” (Ch. 1, p. 2 of 15). The lips, jaw and front of the tongue can be seen; the tongue inside the mouth, the velar port, the vocal folds and the sound waves cannot, so the techniques of speech science “almost all involve ways of making these invisible movements visible, and thus measurable.”

Three systems, divided at one valve

Chapter 1 divides the tract at the larynx, “a valve of cartilage and muscle in the throat that sits on top of the trachea (windpipe)” — you can feel it in the front of the neck as the “Adam’s apple”. Below it is the sub-laryngeal system, the larynx itself is the laryngeal system, and above it the supra-laryngeal system, which splits into the oral tract and the nasal tract (p. 7 of 15).

SystemStructuresContributes
Sub-laryngealLungs, diaphragm, abdominal and ribcage muscles; the trachea, which sits below the larynxThe airstream
LaryngealCricoid, thyroid and two arytenoid cartilages; the vocal folds and vocalis muscleVoicing, aspiration, pitch
Supra-laryngealLower lip, tongue front (tip, blade), tongue body, tongue root; upper lip, upper teeth, alveolar ridge, post-alveolar region, hard palate, velum, uvula, pharyngeal wall; nasal cavityPlace and manner; oral vs. nasal

Two structures do not sort cleanly. The trachea lies below the larynx by the chapter’s dividing line, though it is described in the larynx section — that placement is my inference, not a sentence in the book. The epiglottis is “a flap of tissue attached to the base of the tongue” that folds over the larynx in swallowing; the chapter never assigns it, and it belongs to the tongue while behaving like part of the laryngeal valve. If asked, say which description you are using.

Below the larynx, air is the whole story: the lungs have no muscles of their own, the diaphragm and ribcage muscles move them, and speech happens on the way out, up the trachea — the pulmonic egressive airstream. Speaking on an indrawn breath is possible, but “no language uses the pulmonic ingressive airstream in any systematic way” (p. 8 of 15).

The larynx is four cartilages, two folds and the muscles joining them; the folds run from the thyroid cartilage in front to the arytenoids behind, and they are folds, not strings (“erroneously called the vocal cords by a Medieval anatomist”). Clamped, they stop the air and give a glottal stop; held apart, they let it through voiceless; held together at the right tension, they vibrate and that vibration is voicing. Faster vibration is higher pitch, so the larynx’s linguistic job is “(mainly) to control pitch and voice” (pp. 8–10 of 15).

Above the larynx, sounds get their shape from moving parts against fixed ones. The active articulators are the lower lip, tongue front, tongue body and tongue root; the passive articulators are the upper lip, upper teeth, alveolar ridge, post-alveolar region, hard palate, velum, uvula and pharyngeal wall. The velum is both a passive articulator and a valve on the velar port: lowered, air escapes through the nose and [m] and [n] are nasal; raised, the port shuts and the sound is oral (p. 13 of 15).

Source: Zsiga, Ch. 1, p. 7 of 15 (three systems), p. 8 of 15 (diaphragm, pulmonic egressive, the vocal folds), pp. 9–10 of 15 (cartilages, voicing, pitch, the valve), p. 12 of 15 (the four active articulators), pp. 12–13 of 15 (the passive articulators, the velum).

Five parameters

Every speech sound is a combination of the same five choices: “airstream mechanism / state of the larynx / state of the velar port / combination of active and passive articulator (= place of articulation) / manner of articulation” (Ch. 2, p. 3 of 19). That list is the skeleton of every description you will write, and why the chart is a grid: place and manner “combine relatively freely”.

  1. Airstream. Pulmonic egressive unless stated — the unmarked default, and the one every language uses for its vowels (p. 7 of 19).
  2. Larynx. Voiced or voiceless, aspirated or not, then voice quality (creaky, breathy).
  3. Velum. Open gives a nasal, closed an oral sound.
  4. Place. Which active articulator, against which passive one.
  5. Manner. Complete closure, narrow channel, or none.

The lecture adds a timing version that explains aspiration: in stops, the onset of voicing sorts the sound into three — “voicing before closure = voiced”, “voicing around the time of release = voiceless”, “voicing after release = voiceless aspirated” (Topic 1, slide 24). English “pat” is the third; the [p] of “spot” is the second.

Source: Zsiga, Ch. 2, p. 3 of 19 (the five components, place and manner defined), p. 7 of 19 (pulmonic egressive as default); Topic 1, slide 24 (voicing onset time).

Manner

Manner is “the kind or degree of constriction that is made, independent of where in the vocal tract” (Ch. 2, p. 8 of 19). The first cut separates sounds that obstruct the airflow from sounds that let it resonate. Obstruents are plosives, fricatives and affricates; sonorants are nasal stops and approximants; vowels are always sonorant. The test is pressure: a sonorant has “no build-up of pressure in the supralaryngeal vocal tract”.

A plosive closes completely, lets pressure build and releases with a burst; a fricative narrows without closing, so the air becomes turbulent; an affricate is a stop released into a fricative at the same place, counting as one segment; an approximant narrows without frication, and covers glides, laterals and rhotics. Laterals block the centre line and let air out over the side. Taps and flaps are ballistic — the articulator throws itself at the other and bounces, or strikes in passing — while trills are aerodynamic, the airflow setting an articulator vibrating (Ch. 3, pp. 42–43).

Two manner classes cross the obstruent line, and that is the trap. [m] is a stop by manner, since the lips are as closed as for [p], but a sonorant by class, because the velum is open and no pressure builds: “stop” does not mean “obstruent”. A tap is a sonorant for a related reason — its closure lasts about 20 milliseconds, too brief for pressure to build (p. 42). Affricates need a note: “The IPA chart contains no symbols for affricates” (p. 44), because one can always be written as a stop plus a fricative at the same place, tied with a bar. What makes it one segment is behaviour, not length: English allows [t͡ʃ] word-initially although it forbids other stop-plus-fricative sequences.

Source: Zsiga, Ch. 2, pp. 8–10 of 19 (obstruents, sonorants, the manner list); Ch. 3, p. 42 (laterals, the tap’s 20 ms closure), p. 43 (tap, flap and trill), p. 44 (no affricate symbols), p. 45 (complex segments).

Place

Place pairs an active with a passive articulator, and each active articulator makes several: the lower lip gives bilabial against the upper lip and labiodental against the teeth; the tongue front gives dental, alveolar, retroflex (tip curled back) and post-alveolar (blade); the tongue body gives palatal and velar; the larynx, with no passive articulator, gives laryngeal. English uses nine places (Ch. 2, p. 12 of 19; Ch. 3, pp. 40–41).

Two consonants refuse the one-place template. [w] is a double articulation, velar narrowing plus lip rounding at once, so it is entered under bilabial and velar alike and described as “labio-velar”; [h] uses the larynx as its only articulator, which is why “glottal” is a place as well as a manner.

Place is also where the chart’s blanks come from. There are no labiodental plosives, because a stop there would need “completely perfect teeth” (p. 38); the chart leaves that block blank rather than shading it, “because it is not physically impossible to make such a stop, just highly improbable” (p. 41) — the difference the next section turns on.

Source: Zsiga, Ch. 2, p. 11 of 19 (active + passive = place), p. 12 of 19 (nine English places, [w] labio-velar, [h] laryngeal); Ch. 3, p. 38 (no labiodental plosives), p. 41 (that block blank, not shaded).

The IPA chart

The IPA was created in 1887 on two principles: it would be universal, with enough symbols for every sound in every language, and unambiguous, with a one-to-one correspondence between sounds and symbols (Ch. 2, p. 4 of 19). The chart is five panels, not one — pulmonic consonants, non-pulmonic consonants, other symbols, diacritics (Ch. 3, p. 35) and vowels.

The pulmonic panel is the five parameters in tabular form. Place runs across the top — bilabial, labiodental, dental, alveolar, post-alveolar, retroflex, palatal, velar, uvular, pharyngeal, glottal; manner runs down the side — plosive, nasal, trill, tap or flap, fricative, lateral fricative, approximant, lateral approximant. A cell is one place plus one manner, and “the one to the right represents a voiced consonant” (p. 34). The vowel panel has a different shape, because a vowel has no constriction to locate: height down the side, backness across, the rounded member of a pair on the right (Ch. 4, p. 58). The remaining panels hold clicks, implosives and ejectives, the symbols with no column of their own, and the diacritics that carry detail the chart has no letters for — voiceless, aspirated, nasalized, syllabic.

Then there are the empty cells. Zsiga separates two kinds: “Empty blocks indicate combinations that have not been found in any language; shaded empty blocks indicate combinations ‘deemed impossible’” (pp. 35–36). A shaded cell is a claim about the human vocal tract, not a gap. The blanks matter as much as the symbols: “The symbols answer the question ‘What are the occurring sounds of human language?’ but the blanks answer the question ‘What is a possible sound of human language?’” (p. 36). A pharyngeal or laryngeal nasal is impossible for an anatomical reason — the velum sits above them, so cutting the air off down there leaves nothing to send through the nose (p. 41). Keep the labiodental plosive as the control case: possible, unattested, blank — and shading it would say “impossible”.

Exam shape

The last slide of the Topic 4 deck publishes the scope: “Phonetics (including non-English, but you will be given an IPA chart with the exam)”, then abstraction/contrast/predictability, phonotactics and alternations, with the advice to “check your knowledge with p-sets” (Topic 4, slide 31). No weightings are published, so none are given here.

Three consequences. One: the skill tested is using the chart — find the manner row, find the place column, read the cell, add voicing and the velum — so practise on a symbol you have never seen. Two: non-English data means unseen places — uvular and pharyngeal, retroflex stops, labial-velar stops — and naming them from the chart is the whole task; you need not pronounce them. Three: do not assume English phonotactics: English bans [ŋ] word-initially, Thai has [ŋaː] “tusk” (Ch. 3, p. 36). Same sound, unfamiliar environment.

Vowels

Vowels are open articulations, so “the dimensions of ‘place’ and ‘manner’ are less useful for vowels than for consonants” (Ch. 4, p. 56), and “the term place of articulation is not really appropriate” (Ch. 2, p. 15 of 19). In its place are three coordinates, given in the order to use them: “height (open vs. mid vs. close), frontness/backness, rounding” (Topic 1, slide 10).

The space is a quadrilateral and not a symmetrical one: there is more room for tongue movement along the palate than in the pharynx, so there is more room among the high vowels than the low ones and the shape is “wider at the top than at the bottom” (p. 57). A point on that chart describes what a vowel sounds like, not where the tongue is.

Two vocabularies run at once. The IPA says close and open where the traditional terms say high and low — “the IPA chart uses the term ‘close’ for ‘high’ and ‘open’ for ‘low’” (p. 59) — and the course accepts both, often together: Tutorial A glosses one symbol “open back rounded vowel / low back rounded vowel”. Pick one and do not mix them. The vowel chart is where the grid logic fails: symbols sit at points and are relative, so in a five-vowel system [e] falls “somewhere between cardinal vowels #2 and #3”, [o] between #6 and #7, [a] between #4 and #5 (p. 59). The anchors are Daniel Jones’s cardinal vowels — eighteen, eight primary, defined as extremes and belonging to no language (p. 58).

Rounding is the third coordinate, and in a five-vowel system predictable: all and only the non-low back vowels are round. Front rounded [y] and back unrounded vowels appear only in larger inventories, and marked values imply unmarked ones — “the presence of [y] implies the presence of [i]” (p. 61); French has [ly] “read” against [lu] “wolf” (p. 62). Tense/lax is the fourth dimension English leans on: tense vowels are “somewhat higher and longer than their lax counterparts” and are diphthongized (p. 60); the contrast is rare among low vowels, because tongue-root advancement and tongue-body lowering pull against each other (p. 61).

Three smaller parameters finish the job. Centrality: English uses the middle of the space, [ʌ] “wedge” stressed and [ə] “schwa” unstressed, as in “abut” (p. 63). Length: written doubled or with a length mark, “([aa]), or with a colon after the usual symbol ([a:])” (p. 67) — the book prints a colon; Tokyo [biːru] “beer” against [biru] “building” is the minimal pair. Diphthongs: a vowel that changes position inside one syllable. English has three true ones, and the test is contrast rather than movement: if deleting the offglide does not change the word, the vowel is diphthongized, not a diphthong (p. 68).

Source: Zsiga, Ch. 4, p. 56 (vowels as open), p. 57 (quadrilateral; symbols describe sound), p. 58 (cardinal vowels, vowel chart, rounding), p. 59 (“close”/“high”, five-vowel systems, points between cardinals), p. 60 (tense vowels higher, longer, diphthongized), p. 61 (low vowels, markedness, [y] implies [i]), p. 62 (French [ly]/[lu]), p. 63 (wedge and schwa), p. 67 (length), p. 68 (diphthong vs. diphthongized); Ch. 2, p. 15 of 19 (place not appropriate); Topic 1, slide 10.

From parameters to an answer

The course fixes the order of a description: “Consonants are named in the order of ‘(other) - voicing - place - manner’. Vowels are named in the order of ‘height - backness - rounding’” (Tutorial A, p. 1). The “other” slot takes everything that is not voicing, place or manner — aspiration, nasalization, syllabicity — which is why the key writes [pʰ] as “aspirated voiceless bilabial plosive”. Predictable values may go in brackets: “[m] = bilabial nasal (voiced) stop, and [s] = alveolar (oral) voiceless fricative” (Ch. 2, p. 13 of 19), and the same page shows why: “changing ‘bilabial nasal stop’ to ‘alveolar nasal stop’ changes [m] to [n].”

Vowels take the same order: [y] high (close) front rounded, [ø] mid front rounded, [ʌ] open-mid back unrounded, [ə] mid central unrounded. Non-English data forces you to the chart. Hawai‘ian has five vowel qualities, and Table 4.1 gives them in five words that differ only in the vowel: [kiki] “sting”, [keke] “turnstone”, [kaka] “rinse”, [koko] “blood”, [kuku] “to beat (tapa)” — described in two coordinates each, height and backness, with rounding following backness (p. 59).

Transcription runs the same knowledge backwards.

Step 1: sounds, not letters

Ch. 2’s epigraph is the rule: “Don’t speak of letters when you mean sounds.” “Through”, “though” and “tough” share letters and no vowel. Say the word, then transcribe what you said.

Step 2: segment, then run the five parameters

For each sound: airstream, larynx, velum, place (active against passive), manner. If you cannot name one of the five, you have not identified the segment — go back to the chart, not to memory.

Step 3: choose broad or narrow before writing

Broad gives “only the basic symbols, enough to distinguish different English words”; narrow adds “additional articulatory details or modifications, which are often indicated by diacritical marks” (Ch. 2, p. 6 of 19). English aspiration is a narrow detail, so [pʰ] is narrow and [p] is broad, and neither is more correct.

Step 4: stress, then check every doubtful symbol

Put ˈ before the stressed syllable and verify each uncertain symbol by its row and column: [ɹ] for the English approximant, [ŋ] for the velar nasal, [t͡ʃ] with a tie bar for the affricate.

Worked example: [ˈstɹʌk] and [ˈkaka]

[ˈstɹʌk]: [s] voiceless alveolar fricative; [t] voiceless alveolar plosive, unaspirated because it follows [s]; [ɹ] voiced alveolar approximant; [ʌ] open-mid back unrounded vowel; [k] voiceless velar plosive. [ˈkaka], the Hawai‘ian word: [k] twice, voiceless velar plosive; [a] twice, open (low) central unrounded vowel. Neither needed a diacritic, which is what makes them broad; the [ˈstɹʌk] example is constructed, the Hawai‘ian forms are Table 4.1’s.

Pitfall

Five mistakes cost marks for reasons that are not about understanding.

  • [r] for the English approximant. The course convention is [ɹ], and Tutorial A’s key gives [ɹ] as “(voiced) alveolar approximant”. The book allows [r] as a broad cover symbol for any rhotic (Ch. 3, p. 44), but that is a typographic fallback, and in narrow transcription [r] is the alveolar trill.
  • [ŋ] treated as two sounds. “ng” is one segment, a velar nasal at the place of [k] and [g]; “king” has no tongue-tip gesture.
  • Aspiration in the wrong slot. (Other) – voicing – place – manner: “aspirated voiceless bilabial plosive”, not “voiceless bilabial aspirated plosive”.
  • Slash and square bracket. […] is a surface form; /…/ is an underlying form or phoneme, a claim about the system rather than about articulation. A phonetic description in slashes has changed the question.
  • Blank confused with shaded, and the length mark. Shaded means “judged impossible”, blank means “not attested”, and the labiodental plosive is blank on purpose. For length the book permits [a:] and [aa]; write ː (U+02D0) in your own answers.

Reproduce this from memory

  • Four ways in: dissection (anatomy, not physiology), X-ray (bone; the tongue is a faint cloud; unsafe), sonography (safe; tongue surface), MRI (crisp soft tissue, any slice; slow).
  • Three systems divided at the larynx: sub-laryngeal (air), laryngeal (voice, pitch), supra-laryngeal (place, manner, nasality).
  • Five parameters: airstream, larynx, velum, place, manner; pulmonic egressive is unmarked.
  • Manner: obstruents are plosives, fricatives, affricates; sonorants are nasal stops and approximants; a nasal stop and a tap are sonorants.
  • Place: active + passive; [w] two places, [h] the larynx; nine for English, more for the world.
  • Chart: place across, manner down; left voiceless, right voiced; shaded impossible, blank unattested; affricates by tie bar.
  • Vowels: height, backness, rounding; close = high; quadrilateral wider at the top; [ʌ] stressed, [ə] not; a diphthong needs a contrastive offglide.
  • Answer order: consonants (other) – voicing – place – manner; vowels height – backness – rounding.
