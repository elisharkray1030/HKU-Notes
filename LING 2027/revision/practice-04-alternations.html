Alternations: practice

Exercises on the alternation-analysis procedure: identify the allomorphs, list the environments of each, generalise those environments into natural classes, name the phonotactic constraint that motivates the change, choose the underlying form by the elsewhere case, and state the rule that derives each surface form. IPA is in the course's notation: /…/ for the underlying form, […] for surface forms, * for an impossible form, and features with ± in square brackets.

Background: Alternations.

Quiz

Q1

In Chontal the imperative suffix has two shapes. It is [ɬaʔ] in kan-ɬaʔ 'leave it!' and ko-ɬaʔ 'say it!', but [laʔ] in panx-laʔ 'sit down!' and fuʃ-laʔ 'blow it!'. Which type of alternation from Table 11.1 is this?

Chontal (30) is titled exactly this way: "[ɬ] becomes [l] when preceded by a voiceless fricative", and the voiceless [ɬ] is the basic, elsewhere case. The suffix consonant ends up unlike its neighbour, which is the §11.3.4 definition of dissimilation ("a segment changes to become less similar to another segment"), not assimilation, which makes two segments more alike. The trigger is a voiceless fricative ([x], [ʃ]) and the nasal [n] in kan-ɬaʔ does not trigger it. The grounding Zsiga gives for dissimilation is perceptual: two similar segments may be confusable and change to become less so.

Q2

The English negative prefix surfaces as [ɪn] (inedible, inaudible), [ɪm] (impossible, imbalance) and [ɪŋ] (incorrect). Which allomorph is the underlying form, and on what criterion?

This is the lecture's own worked item (Topic 3, "Understanding alternations"): "The allomorph is [in-] before vowels. This is not conditioned by place assimilation – the elsewhere case … Thus, /in-/ is underlying form." The criterion is diversity of the conditioning set, not frequency: Zsiga chooses /d/ for the same reason ("the set of sounds that take [d] is the most diverse: vowels, sonorants, and voiced obstruents", p. 227), and chooses /n/ in Zoque because "[d], [t], [j], [w] and [ʔ] have nothing particular in common" (p. 228). Frequency is never the test — and where two allomorphs are equally diverse, there is no elsewhere case at all.

Q3

In Tagalog, bukas 'open' is buks-in, putol 'cut' is putl-in, kapit 'embrace' is kapt-in, damit 'fulfill' is damt-in — the vowel that disappears differs from word to word. In Yowlumne, /paʔt/ becomes [paʔit-mi] but stays [paʔt-al]. Which analysis is right for the Tagalog vowel?

This is the diagnostic the chapter tells you to memorise (p. 243): "the quality of epenthetic vowels is predictable, and the quality of deleted vowels is not." Yawelmani's alternating vowel is always [i] (or [u] under rounding harmony), so it is inserted; Tagalog's must be /i/, /o/ or /a/ depending on the word (kapit / putol / bukas), so it is deleted. The environment alone decides nothing: both vowels turn up next to a vowel-initial suffix. Hanunoo (44) is the warning case — a single-quality deletion — where "the analyst just has to try it both ways".

Step 1: identify the allomorphs

One morpheme, three positional variants: [t], [d], [ɨd]. Each column is a list of the words that take that variant. The three variants are allomorphs of a single morpheme (the past-tense suffix), so they cannot contrast — no minimal-pair test applies to an alternation problem. The first question is only: what are the forms to be accounted for?

Step 2: list the environment of each allomorph

Write each environment as the sound immediately before the suffix slot plus `__`:

[t] after tʃ__, p__, f__, s__, ʃ__, k__ · [d] after o__, b__, v__, m__, z__, ɹ__, dʒ__, g__, ŋ__, n__, l__, u__ · [ɨd] after t__, d__.

The lists must be exhaustive and in complementary distribution — check that no sound appears in two columns. (The book's own table of environments prints p__ twice in the [t] column; reproduce it as printed — the duplication is in the source, not an extraction error.)

Step 3: generalise the environments into natural classes

Do not leave the list as a list. Each column must be defined phonetically, and no wider than the data licences:

[ɨd] follows alveolar stops (t, d) · [t] follows all other voiceless sounds (tʃ, p, f, s, ʃ, k) · [d] follows all other voiced sounds — vowels (o, u), sonorants (m, ɹ, l, n, ŋ) and voiced obstruents (b, v, z, dʒ, g).

Step 4: name the phonotactic constraint(s) that motivate the alternation

Two constraints, one per changed allomorph. Constraint 1: two stops with the same place of articulation cannot appear in sequence — so a vowel is inserted between them (that is what produces [ɨd]). Constraint 2: obstruents must agree in voicing — so the suffix is voiceless [t] after a voiceless sound.

Step 5: choose the underlying form, then order the rule

Apply the elsewhere test to the conditioning sets: [d] is taken by the most heterogeneous set (vowels, sonorants and voiced obstruents side by side), so /d/ is the underlying form and the other two are derived. Then apply the rule for the most specific case first (Pāṇini's Theorem): the insertion before an alveolar stop outranks the voicing change, so "hated" gets [ɨd] and not *[hetɪt].

Step 6: write the type and the UR in the course's template

Three slots carry the marks. Type of alternation: name the type(s) from Table 11.1 — this paradigm needs two, epenthesis (the inserted [ɨ]) and local assimilation (the voicing agreement that gives [t]). Motivating phonotactic constraint: the two constraints from Step 4. UR: the elsewhere form from Step 5, /d/. The environment lists and the ordered rule are the working that supports the slots, not extra slots.

Full answer

Allomorphs. /d/ has three surface forms by position: [t], [d], [ɨd].

Environments. [ɨd] after an alveolar stop (t__, d__); [t] after any other voiceless sound (tʃ__, p__, f__, s__, ʃ__, k__); [d] after a vowel, a sonorant or a voiced obstruent (o__, u__, m__, ɹ__, l__, n__, ŋ__, b__, v__, z__, dʒ__, g__). The three lists are in complementary distribution: no sound occurs in more than one list, and together they cover every stem-final sound in the data.

Generalisation. "[ɨd] occurs following alveolar stops (if intervocalic, these will actually be pronounced as taps), [t] occurs following all other voiceless sounds, and [d] occurs following all other voiced sounds" (Zsiga p. 227). Each list is a natural class, stated as generally as the data allow.

Type of alternation. Two, applied in order. Epenthesis — a predictable vowel [ɨ] is inserted to break up the would-be stop cluster, giving [ɨd] (§11.3.6's English past-tense example). Local assimilation — the suffix agrees in voicing with a preceding voiceless sound, /d/ → [t], so the two obstruents share [−voice]; that is the same voicing-agreement constraint that governs the [s]/[z] allomorphs. The [d] form undergoes neither change.

UR: /d/. It is the elsewhere case — "the set of sounds that take [d] is the most diverse: vowels, sonorants, and voiced obstruents" (p. 227). Nothing in that set can be unified, so [d] is the form that is left when no rule applies.

The rule (Zsiga's example (3)).

a. The underlying form of the past-tense suffix is /d/.
b. If the suffix is added to a word that ends with an alveolar stop, a vowel is inserted to derive the surface form [ɨd], otherwise
c. If the suffix is added to a word that ends with a voiceless consonant, the surface form is [t], otherwise
d. The surface form is [d].

Motivation. "[ɨ] is inserted to break up a cluster of consonants ([td] or [dd]) that would otherwise not be licit at the end of a word. The change from [d] to [t] after voiceless consonants brings the sequence into line with the constraint that requires obstruent clusters to agree in voicing" (p. 227). The repaired clusters are "either hard to hear (like a second [d] in [hændd]), or hard to produce (like switching voicing twice in the middle of a consonant cluster like [lpd])". In the lecture's wording: Constraint 1 — two stops with the same POA cannot appear in sequence (therefore a vowel [ɨ] is inserted in between); Constraint 2 — obstruents must agree in voicing ([t] after voiceless obstruents).

Order matters. The insertion rule is the more specific one, so it applies first (Pāṇini's Theorem: "the most specific case first"). If the voicing rule ran first, [het] and [ɹɑt] would take [t] and the vowel would never be inserted. The same suffix also shows that the epenthesis half is genuinely insertion: the inserted vowel is always [ɨ], never a copy of the stem vowel, so its quality is predictable.

Reasoning chain: identify the three allomorphs → transcribe each stem's final sound and write it as an environment → group the environments into natural classes and check complementary distribution → name the two constraints each class repairs → pick the most diverse conditioning set as the elsewhere case (/d/) → apply the most specific rule first so that [ɨd] outranks [t] → fill the course's template: Type of alternation (epenthesis plus assimilation), the two motivating constraints, and UR (/d/).

Step 1: identify what alternates

Three suffixes, each with several variants: the accusative appears as -i, -y, -ɯ, -u; the genitive as -in, -yn, -ɯn, -un; the plural as -ler, -lar. The alternating material is the suffix vowel (and, for the high-vowel suffixes, its rounding); the consonants are stable.

Step 2: list the environments

For each word, record the vowel of the stem and the vowel the suffix takes: jel → -i/-in/-ler (front unrounded); gyl → -y/-yn/-ler (front rounded); kɯz, dal → -ɯ/-ɯn/-lar (back unrounded); kol, kul → -u/-un/-lar (back rounded).

Note what does not condition it: the consonants of the stem are irrelevant (k, d, l, z, ʃ occur with both front and back suffix vowels), and the trigger is not necessarily adjacent to the suffix. The conditioning sound is a vowel.

Step 3: generalise

All suffix vowels agree with the root vowel in backness: [ler] after front vowels (e, i, ø, y), [lar] after back vowels (a, ɯ, o, u). The high-vowel suffixes also agree in rounding: [i] after front unrounded, [y] after front rounded, [ɯ] after back unrounded, [u] after back rounded.

Step 4: name the constraint and the type

Turn the generalisation into a constraint sentence about the language, not a list about these eight words: vowels within a word must agree in backness, and high vowels must also agree in rounding. Because the trigger is not adjacent to the target, this is long-distance assimilation — vowel harmony.

Step 5: choose the underlying form — or show why no single allomorph is it

Run the elsewhere test on the suffix vowels. Each surface allomorph is fully conditioned by the root vowel, so the four accusative conditioning sets are equally specific: no one of them is "most diverse", and none can be picked as the elsewhere case. The stored form is therefore the suffix vowel underspecified for the harmonic features — a high vowel for the accusative and genitive, a non-high vowel for the plural — and the harmony rule fills in [±back] (and [±round] for the high vowel) from the root. State that in the UR slot, and note the parallel with Igbo: where two conditioning sets are equally diverse, there is no elsewhere case.

Full answer

Environments. Accusative: [i] after a front unrounded root vowel; [y] after a front rounded root vowel; [ɯ] after a back unrounded root vowel; [u] after a back rounded root vowel. Genitive: the same four vowels plus -n. Plural: [ler] after a front vowel, [lar] after a back vowel.

Generalisation (Zsiga's wording). "All suffix vowels alternate to match the root vowel in backness. In addition, high vowels also match the preceding vowel in rounding." In feature terms: suffix V → [αback]; a high suffix V is additionally [βround] with the root vowel's β.

Type of alternation. Long-distance assimilation (harmony): "two segments become more similar, even though they are not immediately adjacent" (§11.3.2). Turkish is Table 11.1's flagship vowel-harmony example, the same family as the Igbo prefix in §11.2.

Motivating phonotactic constraint. Vowels within a Turkish word must agree in backness (and high vowels must agree in rounding). Stated as a constraint sentence of the kind the course expects: vowels in a word must have the same value of [±back], and high vowels the same value of [±round]; affix vowels agree with the root vowel. The constraint repairs a suffix vowel that would otherwise mismatch the root.

UR. No single allomorph is the elsewhere case: every suffix vowel is fully predictable from the root, so the four accusative conditioning sets are equally specific. The lexicon therefore stores the suffix vowel underspecified for the harmonic features — a high vowel for the accusative and the genitive (Zsiga's "underlyingly high" form, conventionally an archiphoneme, /-I/, /-In/) and a non-high vowel for the plural (/-lAr/) — and the harmony rule supplies [±back], and [±round] for the high vowel, from the root. This is the "no elsewhere case" situation the Zoque exercise's cross-check describes for Igbo: the stored form is the underspecified vowel, not one of its four surface values.

Confidence note. The reading digest flags the [kɯz] row as a low-confidence glyph read; the generalisation does not depend on it, since the dal row already establishes the back-unrounded class and its -ɯ/-lar suffixes.

Why this is harmony and not local assimilation. In Russian (16) or Catalan (17) the trigger is the immediately adjacent segment. Here the trigger is a vowel anywhere in the stem, and the suffix vowel is what moves. That is the "long-distance" part of the definition, and it is why Turkish sits in a different Table 11.1 row from nasal place agreement.

Reasoning chain: list each suffix allomorph → record the root vowel that goes with it → group roots by the feature the suffix copies (backness; then rounding) → check that the trigger is a vowel, not an adjacent consonant → state the harmony constraint and name the type (long-distance assimilation) → show that no allomorph is the elsewhere case and store the underspecified vowel as the UR.

Step 1: count the segments before and after

Compare input and output segment by segment. Two segments that are still two segments can only be assimilation or dissimilation; if two inputs leave one output, that is coalescence; if a segment disappears, that is deletion; if the same segments in a different order appear, that is metathesis.

Step 2: for segments that survive, ask "more alike or less alike?"

Assimilation makes a segment more like a neighbour; dissimilation makes it less like one. Check the direction of the trigger too: it can be on either side.

Step 3: for the sets with two changes, order them

Two of the four sets need two rules. Work out which change must have applied first, by asking whether the second change could have applied to the input directly.

Full answer

A — dissimilation. The suffix /ɬ/ becomes [l] after a voiceless fricative ([x], [ʃ]) and stays [ɬ] elsewhere (after vowels, and after the nasal in kan-ɬaʔ). The lateral loses its voicelessness and so becomes less like its neighbour: "[ɬ] becomes [l] when preceded by a voiceless fricative", and "the voiceless form is the basic, elsewhere case". Dissimilation, not assimilation — the change moves the two segments apart in voicing, and both segments remain.

B — coalescence. /məN/ + a voiceless stop yields one nasal segment that carries nasality from the prefix and place from the stop: [m] before [p], [n] before [t], [ŋ] before [k]. The output is a single segment sharing properties of both inputs, which is the §11.3.3 definition of coalescence ("two segments merge into one, with the merged segment displaying some properties of each of the input segments"), as against assimilation, where two segments "become more similar, but remain a sequence of two distinct segments".

C — deletion (two ordered deletions). The uninflected forms are shorter than the underlying forms the future reveals: /putaka/, /ŋawuŋawu/, /muŋkumuŋku/. First the word-final vowel deletes (V → Ø / __ #); that "exposes" a final consonant, and then a non-apical consonant left word-finally deletes as well (only [l, n, r] may end a word). Nothing is inserted, and the vowels are gone, not changed. The lesson: "Lardil serves as a dramatic warning against assuming that the uninflected form is always going to be equivalent to the underlying form."

D — metathesis, fed by deletion (two ordered changes). Every prefixed form loses a vowel: /u/ deletes medially, so /ka-ʔusa/ → /kaʔsa/ and /duwa/ → /dwa/. That is deletion. Then, only when the first consonant of the new cluster is a glottal stop, the glottal stop and the following consonant switch places: /kaʔsa/ → [kasʔa], /ka-ʔupat/ → [ka-pʔat], /ka-ʔunum/ → [ka-nʔum]. That is metathesis ("two segments switch in order", §11.3.9). The order is forced: the metathesis only has a cluster to work with after deletion has happened, and it never applies in the two-/u/-deletion rows (ka-dwa, ka-tlu) because there the first consonant is not [ʔ].

Reasoning chain: compare input and output segment counts → test "more alike / less alike / merged / reordered" → assign the Table 11.1 label → for the two-step sets, check whether the second change could have applied to the input, and order the rules accordingly.

(1) State the phonotactic constraint these forms reveal, and name the repair. (2) Explain why súper and surfear do not undergo it. (3) The set adapts more than the initial cluster: using smoking → esmoquin, name the further adaptation this borrowed word shows.

Step 1: sort the data into two halves

Some English words begin with [s] + a consonant (student, smoking, snob, stress, scanner); others begin with [s] + a vowel (salt, super, surf). Only the first group is changed, and the change is always the same.

Step 2: state the constraint from the repair

The repair tells you what was illegal. A fixed [e] is prefixed to the [s] + consonant words and to nothing else, so the illegal thing is precisely a word-initial [s] + consonant sequence. Say the constraint, not the repair: no [s]-consonant sequence at the beginning of a word.

Step 3: test the prediction and look for a second adaptation

A constraint makes a prediction; check it against the data you have not yet used. If the constraint is about [s] + consonant, [s] + vowel words must be left alone — that is exactly what súper and surfear show. Then look for a second thing the set does.

Full answer

1. Constraint and repair. Spanish obeys "no [s]-consonant sequence at the beginning of a word" (the course's own wording for this example). The repair is prothetic [e]: a vowel is inserted before the illegal cluster, so #sC becomes #esC. In the §11.3 typology this is epenthesis — "a segment is inserted" — used to satisfy a phonotactic constraint, exactly the way [ɨ] is inserted in the English past tense or [ɪ] in the plural — the plural vowel is the low-confidence read, so follow the printed [ɪ(z)] and flag the symbol.

2. Why súper and surfear escape. They begin with [s] + a vowel, not [s] + a consonant, so they contain no violation for the constraint to act on. This is the control group that proves the constraint is about the cluster and not about [s] itself — if the rule were "no word-initial [s]", these two words would have to be changed as well.

3. A further adaptation: the borrowed vowels, not just the prothesis. The prothetic [e] is not the only thing the set does. English smoking /ˈsmoʊkɪŋ/ is borrowed as esmoquin [esˈmokin]: the borrowed word's own vowels are remade to Spanish — the stressed [oʊ] surfaces as plain [o], and the final nasal-plus-stop ending is nativised — so the adaptation is two-layered, with [e] repairing the #sC cluster and the vowels inside the word reshaped to the Spanish inventory. (Stress placement is a second, equally visible adaptation: the borrowed words take Spanish stress, as the accents in súper, estrés and escáner show.)

Source note. The salt/student/smoking/snob set and the "no [s]-consonant sequence" wording are Tutorial D's; the wider Spanish table (estrés, escáner) and the loanword/cognate distinction for súper vs estado, surfear vs esculpir come from the Topic 3 lecture and Tutorial D respectively.

Reasoning chain: sort the data by the English sequence, not by the word → read the constraint off the repair rather than stating the repair → name the alternation type (epenthesis) → test the constraint on the unchanged words → name the further adaptation the set shows (smoking → esmoquin) so the answer states two things with discussion.

Full answer

Environments (with features). [m] / __ [+labial, −cont] · [n] / __ [+coronal, −cont] (and before glides and [ʔ]) · [ŋ] / __ [+dorsal, −cont] · [Ø] / __ [+cont]. In prose, as the textbook states it: "[m]: labial stops; [n]: alveolar stops, glides, and glottal stop; [ŋ]: velar stops; [Ø]: consonants that are not stops."

Type of alternation. Local assimilation (nasal place agreement): the nasal becomes more like the immediately adjacent stop, and both segments remain. The fourth allomorph adds a second, different process — deletion of the nasal before a continuant — which is why a full answer names both.

Motivating phonotactic constraint. "Nasals must agree in place of articulation with a following stop" (Zsiga p. 225; the lecture's sentence is "a nasal will agree in its place of articulation with a following stop consonant"). That is the same constraint that produces the English in- allomorphs and the monomorphemic camp/wind pattern: here it operates across a morpheme boundary. For the [Ø] allomorph the textbook states the deletion as part of the rule chain rather than naming a separate constraint — the prefix deletes rather than surfacing before a continuant. State that honestly instead of inventing a constraint.

UR: /n/. "The set of sounds that follow [n] constitute the elsewhere case: [d], [t], [j], [w] and [ʔ] have nothing particular in common. We therefore choose /n/ as the basic form" (p. 228). The conditioning set is the most heterogeneous, which is exactly the elsewhere test that gives English /d/.

Confidence note. The source's fricative-initial nouns (ʃapun 'my soap', sʌk 'my beans', rantʃo 'my ranch') are flagged there as low-confidence glyph reads, so the rows used above for the [Ø] allomorph are faha and lawus, where the read is solid. The analysis does not depend on the flagged rows.

The rule, in the textbook's prose form. a. The underlying form of the prefix is /n/. b. If the prefix is added to a word that begins with a stop consonant, the nasal agrees in place of articulation with the following stop, otherwise c. If the prefix is added to a word that begins with a continuant consonant (fricative or liquid), the nasal prefix is deleted, otherwise d. The surface form is [n].

Second thing to discuss: the voicing alternation. Zoque has a second alternation riding on the first. Stems with an underlyingly voiceless initial stop voice after the nasal: [pama]/[bama], [tatah]/[datah], [kaju]/[gaju], [plato]/[blato], [trampa]/[drampa], [kwarto]/[gwarto]. A rival analysis — word-initial devoicing — fails on the data, because it would apply to 'horse' but not to 'rooster'. Post-nasal voicing, not devoicing, is the rule that fits.

Cross-check on the elsewhere criterion. Not every alternation has an elsewhere case. The Igbo 3sg prefix [ó] ~ [ɔ́] is conditioned by the verb's vowel ([i, e, o, u] vs [ɪ, a, ɔ, ʊ]), and "neither alternant can be described as the elsewhere case"; the prefix is then defined as a mid, back, high-toned vowel with tongue-root position unspecified. Igbo shares vowel harmony with Turkish (Exercise 2), but it fails the UR test that Zoque passes — because in Igbo the two conditioning sets are equally diverse.

Reasoning chain: list the four allomorphs → state each environment with features ([+labial], [+coronal], [+dorsal], [+cont]) → generalise: place agreement before stops, deletion before continuants → name the constraint (nasal place agreement) and type (local assimilation + deletion) → test the diversity of the conditioning sets and choose /n/ as the elsewhere case → write the ordered prose rule and name the second alternation (post-nasal voicing).
