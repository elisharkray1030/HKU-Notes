Alternations: analysing them, and what to expect

Zsiga Ch. 11.2–3 — the reading for the 22 Oct class and short quiz 4. Two jobs sit in these pages: run the analysis that takes a morpheme's allomorphs to an underlying form and a rule (§11.2), and recognise the ten alternation types by name (§11.3). The lecture material previews the method and supplies the answer template; the type names are textbook-only, because no Topic 4 lecture slides exist. This page keeps those two sources apart.

The problem: why morphemes alternate

Alternations are not a new phenomenon invented in Ch. 11 — they are what phonotactic constraints do when morphemes meet. Zsiga's set-up (p. 225): "Phonotactic constraints also give rise to alternations. Alternations occur when morphemes are put together in different combinations. If an illicit sequence is created, a change may occur in the way the morpheme is pronounced."

The bridge from Ch. 11.1 is a single constraint. "Nasals must agree in place of articulation with a following stop" "applies not just within morphemes (like 'camp' and 'wind'), leading to a static generalization, but also when morphemes are combined, giving rise to positional variation (and sometimes positional neutralization)". So a constraint you learned as a description of which words exist also predicts what happens at a morpheme boundary. Tutorial D states the causal chain plainly: "The morpheme in some conditions may violate phonotactic constraints / Therefore, the morpheme has to alternate into an allomorph to conform to the phonotactic constraints." The Topic 3 lecture puts it as a slogan: "Phonotactic restrictions are the main motivations for alternations … alternations exist primarily to satisfy the phonotactics of a given language."

Three terms carry the whole chapter, stated verbatim on p. 225: "each morpheme has only a single entry in the mental lexicon: this is its underlying form or basic form. Just as an underlying phoneme has different positional variants termed allophones, a morpheme has different positional variants termed allomorphs. The different allomorphs (the words that are actually pronounced) may also be termed surface forms."

That sentence is the Ch. 10 → Ch. 11 bridge, and it is worth writing out as a proportion: phoneme : allophone :: morpheme : allomorph. Abstract, stored, in slashes on the left; concrete, pronounced, in square brackets on the right. Zsiga then says the method is the same method: "The steps in solving a phoneme/allophone problem and a morpheme alternation problem are in fact pretty much the same. The difference is one of focus." (p. 226) — the phoneme problem looks across the lexicon for one sound's variants, the alternation problem looks at the variants of one morpheme or affix.

Source: Zsiga, The Sounds of Language (2013), §11.2, book p. 225 (set-up, key terms) and p. 226 (method parallel); Tutorial D "Phonotactics" (Clarissa Ki, 5–6 Oct 2026), "Previewing alternations", slides 14–15; Topic 3 "Phonotactics", slide 38.

Choosing the underlying form: the elsewhere-case criterion

"As with phonemes, we can usually identify the underlying form of the morpheme with the elsewhere case, and assume that the more restricted surface forms are derived." (p. 225) The test is the diversity of the conditioning set: the allomorph whose environment is the most phonetically heterogeneous — and widest — is the underlying form. English [d] qualifies (vowels, sonorants, voiced obstruents). Zoque /n/ qualifies: "The set of sounds that follow [n] constitute the elsewhere case: [d], [t], [j], [w] and [ʔ] have nothing particular in common. We therefore choose /n/ as the basic form" (p. 228). Igbo is the honest exception: "neither alternant can be described as the elsewhere case … we cannot decide … whether [ó] or [ɔ́] is more basic", so the prefix is defined "simply as a mid, back, high-toned vowel, with tongue root position unspecified" (p. 230).

The rule that derives the surface forms

Rules are the reason the analysis is worth doing. Chomsky and Halle (1968, The Sound Pattern of English) "argued that an important part of a speaker's knowledge of a language is the rules that derive surface forms from underlying forms. The rules generate (create) the surface forms from underlying forms; thus linguistic descriptions that follow this basic model are termed generative grammars." (p. 225)

One formalism warning, because it decides whether your answer looks like the book's. Zsiga writes her rules in §11.2 as numbered, lettered prose with an explicit "otherwise" chain — not as A → B / C__D. There is no algebraic rule and no α-notation anywhere on pp. 225–231. Anywhere this page shows something like Ø → ɨ / [+alveolar, −cont] __ d, that is standard notation supplied for revision, not Zsiga's text. When rules are ordered, the ordering principle is explicit: "applying the rule for the most specific case first" — an idea "originally due to the ancient Indian grammarian Pāṇini, and [it] is still known as Pāṇini's Theorem." (p. 227)

Source: Zsiga, §11.2, book pp. 225–228 (the two numbered steps, natural classes, elsewhere case, Pāṇini's Theorem, generative rules), p. 230 (Igbo), p. 231 (generality warning); Tutorial D, slides 15–17 (the course's three-step method); Topic 3, slide 30 (answer template). The flagship example: English past tense

The hook is a four-year-old. Zsiga opens with "the alternation between [d] and [t] that led four-year-old Johnny to say 'knowed' and 'teached'" (p. 225), and closes the analysis with "This is the 'rule' that four-year-old Johnny knew and applied in his spontaneous utterance 'I knowed that before you teached me it.'" (p. 227)

The three allomorphs, verbatim from p. 226, with Zsiga's own inflected examples: "three forms: [t] as in 'reached' [ɹitʃt], [d] as in 'mowed' [mod], and [ɨd] as in 'rotted' [ɹɑɾɨd]."

Those three are whole inflected words. The three word lists that follow print something different — the unsuffixed stem: "the pronunciations show the pronunciation of the unsuffixed verb". Keep the two sets apart; they are the same example but not the same forms.

The generalisation: complementary distribution

Verbatim, p. 227: "the allomorphs are in complementary distribution: no sound occurs in more than one list … each list forms a class that can be defined in phonetic terms: [ɨd] occurs following alveolar stops (if intervocalic, these will actually be pronounced as taps), [t] occurs following all other voiceless sounds, and [d] occurs following all other voiced sounds."

Underlying form and the rule

Underlying form = /d/, the elsewhere case: the sounds that take [d] are the most diverse set — vowels, sonorants, and voiced obstruents. Rule (3), verbatim (the caption over it reads "The overall generalization for forming English plurals is given in (3)"; "plurals" is a typo for "past tense" — the content is entirely the past tense):

  • a. The underlying form of the past tense suffix is /d/.
  • b. If the suffix is added to a word that ends with an alveolar stop, a vowel is inserted to derive the surface form [ɨd], otherwise
  • c. If the suffix is added to a word that ends with a voiceless consonant, the surface form is [t], otherwise
  • d. The surface form is [d].

Note the shape: the specific case is stated first and the chain ends in the default — Pāṇini's Theorem applied to a suffix. In standard notation, supplied for revision: Ø → ɨ / [+alveolar, −cont] __ d applies first, then d → t / [−voice] __. Revision line: /d/ → [ɨd] after an alveolar stop, [t] after any other voiceless sound, [d] elsewhere.

Why the constraint exists

Verbatim, p. 227: "The [ɨ] is inserted to break up a cluster of consonants ([td] or [dd]) that would otherwise not be licit at the end of a word. The change from [d] to [t] after voiceless consonants brings the sequence into line with the constraint that requires obstruent clusters to agree in voicing." The clusters being "fixed up" are "either hard to hear (like a second [d] in [hændd]), or hard to produce (like switching voicing twice in the middle of a consonant cluster like [lpd])." Tutorial D names the same two constraints for the quiz: "Two stops with the same POA cannot appear in sequence (therefore a vowel [ɨ] is inserted in between)" and "Obstruents must agree in voicing ([t] after voiceless obstruents)".

The example (3) caption reads "plurals"; it is a typo for "past tense".

Pitfall

Five traps sit inside this one example.

  • The caption typo. Example (3) is introduced with "The overall generalization for forming English plurals is given in (3)" — but (3) is about the past tense (/d/, [ɨd], [t], [d] sentential forms). Never write "plurals" when describing it; the book's own example (1) is a past-tense list.
  • The vowel. The third allomorph is [ɨd] — ɨ, U+0268. Writing [ɪd] or [əd] is wrong for this example. The ɨ read is settled by one consistent glyph across book pp. 226, 227 and 241 plus the tutorial's own text layer ("a vowel [ɨ] is inserted"). The lecture's separate -z table writes schwa (Ø → ə, [bʌsəz]) — that belongs to the plural exercise, not here.
  • The duplicated environment. The (2) table prints p__ twice in the [t] column (rows 2 and 7). It is in the book, verified twice on the page image. Reproduce it as printed; do not quietly "fix" it.
  • Epenthesis or deletion? If a vowel shows up in one allomorph and not another, ask which analysis the data supports. Zsiga's diagnostic: "the quality of epenthetic vowels is predictable, and the quality of deleted vowels is not." (p. 243) Yawelmani [paʔt] ~ [paʔit] takes a vowel that is always [i] (or [u] under harmony) ⇒ epenthetic. Tagalog [kapit] ~ [kapt] needs /i/, /o/ or /a/ depending on the word ⇒ deleted.
  • Two sets of forms. Example (1) lists stems ([ɹitʃ], [mo], [ɹɑt]); p. 226 lists inflected words ([ɹitʃt], [mod], [ɹɑɾɨd]). Answering "the [t] form of reached is [ɹitʃ]" merges them and loses the suffix you were asked to analyse.

The same logic, twice: in-

Zsiga runs the whole procedure a second time on the negative prefix, with no tables at all — one paragraph, p. 225: "Before a vowel or an alveolar, 'in-' is pronounced [ɪn]: 'inedible,' 'inaudible,' 'indelible,' 'intolerable,' 'insufferable.' But before the bilabials [m], [b], or [p], it becomes [ɪm]: 'immobile,' 'imbalance,' 'impossible.' And before [k] or [g], it's usually pronounced [ɪŋ] … the nasal is velar rather than alveolar." The conditioning environment is the place of the following stop; the constraint is the same one that governs monomorphemic camp and wind: "Nasals must agree in place of articulation with a following stop."

The Topic 3 lecture works the same example and shows the UR slot being filled (slides 28–32, verbatim core): "/in/ — underlying form or basic form"; "A morpheme has different positional variants termed allomorphs. — e.g., /in/ -> [in-], [im-], or [iŋ-]"; "– [in-] before alveolar consonants, /h/, & vowels / – [im-] before bilabial consonants / – [iŋ-] before velar consonants"; "[in-] before vowels … not conditioned by place assimilation — the elsewhere case"; "Thus, /in-/ is underlying form." (Note the lecture transcribes the prefix vowel as [in-]; Zsiga's prose has [ɪn]. The allomorphy is the same three-way split.)

Revision line: /in-/ → [im-] before a bilabial stop, [iŋ-] before a velar stop, [in-] elsewhere. The elsewhere test does the work: the [in-] allomorph's environment (vowels, /h/, alveolars) is the diverse one, so it is stored.

Source: Zsiga, §11.2, book p. 225 (the in- paragraph); Topic 3 "Phonotactics", slides 28–32 (the same example as the lecture's own alternation preview, with the elsewhere case and the UR conclusion).

11.3.1 Local assimilation

Definition (p. 232): "The most common type of alternation is assimilation: two sounds that are different become more alike. Each of the examples in Section 11.2 is one of assimilation … The first three are local assimilations: the sound undergoing the change is immediately adjacent to the trigger of the change. Harmony is a long-distance assimilation." She splits local assimilation into partial vs complete (p. 235), and notes English palatalization is "Obligatory within words … optionally across word boundaries".

Examples. (16) Russian, "an obstruent agrees in voicing with a following obstruent": ot papi 'from papa' but od babuʃki 'from grandma'; sonorants do not trigger it — ot mami, s mamoj keep the voiceless form. (17) Catalan, "/n/ assimilates in place of articulation to a following consonant": só[n] amics 'they are friends', só[m] pocs 'they are few', só[ŋ] grans 'they are big'. (19) Korean: jak ŋ mekta 'take medicine' — /k/ → [ŋ] before [m]. (24) Arabic is the complete assimilation: "the definite prefix [ʔal] is realized with a final alveolar lateral before … the 'moon letters' … Before the 'sun letters' … the lateral becomes identical to the following consonant" — ʔaʃ-ʃams 'sun' vs ʔal-qamr 'moon'.

Malayalam (18). Title: "nasal assimilates in place to a following stop (applies to both /n/ and /m/, no post-nasal voicing)." Two paradigms — /awan/ 'he' and /kamalam/ (a proper name) — show the rule reaching both nasals: awam-parajɲu 'he said', awan-taṭiccu 'he became fat', awaŋ-karaɲɲu 'he cried'; in parallel kamalam-parajɲu 'Kamalam said', kamalan-taṭiccu 'Kamalam became fat', kamalaŋ-karaɲɲu 'Kamalam cried'. Environment: a nasal before a following stop; the change applies to both /n/ and /m/, and there is no post-nasal voicing. Revision line: nasal → [αplace] / __ stop[αplace].

Source: Zsiga, §11.3.1, example (18), book p. 234 (title, the two paradigms, environment and revision line). The two pre-[c] forms the digest flags as low-confidence (awaɲ-ca:ṭi, kamalaɲ-ca:ṭi) are not quoted here.

Rule and motivation. Local assimilation is "ubiquitous because strong articulatory, perceptual, and processing requirements all favor it": in articulation it is hard to switch positions quickly, so one state is maintained across adjacent articulations; perceptual confusion from coarticulation can turn an [s] into what sounds like [ʃ] before [i] or [j]; nasals "are particularly poor at conveying contrastive place information", so [np] and [mp] "just do not sound very different"; and "word recognition can be speeded if there are fewer decisions that need to be made to identify a word." (p. 236)

Source: Zsiga, §11.3.1, book pp. 232–236 (definition and partial/complete distinction at pp. 232, 235; Russian (16) p. 232; Catalan (17), Malayalam (18) and Korean (19) p. 234; Arabic (24) pp. 235–236; motivation p. 236).

11.3.2 Long-distance assimilation (harmony)

Definition (p. 236): "Long-distance assimilation is known as harmony. Two segments become more similar, even though they are not immediately adjacent." Sub-types she names: vowel harmony, consonant harmony ("rarer than vowel harmony, but … sometimes attested"), and tone spreading. Featural limits (p. 237): "Long-distance assimilation of nasality and laryngeal features are also attested, but long-distance assimilation of primary place is rare or non-existent."

Examples. (25) Turkish vowel harmony: jel 'wind' ~ jel-ler 'winds' vs dal 'branch' ~ dal-lar 'branches'; the accusative alternates too — jel-i, gyl-y, kol-u, dal-ɯ. (26) Chumash consonant harmony: "the sibilant consonants in a word ([s], [ʃ], [tʃ]) must be either all alveolar or all post-alveolar, agreeing with the final sibilant in the word" — s-ixut 'it burns' vs ʃ-ilakʃ 'it is soft'. (27) Shona tone spreading: "The high tone from a verb root spreads rightward, causing all the suffixes to become high-toned" — ku-téng-á 'to buy', ku-téng-és-á 'to sell'.

Rule. Verbatim (Turkish): "All suffix vowels alternate to match the root vowel in backness. In addition, high vowels also match the preceding vowel in rounding." Revision line: suffix V → [αback] / V[αback]; a high V also matches the preceding vowel in rounding.

One caution: unlike §11.3.1, this sub-section gives no dedicated articulatory or perceptual paragraph — only distributional statements. Do not invent a causal account for harmony and attribute it to Zsiga.

Source: Zsiga, §11.3.2, book pp. 236–238 (definition and featural limits at pp. 236–237; Turkish (25) pp. 236–237; Chumash (26) p. 237; Shona (27) pp. 237–238).

11.3.3 Coalescence

Definition (p. 238): "Coalescence … in which two segments merge into one, with the merged segment displaying some properties of each of the input segments" — as against assimilation, where two segments "become more similar, but remain a sequence of two distinct segments." That contrast is the definition; if your output is still two segments, you have assimilation, not coalescence.

Example. (28) Indonesian nasal fusion, prefix /məN/ (capital N = "a nasal stop whose underlying place is undetermined"): məN + pilih → məmilih 'to choose', məN + tulis → menulis 'to write', məN + kasih → məŋasih 'to give'. Rule, verbatim: "the nasal and stop merge into a single nasal segment that has the place of the stop." One output segment: nasality from the prefix nasal, place from the stop — properties of both inputs. (29) Xhosa vowel coalescence: "/a/ followed by a high vowel results in a mid vowel with the backness and rounding features of the second vowel" — wa + inkosi → wenkosi 'of the chiefs', na + umntu → nomntu 'with the person'.

Test, not just illustration. Zsiga gives no separate causal paragraph for coalescence — the load-bearing point is definitional: if the output is still two segments, you have assimilation; only when nasality from one input and place from the other end up inside a single segment is it coalescence.

Source: Zsiga, §11.3.3, book p. 238 (definition; Indonesian (28) and Xhosa (29) as printed).

11.3.4 Dissimilation

Definition (p. 238): "The opposite of assimilation is dissimilation: a segment changes to become less similar to another segment. Though much rarer than assimilation, dissimilation can take place both locally (adjacent segments) and long-distance (non-adjacent segments). It is thought that the grounding of dissimilation is mostly perceptual: two similar segments may be confusable, and change to become less so." (Her In Focus box adds an articulatory angle: dissimilations "often involve fricatives, laterals, and rhotics" — "the basis of most tongue twisters like 'she sells seashells'".)

Examples. (30) Chontal, local and in voicing: "[ɬ] becomes [l] when preceded by a voiceless fricative" — the imperative suffix alternates ko-ɬaʔ 'say it!' (elsewhere, "the voiceless form is the basic, elsewhere case") with panx-laʔ 'sit down!' after [x] and fuʃ-laʔ 'blow it!' after [ʃ]. (32) Latin, long-distance: "The adjectival suffix [-alis] became [-aris] if another [l] preceded anywhere in the word … The exception is that if an [r] intervenes between the two laterals, as in [later-alis], the dissimilation is blocked" — nav-alis 'naval' vs lun-aris, sol-aris; blocked in flor-alis. Hence English naval beside solar.

Source: Zsiga, §11.3.4, book pp. 238–239 (definition p. 238; the In Focus box at the foot of p. 240; Chontal (30) and Latin (32) p. 239).

11.3.5 Lenition and fortition (and debuccalization)

Definitions (p. 240): "lenition: sounds become weaker or more open. Among consonants, stops change to fricatives, and fricatives change to approximants." · "The opposite of lenition is fortition: weaker, more open sounds become more constricted (stronger). One common variety of fortition is post-nasal hardening: fricatives become stops or affricates following nasals." · "Lenition of vowels is often simply called reduction, and is often related to stress." · "An extreme case of lenition is debuccalization, literally 'cutting off the mouth.' In debuccalization, a consonant loses its oral articulation and becomes either [h] or [ʔ]."

Examples. (33) Florentine Italian, "voiceless stops become fricatives in between vowels": [kaza] ~ [la xaza] 'house', [torta] ~ [la θorta] 'cake', [palla] ~ [la ɸalla] 'ball'; in casual speech intervocalic [x] can lenite further to [h] — "making this a process of debuccalization". (34) Setswana, post-nasal hardening: supa / xo-supa / n-t͡sʰupa 'point at me'. (35) English, lenition tied to stress: "Stressed syllables realize full stops and full vowels, while unstressed syllables realize lenited taps and reduced vowels" — atom [ˈæɾəm] vs atomic [əˈtʰɑmɪk]. Debuccalization, (36) Slave: "all word-final consonants are pronounced as [h]" — possessed ts'ad-é 'hat' but non-possessed ts'ah; the stored form is /ts'ad/ and a general rule reduces it in final position. Also Caribbean Spanish syllable-final [s] → [h] ([lah gatah]) and English [t] → [ʔ] ([kʰæʔ]).

Placement note: debuccalization is a row in Table 11.1 but has no sub-section of its own — this is where it lives.

Source: Zsiga, §11.3.5, book pp. 240–241 (definitions p. 240; Florentine (33), Setswana (34), English (35) p. 240; Slave (36) pp. 240–241 with the lexicon principle in the In Focus box on p. 241).

11.3.6 Epenthesis

Definition (p. 241): "Epenthesis means insertion. Usually, epenthesis applies to break up a sequence of segments that violates the phonotactic restrictions of the language."

Examples. English is the first one, and it is the page's own flagship in another guise: "/wɪʃ-z/ becomes [wɪʃɨz]" in the plural (the plural vowel's symbol is a low-confidence read in the source digest, ambiguous between ɨ and ɪ, so the digest recommends writing ɪ(z); the past-tense [ɨd] below is settled), and in the past tense the vowel "breaks up a sequence of two alveolar stops: /bæt-d/ becomes /bætɨd/ (and then [bæɾɨd] by tapping)." (37) Yowlumne, titled "CCC → CVCC": a consonant-initial suffix would create a three-consonant cluster, which the language never allows, so a high vowel is inserted between the last two root consonants — /paʔt/ 'fight' has paʔt-al (vowel-initial suffix, no epenthesis) but paʔt-mi → [paʔit-mi]; /lihm/ 'run' → lihim-mi. The vowel is high and predictable, and undergoes rounding harmony with a high round root vowel (/ʔugn/ 'drink' → ʔugun-mu, else [i]). (38) Basque, glide epenthesis: the glide [j] breaks up two vowels — [mutil-e] 'the boy' but [ari-j-e] 'the thread'. (39) English consonant epenthesis: "an epenthetic consonant can break up a sequence of nasal followed by fricative, so that 'prince' and 'prints' are homophones" — [sʌmpθɪn] 'something', [warmpθ] 'warmth', [prɪnts] 'prince' (the epenthetic stop is homorganic: [p] after [m], [t] after [n]).

Source: Zsiga, §11.3.6, book pp. 241–242 (definition and the English plural/past case p. 241; Yowlumne (37), Basque (38) and English (39) pp. 241–242). Low confidence: the symbol for the plural epenthetic vowel is flagged in the source digest as ambiguous between ɨ and ɪ, with a recommendation to follow the printed [ɪz] for the plural and flag it; the past-tense [ɨd] is not in doubt.

11.3.7 Deletion

Definition (p. 243): "The opposite of insertion is deletion: a segment that is part of the underlying form of the word is not pronounced. Sometimes deletion rather than epenthesis can apply to repair a difficult or illicit sequence of consonants."

Examples. English, "in all but the most careful speech": desktop is pronounced [dɛstap] (the medial /k/ goes, in a three-consonant cluster) and grandmother is pronounced [ɡræmmʌðɚ] (medial /d/ deletes between nasals, with the nasal assimilating). (40) Tagalog: "When a vowel-initial suffix is added, the second vowel of the root deletes" — bukas 'open' → buks-in / buks-an; the write-up shows a two-step derivation, "/ganapin/ → /ganpin/ → [gampin]" (deletion, then nasal place assimilation). (41) Lardil adds the warning: "The only consonants allowed in word-final position are apical … [l, n, r]. Lardil has a rule of word-final vowel deletion, however … the 'exposed' consonants are deleted as well" — puta 'short' with future putaka-r, so the stored form is /putaka/. "Lardil serves as a dramatic warning against assuming that the uninflected form is always going to be equivalent to the underlying form."

The diagnostic (p. 243) — memorise it: "the quality of epenthetic vowels is predictable, and the quality of deleted vowels is not."

Source: Zsiga, §11.3.7, book pp. 243–244 (definition, the English forms and the diagnostic p. 243; Tagalog (40) p. 244; Lardil (41) p. 244). Low confidence: the ink of the two English deletions ([dɛstap], [ɡræmmʌðɚ]) is flagged in the source digest as a medium-confidence read.

11.3.8 Lengthening and shortening

Definitions (p. 244): "Sometimes, long segments can become short, and short segments can become long." · "Such lengthening after deletion is known as compensatory lengthening." · "The opposite of compensatory lengthening is closed-syllable shortening."

Examples. (42) Komi, compensatory lengthening: "/l/ deletes in either pre-consonantal or word-final position. After the deletion, the vowel then lengthens, as though to make up for the loss of the lateral" — kil-i 'I heard' vs kiː-ni 'to hear'; vøl-ys 'from a horse' vs vøː 'horse'. (43) Yowlumne, the mirror image: a root with an underlyingly long vowel keeps it before a vowel-initial suffix (laːn-al) but shortens before a cluster of two consonants (lan-mi).

Source: Zsiga, §11.3.8, book p. 244 (definitions; Komi (42) and Yowlumne (43) as printed). Low confidence: the source digest prints the length mark in (42)/(43) as ":" and flags the ink as ambiguous; ː (U+02D0) is the standard symbol and the one used here.

11.3.9 Metathesis

Definition (pp. 244–245): "Metathesis means 'changing places': two segments switch in order."

Examples. English historically: "In the history of English, metathesis occurred in r + vowel sequences. The word 'horse' used to be [hros] … and 'bird' used to be [bridde]." (44) Hanunoo is the instructive one, because metathesis comes after a deletion: the language's deletion targets only /u/, which creates a consonant sequence, and "if the first of those two consonants happens to be a glottal stop … the glottal stop and the second consonant switch places. Thus /ka-ʔusa/ once becomes /kaʔsa/. Then metathesis switches /ʔs/ to /sʔ/ resulting in [kasʔa]" — ʔusa 'one' → ka-sʔa 'once'; compare duwa 'two' → ka-dwa 'twice' (deletion only) and lima 'five' → ka-lima 'five times' (nothing happens).

Source: Zsiga, §11.3.9, book pp. 244–245 (definition and the English historical cases p. 244; Hanunoo (44) pp. 244–245).

11.3.10 Morphological interactions

The two regimes (p. 245, verbatim): "Sometimes, phonological alternations apply generally, whenever the appropriate conditions are met. Most of the examples that have been cited in this chapter are like this. The English tapping rule, for example, applies whenever a /t/ or /d/ is between two vowels, the second of which is unstressed. It does not matter if the sequence is within a morpheme ('atom'), across a morpheme boundary ('added'), or even across a word boundary ('up and at 'em')." · "Sometimes, however, phonological alternations apply only in specific morphological contexts. One common pattern is for alternations to apply to affixes, but not within roots."

The worked case. Zoque: "Nasal assimilation in Zoque, for example, applies to prefixes, but not within words, as illustrated by [ɲ-dʒoʔnɡoja] 'my rabbit' … Nasal place assimilation applies to the prefix (/n + dʒ/ → [ɲdʒ]), but not to the [ng] sequence within the noun." The same constraint is active in one morphological position and inert in another.

The four patterns she lists: (1) alternations restricted to affixes (Zoque, above); (2) restricted to a limited class of affixes — English in- is obligatory ("imbalance") while un- is optional ("unbalanced", [n] ~ [m]); (3) lexical exceptions — Turkish [mavi] 'blue' and Igbo [akpi] 'scorpion' break their languages' harmony patterns; (4) general/specific ordering — "lexical rules" (which refer to lexical information: affix vs root, lexical classes or items) versus "post-lexical" rules (general, exceptionless), "following Pāṇini's theorem that the more general occurs after the more specific."

Source: Zsiga, §11.3.10, book p. 245 (the two regimes, the Zoque prefix example, and the four listed patterns). Low confidence: the Zoque form [ɲ-dʒoʔnɡoja] is flagged in the source digest — the initial nasal agrees with the following [dʒ], which supports ɲ, but the read is marked with residual risk.

Q1

A classmate argues that the underlying form of the English past-tense suffix must be /t/, because twelve of the listed verbs take [t] while only two environment rows take [ɨd]. Your reply is:

The elsewhere test is about the diversity of the conditioning set, not the number of lexical items: "we can be secure in choosing [d] as the elsewhere case, as the set of sounds that take [d] is the most diverse" (Zsiga p. 227). A narrow, predictable environment is the mark of a derived surface form — that is what disqualifies [ɨd] and [t].
