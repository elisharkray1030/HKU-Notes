Phonemes and allophones: abstraction, contrast, predictability

Zsiga Ch. 10 (book pp. 199–221), the week-4 reading for the 24 Sep lecture and for Tutorial C on 28–29 Sep. The chapter asks one question repeatedly: what is stored in the mental lexicon, and what can be predicted from context? Its answer is a procedure — find minimal pairs, list environments, generalise, then take the elsewhere case as the stored form. This page keeps the book's step numbering and the course's apart, works Swahili and Florentine Italian through, and names the four places where the procedure stops being mechanical. Ch. 11 runs the same logic on morphemes (p. 225).

Why abstraction is unavoidable

Zsiga's opening move is to say what phonetics cannot do. Chapters 1–9 studied how sounds are produced and perceived; Ch. 10 asks about the relationships between them. Her sentence (p. 199): "An understanding of the physical aspects of sound production and perception is certainly crucial to linguistic study" — but "the next step is to move beyond the physical characteristics to study the more abstract systematic relationships between sounds". That study is phonology, and its object is a pattern, not a waveform.

The evidence that the pattern is real is Sapir's 1933 paper "The psychological reality of the phoneme", which Zsiga retells on pp. 200–201. Sapir listed [dìní] 'it makes a sound' and [dìní] 'this one' as Sarcee homophones; his speaker John Whitney insisted the first ended in a [t]. Whitney was right: with the suffix [-i], 'this one' yields a long final vowel, 'it makes a sound' yields [dìnítí]. The moral (p. 200): "in Whitney's mental dictionary, 'this one' was stored as /dìní/, but 'it makes a sound' was stored as /dìnít/." A consonant that is usually not pronounced is part of the word anyway — and that is where the notation comes from (p. 201): "When the reference is to sounds as actually pronounced, square brackets are used: [t]. When the reference is to a more abstract level (the underlying representation), slashes are used: /t/."

Two more cases generalise the point. Four-year-old Johnny produced "I knowed that before you teached me it" (pp. 201–202), forms he cannot have memorised because nobody around him says them; and Jean Berko Gleason's wug test (p. 202) turned the anecdote into an experiment, with children reliably supplying the plural with [z] for a word they had never heard.

Mazi's case (p. 203) is the one to remember, because it is about contrast rather than alternation. A Japanese-dominant speaker of English produces [ɹ] and [l] perfectly yet cannot recall whether a new street name began with one or the other: "Once words have been sorted into this more general 'bin,' the memory of exactly which non-nasal coronal approximant it is gets lost." Japanese has one such stored category; English has two.

Contrast, predictability, and what a phoneme is

Zsiga offers her definition as a preliminary attempt, then says it needs fleshing out. "A phoneme is a label for a set of sounds that all count as basically the same." (p. 204) Two consequences follow. It is an abstraction: "a phoneme is an abstraction. It is a label for a set of sounds, not a particular sound in itself. As such, a phoneme cannot actually be pronounced." And its members are "positional (or contextual) variants: which allophone appears … depends on the context".

Introspection goes part of the way — English speakers are sure the [p] of "spill" and the [pʰ] of "pill" are the same sound — but it is not reliable across languages, so the analysis runs on distribution (p. 204): "If two sounds are contrastive, they must be members of different phonemes. If two sounds are in complementary distribution, they are more likely allophones of the same phoneme."

Contrast is diagnosed by a minimal pair: "Pairs of words that differ in only a single sound in the same position within the word are called minimal pairs." (p. 204) English [d] and [ð] give "den"/"then" and "bade"/"bathe", and since the sounds are the only difference, "the existence of minimal pairs is proof that two sounds are contrastive, and thus belong to different phonemes". Contrast also means unpredictability: told that a word rhymes with "when" and starts with [d] or [ð], you cannot guess which (p. 205).

Spanish runs the same two sounds the other way. There are no minimal pairs; [d] occurs word-initially and after [n], [ð] occurs after a vowel, and the two never overlap — "In initial position, there is only [d], never [ð]. After a vowel, there is only [ð], never [d]." (p. 205) So Spanish has one phoneme /d/ with two allophones, and a Spanish speaker learning English has to acquire a contrast her grammar lacks.

The Thai/English pair shows that phonemes are language-specific, not phonetic: both languages have [pʰ tʰ kʰ p t k], yet Thai contrasts aspiration and English does not: "Aspiration is never the only difference between two words." (p. 206) Hence the lecture's take-home line, "Phonemes should be in your mental grammar, because they are not predictable" (Topic 2, slide 60).

The terms travel in fixed sets, printed as two blocks on p. 205. Learn them as two rows:

  • Minimal pairs — unpredictable — contrastive — separate phonemes
  • Complementary distribution — predictable — non-contrastive — allophones of one phoneme

§10.2.2: the procedure, and the two numberings inside it

The section starts where students do not want to start: "An analysis must begin, always, with some data." (p. 207) Then comes a trap — Zsiga numbers her procedure twice. On p. 207 data collection is "step one" (skipped in her examples), and "the second step in a phonemic analysis is to identify a set of sounds that are suspected to belong to the same family". Two pages later she restarts: "Step 1 is to look for minimal pairs … Step 2 is to list the environments where each sound occurs." (p. 209), then "Step 3 is to look for a pattern." (p. 210). So "step 1" means data collection on p. 207 and minimal pairs on p. 209.

Swahili is the first worked case (pp. 207–209). The candidates are [ɔ] and [o], and there are no minimal pairs, so the analysis moves to environments, with # for a word edge: "#__ indicates a word-initial sound, and __# indicates a word-final sound" (p. 208). The preceding sound predicts nothing — both vowels follow [g] and both occur word-initially — but the following sound does: [ɔ] occurs before nasals and [o] never does. The distribution is complementary, so the two are allophones of one phoneme.

The step that separates an answer from a list is the move from sounds to a natural class.Listing [ɔ] before [m, n, ɲ, ŋ] is accurate and still not the generalisation, because "phonological distributions seldom, if ever, depend on random lists of sounds with nothing in common" (pp. 208–209). Those four consonants are all nasals; the complement [t, d, k, g, #] has nothing in common, which is what makes it the elsewhere case. Hence /o/ is stored: "it is simpler to state a generalization when the elsewhere case is chosen as the basic form, and the more restricted variant as the derived form" (p. 209).

The course numbers this procedure one step longer. Tutorial C teaches "four steps of analyzing phonemes and allophones" (slide 2): Step 1 minimal pairs, Step 2 describe the phonological conditions for each candidate in each word, Step 3 generalise the patterns without overgeneralising, Step 4 determine the underlying representation — "the one with less predictability and wider distribution (elsewhere)" (slides 14–15). Zsiga's Step 3 matches; her unnumbered elsewhere-case paragraph becomes Step 4. If a question says "analyse", write the four steps: they end where the marks are.

Pitfall

Five traps, each producing an answer that looks right.

  • A phoneme is not a sound. "The phoneme [t]" contradicts p. 204 — a phoneme "cannot actually be pronounced". Slashes for the stored category, brackets for the output, and the same discipline in the answer sentence: "/k/ is pronounced as [x] between vowels".
  • Complementary distribution is not enough. English [h] is onset-only and [ŋ] coda-only, so they are in complementary distribution and plainly not allophones. What is missing is alternation: "there are no sets of related words where one form of the word has [h] and another form has [ŋ]. That is, an allophonic relationship is not supported by alternations." (p. 213)
  • Unpredictable does not mean "free variation". The lecture's decision line ends "If not, we determine it to be in 'free variation'" (Topic 2, slide 24), but Venda shows the other outcome: no minimal pairs and no conditioning pattern, yet two phonemes (pp. 210–211). Different meaning means contrast; no meaning difference means free variation; no pattern at all still means two phonemes.
  • "Step 1" is ambiguous in Zsiga. Data collection on p. 207, minimal pairs on p. 209. Never tell an examiner that her "Step 4" is the underlying form; she does not number it.
  • Minimal-pair hygiene. One sound, same position, nothing else different. Near-minimal pairs — "two words that are almost exactly alike" (p. 204) — are weaker evidence singly; several, each differing differently, establish unpredictability (p. 211).

§10.3: four things that stop the procedure being mechanical

The first is that you cannot always name a basic allophone. English light [l] and dark [ɫ] are in complementary distribution — [l] in onsets, [ɫ] in codas — so they are one phoneme, but neither is the elsewhere case: "It is equally simple to say /l/ becomes [ɫ] in the syllable coda" (p. 211). Her solution is to name the phoneme after neither: example (10) labels it /lateral/, and since "linguists do not like randomness" (p. 212), a more abstract label beats an arbitrary choice.

The second is the relation between similarity and distribution. Similarity is a clue, not a test: "the sharing of phonetic characteristics is a good clue, but not a reliable determinant of phonemic or allophonic status" (p. 213). Venda's alveolar [n] and dental [n̪] sound nearly identical, yet the environment lists overlap on [a__a], [e__e] and [i__o], so nothing conditions the choice and they are separate phonemes (pp. 210–211). At the other extreme, the allophones of American English /t/ are seven unrelated things — aspirated, unaspirated, glottalized, glottal stop, tap, affricate, and zero — and "there is no one phonetic feature that every allophone shares" (p. 212). A family, not a template.

The third is free variation: "Sounds that are in free variation occur in the same context, and thus are not predictable, but the difference between the two sounds does not change one word into another." (p. 213) Zsiga's example is the /t/ of "forgot your", glottalised by some speakers and affricated by others; both are allophones of /t/ there, and the choice carries no meaning difference. She adds a hedge — "truly free variation is rather hard to find" — since such choices are often conditioned by dialect or register.

The fourth is positional neutralization: "two sounds are contrastive in one position, and not contrastive in another. The two sounds still belong to two different phonemes" (p. 214). English /t/ and /d/ contrast — "time"/"dime", "mate"/"made" — but both become a tap between vowels when the second is unstressed, so "kitty" and "kiddy" can be homophones: "the phonemic difference between /t/ and /d/ is neutralized in this intervocalic position." The hedge matters: the preceding vowel's duration may still cue which phoneme was meant, so a linguist "must be careful in claiming that a distinction is truly neutralized" (p. 214).

§10.4: why phonemic analysis declined

Phonemic analysis was the flagship method of Structuralism, and Zsiga ties its fate to a psychological paradigm. Structuralist work meant "fully specifying the phonemic and allophonic relationships among the sounds of a language" (p. 214), and the dominant psychology of the period was Behaviorism — Skinner's Verbal Behavior, Bloomfield's view of language as something a person does rather than knows (p. 215). On that view there was nothing to say about stored representations, so inventory plus distribution was the limit.

Its own edge cases broke it: "Complications such as free variation and positional neutralization, which contradict a simple and fully specified mapping between phonemes and allophones, as well as the failure of complementary distribution to fully predict allophonic status (as in English [h] and [ŋ]), led them to question whether it was always possible or necessary to completely determine the 'family' relationships between sets of sounds." (p. 215) Chomsky's alternative asked what a speaker knows rather than does, and named three kinds of knowledge: a memorised lexicon, a sense of which strings are possible words, and "a set of rules that convert stored lexical items (underlying representations) into actual pronunciations (surface representations)" (p. 215).

Two things survive the shift, and both are on your syllabus. Contrast and positional variation remain central: "minimal pairs diagnose different phonemes; complementary distribution diagnoses allophonic status" (p. 216). And the phoneme/allophone vocabulary is the vocabulary Ch. 11 reuses for morphemes and allomorphs.

Exam shape

The lecture's goal slide is three items — "Define Phonemes vs. Allophones / Determine how to derive allophones from a phoneme / Hands-on exercise on allophone derivation" (Topic 2, slide 3) — so expect a definition question and a data question, not an essay on Sapir.

The data question runs Tutorial C's four steps, and the expected answer has a fixed shape: name the candidate sounds, report that minimal pairs do or do not exist, give each environment as a__a or #__k, generalise each list to a natural class ("before nasal consonants", "between vowels"), state that the distribution is complementary, take the elsewhere case as the underlying form, and write the one-line generalisation ("/k/ is pronounced as [x] between vowels"). The lecturer models the same four questions on the book's Swahili and Florentine Italian (Topic 2, slides 21–31), and Tutorial C then works textbook (9), (10) and (11) on pp. 218–219 — the Russian [l]/[lj], Farsi rhotic and Tiwi [t̪]/[t] problems, all answerable in that frame.

Q1

Language X shows [s] in a__u, #__a, #__e, #__o, and [ʃ] in a__i, #__i. Which analysis is right?

The lists do not overlap and [ʃ]'s conditioning set is a natural class — the high front vowel [i] — which is the diagnostic on p. 205. Two phonemes would need contrast in the same environment with a meaning difference; free variation would need the same environment with no meaning difference. And Step 1 comes first: "if any can be found, the analysis is finished" (p. 208).

Q2

For Florentine Italian, why is the underlying form /k/ rather than /x/?

The criterion is which environment can be stated as a single generalisation and which is the leftover, not a word count and not a phonetic hierarchy: "Because [k] is the elsewhere case, we choose /k/ as the label for the underlying phoneme: /k/ is pronounced as [x] between vowels" (p. 210). The six-word [x] column and five-word [k] column make the counting argument look tempting, which is why the book states its reason.

Q3

English [h] is onset-only and [ŋ] coda-only. What follows from that complementary distribution?

This is §10.3.2's counter-example to mechanical application: "there are no sets of related words where one form of the word has [h] and another form has [ŋ]. That is, an allophonic relationship is not supported by alternations" (p. 213). Free variation fails twice — the two sounds never share a context, and their difference is not meaning-neutral.

Discuss

Take a position: naming the English phoneme /lateral/, after neither allophone, is a better analysis than choosing /l/. Evidence that would change your mind: Zsiga's remark that "linguists do not like randomness" (p. 212), the fact that either label makes the same predictions, and the course's four-step template, whose Step 4 assumes a base form can be named.

Reproduce this from memory

  1. Two levels: the phoneme is stored and abstract (/…/); allophones are pronounced and positional ([…]). A phoneme cannot be pronounced.
  2. Minimal pairs diagnose contrast and unpredictability; complementary distribution is predictable and non-contrastive.
  3. Procedure: minimal pairs; environments with #; natural classes; underlying form = the elsewhere case; then the rule in one sentence.
  4. Zsiga numbers Steps 1–3 (pp. 209–210) and leaves the elsewhere choice unnumbered; the course numbers four steps, with the underlying form as Step 4.
  5. Complications: no basic allophone in some splits ([l]/[ɫ], so /lateral/); similarity is a clue, not a test (Venda); complementary distribution is not sufficient ([h]/[ŋ]); free variation is unpredictable but meaning-neutral; neutralization hides a contrast in one position (tapping).
  6. Decline: free variation and neutralization contradict a one-to-one mapping, so the field moved to underlying representations and rules (p. 215).
