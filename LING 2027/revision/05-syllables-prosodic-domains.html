Syllables and prosodic domains

Zsiga, Chapter 15. Earlier chapters taught you to describe individual sounds, identify phonemes and allophones, and analyse sound patterns in strings. This chapter adds the next question: how are those strings grouped? Syllables, phonological words and phrases are not just labels for speech chunks; they can determine where a sound is allowed, where it changes, and how much prominence a unit carries.

The chapter in one line

Phonological patterns often make sense only when we represent not just which segments occur, but which prosodic constituent they belong to.

2. Why phonology needs syllables

Two kinds of evidence make syllables necessary. First, speakers can count syllables; a theory of what speakers know should account for that. Second, sound patterns refer to syllable position. In English, the relevant /t/ and /l/ variants in a word-medial position depend on whether the consonant is syllable-final or syllable-initial, not simply on whether it is in the middle of a word. That is the same kind of move you made in Ch. 10: infer the conditioning environment from the distribution. The environment is now defined by a constituent, not just by adjacent segments (p. 331).

Syllables also connect Ch. 11’s static patterns to alternations. In Icelandic, a glide can occur at a syllable onset but not in the cited consonant-plus-glide sequences at a syllable end. When an affix would leave the glide without a legal syllabification, the glide deletes. Japanese loanwords repair clusters by inserting vowels; German obstruents devoice in syllable-final position. These are not three unrelated tricks: each is a response to the language’s syllable-structure restrictions (pp. 332–333, 338). Compare Russian word-final devoicing: it still applies at the end of a phonological word before a following voiced sound, so the relevant edge is the word boundary, not merely a syllable boundary (p. 343). That contrast is a reminder to identify the domain the evidence supports rather than treating every edge-conditioned process as syllabic.

Ch. 11 bridge

Static generalisation: a lexicon-level restriction describes which shapes occur. Alternation: when morphology or borrowing creates a bad shape, a repair changes the output. Chapter 15 supplies a possible structural explanation for the restriction: the segments cannot be licensed in the syllable position the string would give them.

3. Syllables: the peak, then the parts

Sonority gives a useful first model

Sonority is the relative openness of the vocal tract, which corresponds to relative loudness. From most to least sonorous, Zsiga’s scale is: low vowels → mid vowels → high vowels and glides → rhotics → laterals → nasals → fricatives → plosives (pp. 333–334). Do not substitute the binary [±sonorant] feature for this ranking: the scale orders classes by degree.

A typical syllable rises in sonority toward one peak and falls away from it. The peak is normally a vowel. This explains why a sequence such as the onset of print can rise from stop to rhotic to vowel, then fall through nasal to stop: one peak supports one syllable. The principle is useful, not a complete definition. English sprints and sixths have sequences that violate the simple sonority pattern; Zsiga discusses special analyses such as word-edge appendices. Adjacent sounds with similar sonority can also make syllable counts vary, as in the contrast between hid names and hidden aims despite the same segment string (pp. 334–335).

A syllable’s nucleus need not be a vowel. In the pronunciation of hidden discussed by Zsiga, the final [n̩] is syllabic and forms the second syllable’s nucleus; English also has syllabic [l̩] in the second l of ladle and [m̩] in prism. The small mark under the consonant indicates syllabicity (p. 333).

Do not overclaim

Sonority is a strong diagnostic for likely syllable organisation, not an algorithm that predicts every syllabification. Treat exceptions and speaker variation as evidence about the limits of the model, not as noise to erase.

The constituent model

To state syllable patterns precisely, divide the syllable (σ) into onset + rhyme; divide the rhyme into nucleus + coda. The nucleus is the sonority peak. The rhyme is nucleus plus coda. A syllable always has a nucleus in this model, but it need not have an onset or a coda. A coda-less syllable is open; a syllable with a coda is closed (pp. 335–336).

These labels let you state a constraint about the right unit. For example, a language can prohibit codas, restrict which consonants can be codas, or disallow complex onsets. In the book’s typology, CV is the least-marked syllable type: languages may require onsets, prohibit codas, or do both. No language requires codas, and no language forbids onsets (pp. 336–337).

4. Syllable constraints predict repairs

Three principles are especially useful to keep distinct:

  • Sonority sequencing: sonority normally rises through the onset to the nucleus, then falls through the coda.
  • Priority of onsets: when a consonant can syllabify as the onset of the next syllable, languages tend to put it there rather than leave it as the previous syllable’s coda.
  • Onset / NoCoda preferences: syllables tend to have onsets and avoid codas, though languages rank or enforce these preferences differently.

Now use them to explain a repair instead of memorising a label. Japanese borrowing adaptation inserts vowels to fit words to its syllable structure: strike → [sutoraiku] breaks up the initial cluster, while baseball → [besubaru] also illustrates vowel insertion alongside other segment adaptations (p. 332). Yowlumne likewise inserts a vowel when a consonant-initial suffix would create a sequence that cannot be syllabified under its ban on complex onsets and codas. When syllabifying, priority of onsets determines which side of the boundary an available consonant joins (p. 338).

Constraints can be more specific than “no clusters.” Languages may limit coda consonants, the number of consonants in a cluster, or the sonority distance between cluster members. English onsets and codas do not have identical restrictions: a sequence that is disallowed at an onset can be permitted in a coda. State the position and the class the data support; do not turn one observed gap into a broader rule than the evidence licenses (pp. 337–338).

5. Morae: a second view of syllable structure

Onset–nucleus–coda structure is not enough for every pattern. Some languages group together a long vowel and a short vowel plus coda, even though one has length inside the nucleus and the other has a coda. Mora (μ) is the unit used to represent this syllable weight: a short vowel has one mora; a long vowel has two. In languages where a coda is moraic, it adds weight too. A one-mora syllable is light; a two-mora syllable is heavy. Onsets do not add a mora (pp. 339–341).

This explains why a process may refer to “two slots in the rhyme” rather than simply “a long vowel” or “a coda.” In Yowlumne, a long vowel and a coda cannot both occupy the limited rhyme; when a root-final consonant must become a coda before a consonant-initial suffix, the vowel shortens. Weight also matters for minimal-word constraints and stress. Japanese poetry counts morae rather than syllables: the chapter notes that a haiku follows a 5–7–5 mora pattern (pp. 339–341).

English happy exposes a tension between two principles. Priority of onsets favours placing the single intervocalic consonant in the second syllable; stress-to-weight favours making the stressed first syllable heavy by treating that consonant as its coda. Ambisyllabicity is one proposed compromise: the consonant is associated with both syllables. The point is not that every analysis must use it, but that different representational principles can compete (p. 341).

6. Above the syllable: phonological words and phrases

The proposed hierarchy runs from the largest domain to the smallest: utterance (U) → intonational phrase (I) → phonological phrase (Φ) → phonological word (ω) → foot (F) → syllable (σ). Morae are sub-syllabic. Chapter 15 focuses on the phonological word and phrase; stress and feet receive fuller treatment in Ch. 16, and tone/intonation in Ch. 17 (pp. 341–342).

A crucial distinction from syntax: a phonological domain is the span within which a sound process applies; it need not match a syntactic word or phrase. Turkish vowel harmony applies within a phonological word, not across a word boundary. Igbo compounds can be one syntactic verb while behaving as two phonological words for harmony. Conversely, a reduced copula or a clitic can attach phonologically to a neighbouring word even though syntax treats it separately (pp. 342–346).

At the next level, a phonological phrase can group words together. Igbo vowel assimilation applies across adjacent words within a phrase. Korean provides a more tightly specified example: voiceless unaspirated stops become voiced between two sonorants, and this can happen within a word or across a word boundary as long as the segments are inside one phonological phrase. Example (24b) gives motun kulim → [moduŋɡulim] (“every picture”). It shows two instances of voicing: /t/ → [d] within motun, and /k/ → [ɡ] across the word boundary. The [n] → [ŋ] before /k/ is a separate place-assimilation effect. The voicing process is blocked when a phrase boundary intervenes. This is the central diagnostic: if a process crosses some word boundaries but stops at a larger boundary, the relevant conditioning domain is not simply adjacency or a morphological word (pp. 346–348).

How to diagnose a domain
  1. Find where the process applies inside a word.
  2. Check whether it applies across a word boundary.
  3. Compare cases where the segment string is similar but phrasing differs.
  4. State the smallest prosodic domain that accounts for the pattern, and note mismatches with syntax rather than assuming the boundaries coincide.

The hierarchy is a model, not a settled inventory with every theoretical question resolved. Zsiga notes open debates about which categories are needed, how they are defined, and whether they must be exhaustively parsed and strictly layered. The chapter’s evidence supports the more modest conclusion: phonological constituents above the segment matter, and phonological rules can be sensitive to them (p. 348).

7. Quick checks

Q1

Which statement best captures the relationship between sonority and the syllable?

The chapter explicitly treats the sonority account as useful but imperfect: syllabic consonants, edge appendices and variable syllabification complicate it.

Q2

Assuming coda consonants are moraic, which syllable is heavy?

Onsets do not add a mora. A long vowel has two; a short vowel plus a moraic coda can also be bimoraic.

Q3

A sound process applies across some word boundaries but stops at a phrase boundary. What is the strongest first analysis?

Phrase-level patterns can cross word boundaries but are blocked at a larger prosodic boundary. A syntactic word or phrase is not automatically the phonological domain.
