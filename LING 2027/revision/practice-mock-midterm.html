Mock midterm: Tutorial E worked through

Tutorial E is the course's own midterm-preparation problem set: three problems across four pages, each one asking you to analyse data you have never seen. It publishes no answers, so the model answers below are mine, written to be defensible line by line: environment lists first, underlying form second, and a named flag wherever the data permit two analyses. Attempt each problem on paper before opening the working.

Related: Phonotactics · Phonotactics practice · Alternations · Alternations practice.

The paper

Tutorial E runs to four pages and holds three problems. Problem 1 is Hungarian nominative versus dative, with parts (a)–(d) and the bracket [3] + [3] + [2] + [2] = 10 printed on the question line. Problem 2 is Spanish careful versus casual speech across word boundaries, datasets X, Y, Z, parts (a)–(c), and it prints no bracket breakdown at all. Problem 3 is acquisition data from an English-speaking child aged 1;11, datasets X, Y, Z, parts (a)–(c), with [3] + [5] + [2] = 10 printed.

The instructions matter as much as the data. Problem 2 part (a) says: "Describe the alternation patterns in X, Y and Z in words, with reference to natural classes (IPA chart 'categories', like bilabial, fricative, front/back, etc.). You do not need to propose formal rules or phonotactic constraints." Part (b) says: "You do not need to justify your answer." So on this paper, prose and natural-class labels earn the marks, and an answer that spends its space on arrows and slashes is answering a different question. Problem 3 part (c) is the only place that asks you to speculate, and it asks for a reason, not a rule.

Step 1: cut the suffix off each dative form

The dative is the nominative plus a suffix, so subtract: ɔblɔk + ? = ɔblɔknɔk. The added material is [nɔk] in the first three and [nɛk] in the last five. Two allomorphs, differing in one vowel; the [n] is stable and is not part of the alternation.

Step 2: find the conditioning segment

For each stem, write its last vowel — not its first vowel, and not its final segment. ɔblɔk → [ɔ]; bi:ro: → [o:]; glyko:z → [o:]; yʃt → [y]; ʃofø:r → [ø:]; kɛrt → [ɛ]; tsi:m → [i:]; rɛpɛs → [ɛ]. The stems end in consonants and vowels indifferently, so the final consonant cannot be the trigger; the last vowel can.

Step 3: test the obvious features, including the ones the data refute

Height: [i:] and [y] are high and take [nɛk], but the mid vowels go both ways ([ɔ, o:] take [nɔk]; [ø:, ɛ] take [nɛk]) — so height alone cannot condition the suffix. Rounding: [ɔ, o:, y, ø:] are rounded and take both suffixes — so rounding does not split them either. Backness does: [ɔ, o:] are back; [y, ø:, ɛ, i:] are front.

Now the two traps. [y] appears in both columns — glyko:z takes [nɔk], yʃt takes [nɛk] — so naming [y] as the conditioning sound is false. And ʃofø:r contains a back [o] yet takes [nɛk], while glyko:z contains a front [y] yet takes [nɔk], so the trigger is not "any vowel of the stem": it is the last one.

Step 4: run the elsewhere test before answering (c)

Set A takes [nɔk]: the stems end in [ɔ] and [o:], both [+back]. Set B takes [nɛk]: the stems end in [y], [ø:], [ɛ], [i:], all [−back]. Each set is describable by one feature value. Neither set is a heterogeneous grab-bag like the set that takes English [d] — vowels, sonorants and voiced obstruents together — and neither is a singleton. So there is no elsewhere case, and the honest answer to (c) is "not possible".

Full model answer

(a) The two allomorphs. [-nɔk] and [-nɛk].

(b) Environments, with features.

  • [-nɔk] occurs after a stem whose last vowel is [+back]: [ɔ] in ɔblɔk, [o:] in bi:ro: and glyko:z.
  • [-nɛk] occurs after a stem whose last vowel is [−back]: [y] in yʃt, [ø:] in ʃofø:r, [ɛ] in kɛrt and rɛpɛs, [i:] in tsi:m.

Described in the course's vowel format (height – backness – rounding): [ɔ] open-mid back rounded, [o:] close-mid back rounded, [y] close front rounded, [ø:] close-mid front rounded, [ɛ] open-mid front unrounded, [i:] close front unrounded. Two back rounded vowels against four front vowels of mixed rounding — the mixed rounding is what rules rounding out as the conditioning feature.

Said as one environment with a variable: the suffix vowel is [αback] when the stem's final vowel is [αback]. Height and rounding are irrelevant, and so is the consonant that closes the stem: [k] (ɔblɔk), [z] (glyko:z), [t] (yʃt, kɛrt), [r] (ʃofø:r), [m] (tsi:m) and [s] (rɛpɛs) occur, and no natural class of consonants picks out the [nɔk] stems — [k] and [z] take [nɔk], but [s], [t], [m] and [r] take [nɛk].

(c) No — the data do not determine an underlying form.

(d) Justification. The elsewhere case is the criterion: the stored form is the allomorph whose conditioning set is the most diverse and least generalisable, and the others are derived from it (Zsiga p. 225, and the English [d] argument on p. 227). Here both conditioning sets are single natural classes defined by one feature value — [+back] vowels and [−back] vowels — so neither is more generalisable than the other, and neither is the elsewhere case. There is no non-arbitrary choice between them. Compare Igbo, where "neither alternant can be described as the elsewhere case" and the prefix is therefore defined "simply as a mid, back, high-toned vowel, with tongue root position unspecified" (p. 230). The parallel answer here: the suffix is stored as /-nVk/, with the backness of V unspecified, and the harmony rule fills it in from the stem's last vowel.

The rival argument, and why it fails. The strongest case for a yes-answer is that set B is wider: five stems and four distinct vowel qualities, against three stems and two qualities. If "most diverse" meant "biggest", /-nɛk/ would win. But the test is whether the set can be unified by a feature, not how many segments it contains — the English [d] set happens to be both the widest and the most heterogeneous, and Zsiga argues from the heterogeneity, not the size (p. 227); Zoque picks /n/ for the same reason, because its set is a mixed bag of [d], [t], [j], [w], [ʔ] (p. 228). Both Hungarian sets unify, so neither wins. Counting words or segments is not the criterion.

In the course's template. Type of alternation: long-distance assimilation — vowel harmony. The trigger is not adjacent: the [n] of the suffix — and, where the stem ends in a consonant, that consonant too — stands between the two vowels, which is exactly what "long-distance" excludes from local assimilation (p. 236). Motivating phonotactic constraint: vowels separated by a morpheme boundary must agree in backness — the course's model sentence for this shape of problem is "Vowels separated by a morpheme boundary must have the same value of [+/- high]", and here the feature is [±back] (Tutorial D, p. 35, Step 3). UR: not determinable; store /-nVk/ with [±back] unspecified on the suffix vowel, and derive [αback] from the stem's last vowel.

The order of the argument, for the exam. Allomorphs (two) → environments (last vowel of the stem, in features) → generalisation (backness agreement, computed on the final vowel, not on the word) → elsewhere test (fails, and say why) → UR (underspecified) → constraint sentence.

What the data permit but do not force. Two other analyses fit every form on the page: store /-nɔk/ and derive [nɛk] by a fronting rule, or store /-nɛk/ and derive [nɔk] by a backing rule. Each is as consistent with the eight words as the other, which is precisely why (c) is answered "not possible". If a later question asks you to choose, choose the underspecified analysis and say that the choice is analytic economy, not evidence. Nothing in Tutorial E's eight forms breaks the tie.

Glyph note. The handout prints ɔ for the back rounded vowel throughout (ɔblɔk, nɔk). Hungarian is conventionally transcribed with ɒ, so if you have seen ɒblɒk elsewhere, that is the same vowel in a different symbol — the page's own symbol is ɔ, and the analysis uses backness only, so nothing turns on it. Described as printed, [ɔ] is an open-mid back rounded vowel; the vowel Hungarian writes ɒ is open back rounded.

(a) Describe the alternation patterns in X, Y and Z in words, with reference to natural classes. Before opening the working, do one thing on paper: underline the last segment of the first word and the first segment of the second word in every row. The pattern is in those two columns.

Step 1: check that the same thing changes in every row of a set

X: paga → pag, la → l, casa → ca, niña → niñ. In every row the first word loses its final vowel and the second word is untouched. Y: mi → mj, me → mj, pague → pagj, porque → porkj. Here nothing is lost; the first word's final vowel turns into [j]. Z: tu → tw, su → sw, como → comw, tengo → tengw, lo → lw. Again nothing lost; the vowel turns into [w].

Step 2: list the vowels that change, and the vowels that follow them

X: every target is [a] — four out of four. Y: targets are [i] (three rows) and [e] (three rows). Z: targets are [u] (four rows: tu alma, tu epoca, tu ijo, su omero — the words tu and su) and [o] (three rows: como, tengo, lo). The following word always begins with a vowel: [e], [i], [u], [o], [a]. So the target set is {[a]} for X, {[i], [e]} for Y, {[u], [o]} for Z, and the trigger set is "any vowel" in all three.

Step 3: name the natural classes

[a] is the low vowel and has no glide counterpart: the glides are [j] and [w], the non-syllabic versions of the high vowels. [i] and [e] are front, non-low. [u] and [o] are back, non-low and rounded. So X deletes a low vowel, Y glides a front non-low vowel to [j], Z glides a back non-low vowel to [w]. One repair is unavailable to [a] for a representational reason, so it gets the other repair.

Step 4: test the trigger, both ways

Suppose the first word were the trigger. Then the change would have to be in the second word — but the second word's vowel is the one that survives untouched in all seventeen rows. Suppose the second word is the trigger: the change is always in the first word's final vowel, and always when the second word begins with a vowel. That fits every row in all three sets, and the paper says the trigger must be consistent across the data, so it is the second word.

Full model answer

(a) The three patterns, in words.

X — deletion of a low vowel. When the first word ends in the low vowel [a] and the second begins with any vowel, the [a] is not pronounced. The consonant before it now stands next to the second word's vowel: [pag evita], [l iglesia], [ka sumilde], [niɲ orgullosa]. The vowel is deleted, not reduced and not glided — the segment count drops by one.

Y — front vowels become [j]. When the first word ends in the front vowel [i] or [e] and the second begins with a vowel, that vowel is pronounced as the palatal glide [j]: [mj], [pagj], [porkj]. Nothing is deleted; the vowel loses its status as a syllable nucleus. Note that [e] and [i] both give [j] — the repair does not copy the vowel, it substitutes the front glide.

Z — back vowels become [w]. The same thing with the labial-velar glide: final [u] or [o] before a vowel-initial word is pronounced [w] — [tw], [sw], [comw], [tengw], [lw]. Again both [u] and [o] give the one glide [w].

In the course's vowel format (height – backness – rounding): [a] is an open front unrounded vowel, [i] and [e] are close and close-mid front unrounded, [u] and [o] are close and close-mid back rounded. So the three target sets are "open front unrounded", "front and non-open", "back, non-open and rounded" — the last one being the only set where rounding goes without saying.

In natural-class terms, the three sets partition the first word's final vowel by height and backness: low [a] deletes; non-low front [i, e] becomes [j]; non-low back [u, o] becomes [w]. If you want the feature gloss: [−high, +low] → Ø; [−low, −back] → [j]; [−low, +back] → [w], in the environment ___ V.

(b) Trigger: the second word. The paper asks only for the phrase, but be able to say why in one line: the target (the segment that changes) is the first word's final vowel, and the condition on the change is the initial vowel of the second word. The first word cannot be the trigger, because a trigger is what the change depends on, and the second word's vowel is never the thing that changes.

(c) Motivation. The one motivation that covers all three sets: a sequence of two adjacent vowels across a word boundary is not allowed — the two vowels may not stand next to each other as separate syllable nuclei — so the first one is made non-syllabic ([j], [w]) or, if it cannot be, deleted. Every row in the three sets is a repair of exactly that configuration, and no row repairs anything else.

A second motivation the paper invites you to add if you think one is not enough: the repair leaves no syllable without an onset. In X the consonant left standing by the deletion now comes before the following vowel — the [g] of pag before [e], the [l] of l before [i], the [s] of ca before [u], the [ɲ] of niñ before [o] — and in Y and Z the glide does the same job ([mj], [tw]). Say it as a compatible motivation rather than a competing one: the data are equally consistent with both, and the paper explicitly allows more than one.

What makes [a] different. The three sets are not three unrelated rules. Spanish has two glides, [j] and [w], which are the non-syllabic counterparts of high vowels; the language uses them for mid vowels too, as [me urge] → [mj urge] and [como eva] → [comw eva] show. A low vowel has no glide counterpart in the system, so the only repair left for [a] is deletion. That single fact explains the split into three sets and is worth one sentence in the answer: glides are available for non-low vowels, and not for [a].

Where the constraint stops. The careful column is the same phrases with the vowels intact, so the ban is not a property of Spanish in general — it is a property of the casual register. Say "in casual speech" when you state it. An answer that claims Spanish never allows two adjacent vowels across a word boundary has overclaimed, and the paper's own careful column is the counterevidence.

Two analyses the data permit. First, this can be described as one constraint with three repairs (above), or as three separate prohibitions — no [a] before a vowel, no front vowel before a vowel, no back vowel before a vowel. The three-prohibition version fits the data and loses the generalisation; the one-constraint version is the better answer but is a choice, so say which you are giving. Second, if a question forces a Table 11.1 label: X is deletion; Y and Z are glide formation, and Table 11.1 files "Llogoori glide formation" under dissimilation (p. 233), which will look wrong for a process that seems to make the vowel more like its neighbour. That is a labelling quirk of the table, not an argument — the paper's own instruction ("you do not need to propose formal rules or phonotactic constraints") means you should describe the process and not go hunting for a type name here.

One more honest limit. Seventeen phrases show a pattern; they do not prove the constraint is absolute even in the casual register. If you are asked how strong the claim is, say what the data support — "in these phrases, casual speech resolves every vowel-vowel sequence" — and not "casual Spanish forbids hiatus".

Write one sentence per set before opening the working. The mark in (a) is one mark per set, so a set you leave vague costs a third of the question.

Step 1: line up the adult form and the child form, segment by segment

X: [kʌp] / [tʌp]; [okej] / [otej]; [fɔɹk] / [fɔɹt]. One segment differs in each, and it is [k] → [t] every time. Y: [goʊt] / [dowt]; [gɹæmpɑ] / [dæmpə]; [dɪɡɚ] / [dɪdəɹ]. One segment differs, and it is [g] → [d] every time; the [ɹ] of Grandpa and the vowels also change, but those are extra, not the shared pattern.

Step 2: name the shared change in X and Y, and notice how the sets are split

[k] → [t] and [g] → [d] are the same change: a velar stop becomes the alveolar stop with the same voicing. X and Y are split by voicing, not by position — X has the change word-initially (cup), between vowels (okay) and word-finally (fork), and Y has it word-initially, inside a cluster, and between vowels. Say this in the answer: it shows you have seen that the paper's three sets are not three unrelated processes.

Step 3: look at Z separately, because it is not clean

[dɔg] → [dɔt]: the final [g] surfaces as [t]. [eg] → [eɟt]: the final [g] surfaces as [ɟ] followed by [t]. [ɟ] is the voiced palatal stop — the velar has moved forward only as far as the palate, and a [t] follows it. So Z is not just "fronting": in dog the result is alveolar and voiceless, in egg it is palatal and voiced plus an extra [t].

Step 4: choose the pattern for (b) before writing (c)

Pick X or Y for (b): it is one change, in every item, with no leftovers, so a single constraint explains it. If you pick Z you have to explain two things at once and the data are too thin (two words) to decide between them, which is a worse use of a 5-mark answer.

Full model answer

(a) The three patterns.

X — velar fronting of the voiceless stop. Every adult [k] is produced as [t], in all three words and in every position: word-initially in cup, between vowels in okay, word-finally in fork. Nothing else in the words changes.

Y — velar fronting of the voiced stop. Every adult [g] is produced as [d]: word-initially before a vowel (goat), word-initially in a cluster (Grandpa, where the [ɹ] is also lost and the final vowel reduces), and between vowels (digger, where the final [ɚ] surfaces as [əɹ]). The shared error is the [g] → [d]; the other differences are additional and should be named as such rather than folded into the pattern.

Z — no voiced stop word-finally. Both words end in a voiceless [t] where the adult has a voiced [g]: dog [dɔg] → [dɔt], egg [eg] → [eɟt]. The shared statement is that the child's form does not end in a voiced stop. egg is the untidy one and should be flagged: the velar is fronted only to the palatal [ɟ], and a [t] follows it, so this set can also be described as fronting plus final devoicing with the palatal surviving. Two words cannot decide between the two descriptions, and an answer that says so is stronger than one that pretends the set is as clean as X and Y.

No type name is needed. Table 11.1's twelve rows have no entry for "fronting" or for a place-changing substitution, so do not go looking for one: the paper asks you to describe the pattern in words, and "velar fronting" is the descriptive term for it. The same advice applies to Problem 2, where the paper says outright that formal rules are not required.

(b) One pattern and its motivation — fronting. Take X (and Y with it). The motivating constraint: the child's grammar does not allow velar stops — [−sonorant, +dorsal] is prohibited — so every dorsal stop is replaced by the alveolar stop that matches it in voicing: a voiceless velar plosive becomes a voiceless alveolar plosive ([k] → [t]) and a voiced velar plosive becomes a voiced alveolar plosive ([g] → [d]). The repair is substitution, not deletion (no segment is lost) and not assimilation (nothing in the neighbouring segments is velar: in okay the [k] stands between two vowels and still fronts, and in fork it follows [ɹ]). The constraint is about place of articulation and nothing else, which is why voicing survives the substitution intact.

If you would rather answer (b) on Z, the motivation is the mirror image: no voiced obstruent word-finally, repaired by devoicing (with fronting in dog and a palatal in egg). That answer is defensible for dog and awkward for egg, and the awkwardness has to be admitted.

(c) Why the wrong constraint gets built early. Four points; the last two are inference, and are marked as such.

  • It fits all her data. The constraint is not arbitrary from where she stands: no word she produces contradicts it, so nothing in her lexicon forces her to give it up. A grammar is only revised when the evidence pushes against it.
  • It is the simpler grammar. A system with coronal and labial stops only is a subset of the adult inventory, and a subset grammar generates a subset of the adult forms — which is what her output is.
  • Coronals are the least marked place. Zsiga's general claim about constraints is that they "rule out more difficult, marked, sequences, while permitting those that are unmarked" (p. 223). Applying that to place of articulation is my inference, but it is the standard reason a fronting process targets dorsals and lands on coronals rather than the reverse.
  • The evidence that will dislodge it is minimal-pair evidence. Adult words that contrast [k] with [t] and [g] with [d] in the same environment — cap beside tap, goat beside dote — are exactly what her grammar cannot yet represent, and hearing them as distinct is what forces the velar into the inventory. Until that happens, the constraint is not an error she can hear.

Why "constraint" and not "she cannot say [k]". The paper's premise is that the errors are phonotactic, and the data support it: the change applies to every velar in every position, including one inside a cluster and one between vowels, and it never applies to [t] or [d]. A purely articulatory account would have to explain why the substitution is always to the homorganic-in-voicing alveolar, and why [g] → [d] tracks [k] → [t] exactly. Say that if the question invites you to doubt the premise; do not spend the 2 marks of (c) on it.

Pitfall

Four traps are built into Tutorial E, one per part. Each costs a whole bracket, not a fraction of one.

  • Hungarian (b): naming [y] as the conditioning sound. [y] appears in both columns — glyko:z takes [nɔk], yʃt takes [nɛk] — so any description that sorts by "front rounded vowel" is false of the data. The conditioning sound is the last vowel of the stem, and the feature is backness.
  • Hungarian (c): producing a UR because the template has a slot for one. The question explicitly allows "it is not possible", and that is the answer the data support. A confident /-nɔk/ with no defence reads as not having run the elsewhere test at all.
  • Spanish (b): calling the first word the trigger. The first word supplies the segment that changes — that is the target. The trigger is what the change depends on, and the paper's hint ("the trigger should be consistent across all items") is there to make you check every row rather than the first one.
  • Acquisition (a): letting egg spoil Z, or hiding it. [eɟt] contains a palatal [ɟ] and a final [t], so Z is not the same tidy one-change pattern as X and Y. Name the shared change (no voiced stop word-finally) and say in one sentence what is untidy about egg. Silence about it looks like you did not notice; building the whole answer on it wastes the set.

Checkpoints

Q1

Hungarian: which description of the environment is correct?

Option 2 fails on two words: glyko:z contains the front [y] and takes [nɔk]; ʃofø:r contains the back [o] and takes [nɛk]. Option 3 fails because [y] and [ø:] are rounded and take [nɛk]. Option 4 fails because bi:ro: ends in a vowel and takes [nɔk] while yʃt ends in a consonant and takes [nɛk]. What the columns actually split on is the backness of the last vowel — which is also why [y] cannot be named as a conditioning sound: it appears on both sides.

Q2

Hungarian, part (c): can an underlying form be determined from these eight words?

The test is the diversity of the conditioning set, not frequency and not markedness: "we can be secure in choosing [d] as the elsewhere case, as the set of sounds that take [d] is the most diverse" (p. 227). Here each set is one feature value — [+back] and [−back] — so neither is more diverse, and the Igbo precedent applies: "neither alternant can be described as the elsewhere case", and the prefix is defined with the relevant feature unspecified (p. 230). Option 3 is the frequency trap; option 4 is wrong twice over — the conditioning is the vowel's backness, not the presence of a vowel, and [nɔk] also occurs after consonant-final stems (ɔblɔk, glyko:z).

Q3

Spanish, part (b): which word in each phrase triggers the alternation?

The trigger is the segment the change depends on; the target is the segment that changes. Here the target is always the first word's final vowel, and the condition is that the second word begins with a vowel: the change happens in exactly the rows where the following word is vowel-initial, and the careful column, which keeps every vowel, is the control. Option 2 confuses target with trigger. Option 4 is worth ruling out explicitly, because the careful/casual contrast is real: register is what lets the process apply at all, but within the casual column the change happens exactly when the next word begins with a vowel, and the paper asks for one trigger consistent across every item.

Q4

Acquisition, sets X and Y: what do they show together?

X gives [k] → [t] in cup, okay and fork — initial, intervocalic and final; Y gives [g] → [d] in goat, Grandpa and digger — initial, in a cluster, and intervocalic. So the change is place-only and position-free, and voicing is preserved, which is why option 1 is the statement that covers all six words. Option 4 is the trap the paper builds in by splitting the sets by voicing rather than by position: the split looks positional until you check.
