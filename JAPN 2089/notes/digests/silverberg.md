## 1. "The Construction of 'Modernity'" (pp. 30–31)

Silverberg opens on Gonda Yasunosuke refusing to send Waseda students to imported theory (p. 30): "Tell us, they asked Gonda, what were the authoritative texts they could find at Maruzen, the emporium specializing in foreign books? They wanted the real thing—Western language theoretical sources. '**Forget it,' replied Gonda, 'there aren't any in the Maruzen catalog. Go to Asakusa—Asakusa's your text.**'" He "demanded personal experience that would give them an understanding of cultural forms that a reading of printed texts (and, moreover, of imported printed texts) could not yield," sending them to Asakusa Park "where female and male laborers from large- and small-scale industries, artisans, and white-collar middle-class nouveaux riches mingled to play" (Gonda 1922a). Her periodisation: by 1921 the live *Chūshingura* dolls, women acrobats, elephants and Meiji peep shows "had receded into history"; popular entertainment meant "theater, *yose* or traditional vaudeville, and the newest and most popular of the popular amusements, the moving pictures in the sixth district of the park."

Self-definition (pp. 30–31): "**I term these critics 'ethnographers,'** because, like Gonda, they did fieldwork in urban, suburban, and rural society in prewar Japan during the 1920s and early 1930s and were concerned more with newly created habits than with folklore. These writers worked to study a rupture in social relationships through discourse preoccupied with mores and customs (*teiō* and *fūzoku*) and in the process exhibited a profound awareness of the ongoing construction of a new culture shared by all but at the same time **differentiated by gender and class**."

Stakes (p. 31): "This Japanese ethnography of modernity is important because it gives us glimpses of the barely explored cultural experience of the Japanese working class. Moreover, it gives us a means of overcoming the standard interpretations of the relationship of Japanese culture to the West … These interpretations have too often been premised on notions of **otherness** that cannot do justice to questions of agency, history, or the Japanese history of the consciousness of agency."

**The anti-"borrowing" thesis** (p. 31): the ethnographers' texts show "how Japanese women and men of different classes integrated the relationship between East and West in different ways, **not through borrowing or a double life** enabling them to switch back and forth from white-collar suit to kimono, but via the construction of a complex identity informed by a type of cultural **code-switching** whereby elements of Western material and mass culture were integrated into everyday practice." She takes the term from Gumperz — "conversational code switching can be defined as the juxtaposition within the same speech exchange of passages of speech belonging to different grammatical systems or subsystems" (Gumperz 1982:35) — and aims it squarely at **Seidensticker's binary "double life"** (1983:90). Caveat: "the notion of one monolithic Japanese 'code' combined with a unified Western 'code' must be avoided."

**Theory kit** (p. 31 n. 1): de Certeau, that "consumption was a form of production because the consumer of images and objects also works with them to make or do something"; Bourdieu's "strategies" vs de Certeau's "tactics"; Lefebvre on social space (used for **Nakano Shigeharu**); but — "de Certeau's recognition of the crucial tension of constraint within agency … **was not developed by the Japanese critics**." Mohanty supplies the practice frame: "humans make their world; they make their world in conditions they inherit and that are not all within their control"; "Culture is thus best appreciated as defining the realm of human choices in (potentially) definable contexts, choices of individuals and collectives as potentially self-aware agents" (Mohanty 1989:19–20).

## 2. Constructivism, and the reconstruction/construction distinction (pp. 32–35)

Thesis (p. 32): "This article characterizes the approach of the Japanese ethnographers of modernity as '**constructivist**,' borrowing a term from European cultural history appropriated by the artist and critic Murayama Tomoyoshi. It then discusses the work of Kon Wajirō and Gonda Yasunosuke in order to ask how inquiry into the Japanese ethnography of modernity can enable us to begin to reconceptualize the aspect of the cultural history of the interwar years that has been referred to as '**Americanization**.'"

Three claims (p. 32): "Kon and Gonda alert us to the construction of a historically grounded and historically conscious culture constituted by practices of daily life. They insert class and gender into our understanding of the state of Japanese mass culture through their documentations of how **the place of production was also a place of consumption**. Their findings suggest that **consuming could be a form of challenging the productivist and essentialist ideology of Meiji, Taishō, and Shōwa Japan**." On dehistoricisation in the 1940s she quotes Harootunian (1988:437): "folk was substituted for class, and the production of place supplanted the place of production."

Roster (p. 32): "The modernist Kawabata Yasunari, the anarchist woman writer Hayashi Fumiko, the artist Kishida Ryūsei, graphic artists such as the stage designer Murayama Tomoyoshi, and the two figures I focus on here, **Kon Wajirō, the inventor of the discipline 'Modernology,' and Gonda Yasunosuke, the theorist of mass play**" — figures who "do not belong to an identifiable movement or corner of the canonized prewar Japanese intellectual world." Their warrant: "The authority of the Japanese ethnographers of modernity (I am purposefully eschewing the terms indigenous and native) rested not on the claim 'I Was There,' but rather '**We Are All Here Now**' building a new way of life, synthetic, syncretic, differentiated by the demands of a capitalism that both represses and demands a creativity by workers (men and women) who wish to give meaning to everyday life." They "made the familiar strange as they constructed ways of being in culture while looking at culture" (Clifford 1988; Marcus and Fischer 1986).

The coexistence claim (p. 32): "The work of the modern ethnographers illuminates how, during the 1920s, **an ethnocentric state ideology and increasingly repressive legislation aimed at the control of culture coexisted with a consumer culture** organized around the consumption of things, images, and entertainment. The results of their research illustrate how this consumer culture revealed class cleavages, and the fluidity of gender and culture identity."

**Kawabata's political weight** (pp. 32–33): his fieldwork-based serial *Asakusa Kurenaidan* defined the modern as "eroticism and nonsense and speed, and humor like the comics about current events, and jazz songs and women's legs." Her gloss: "the erotic and the supposedly grotesque cultural politics of the closing years of the prewar moment *were not* nonsense and therefore cannot be dismissed as tangential to the political maneuverings taking Japan into the Pacific War."

**The 1928 roundtable "A Chat About Modern Life"** (*Shinchō* 1/28:123–47) (p. 33) — her key source for the Americanisation discourse: "The left-wing writers and critics agreed that the source of the *modan* (modern) was America and that 'Americanization' was taking place in Europe and in Japan, but **the question they couldn't answer was: If the cause of the modern is in America what is the source of the modern in America?**" Against **Ōya Sōichi**, whose "indictment of Japanese *modan raifu* (modern life) as lacking content and history, emotion, morals, and ideals remains largely unquestioned," she sets Murayama Tomoyoshi: "The source is in America because America is the world's largest capitalist country" (p. 33).

**The load-bearing historiographical move** (p. 33): "The post-earthquake ethnographers organized their analysis of society and sociocultural change **not around the concept of *reconstruction* as has been argued (Kano 1974) but by the notion of the process of *construction*.** The distinction is subtle but significant—the consciousness of the era was premised not on the idea of rebuilding but of building anew, the same approach that informed the ideology of the Soviet constructivist artists of the 1920s."

Murayama (1901–77) "introduced the revolutionary ideas of the European avant-garde to Japan in 1924, after spending a year in Germany. He called himself a constructivist while engaged in the task of editing *Mavo*, the Japanese dadaist constructivist journal," and inserted *Mavo* (Tokyo) into "The New Arts Journals of the World" beside *De Stijl*, *Blok* and *Stavba* (p. 34). She stretches Bann's "sympathetic metaphor" "to categorize not only the artistic activity in Japan of the 1920s, but also the thinking of the Japanese modern ethnographers and to suggest that the metaphor is an appropriate means of looking at the social forms of the era" (p. 34).

Precision on the claim (p. 34): "The ethnography of modernity was constructivist *not* because Japanese ethnographers claimed membership in any international avant-garde associating itself with the work of Tatlin, Rodchenko, or Malevich, but because it was premised on the same principles as those of the Soviet avant-garde of the 1920s. The Soviet artists (like the Japanese ethnographers) celebrated technology and aimed to eliminate barriers between work, leisure, production, and culture. Their agenda was the revitalization of everyday life through the intervention of an art of the streets. They acknowledged the spectator as participant and their vision was utopian as expressed in their belief in the emancipatory potential of mass culture."

**Against Adorno** (p. 35): they "documented the fetishization of commodities under capitalism, but who also (unlike Adorno, whose conception of the 'culture industry' left no room for resistance) **made place for *practice* in their critique of Japanese commodity culture as the site where the human body engaged with the object world** … the Japanese ethnographers went where Huyssen conjectures Adorno might have gone."

**Keyword** (p. 35): "Synonyms for the term construction, such as *kōsei* (fabrication) or Murayama's *kenetsu* (the act of building), recur and the method for conceptualizing society is not to talk in terms of 'Civilization,' the key word of Meiji …, or in terms of 'Culture,' the term appropriated by earlier Taishō intellectuals challenging an ideology of productivism. Instead, the governing term is often ***seikatsu*, a term best translated as 'everyday practice,'** for to use the more literal 'life-style' or even 'everyday life' would not do justice to the underlying notion of the construction of everyday social activities." Against historians tying *seikatsu* to rationalisation (Minami 1982, 1987) she poses the ethnographers' "clear commitment to the individual and collective creation of culture by men, women, and children who might *not* be, but could be made aware of, the implications of their consumer choices" — "culture as a series of practices was being constructed in the streets. Their project was documentation of these practices, often aimed at transformation."

**Gender note** (p. 35 n. 5): "**the ideological construct of the 'modern girl' projected a transgressive femininity onto mass culture.** Huyssen's thesis regarding the feminization of mass culture corresponds to my own argument about the Japanese modern girl."

## 3. Kon Wajirō: "The Play of Identity, or Putting Things Together in the Present" (pp. 35–44)

"**Kon Wajirō (1888–1973) studied how identity was constructed in what I term the 'play of identity' constituted in the context of commodified material culture.** Gonda Yasunosuke (1887–1951) investigated the identity of play as a series of practices constructed within the class context of Japanese capitalism" (p. 35).

Biography (pp. 35–36): at the 1923 quake "a successful professor of architecture and industrial design at Waseda University"; he had surveyed farmhouses with **Yanagita Kunio** (1918); "After the quake, Kon walked through the burned-out landscape of Tokyo, sensing that there were artifacts and processes he must watch closely." He organised the "**Society for Barrack Decoration**" (*Barakku sōshokukai*) and, with the group Action, decorated "a cafeteria in Hibiya Park and the Cafe Kirin in Ginza in expressionist, dadaist patterns and colors that drew the ire of established architects." "It was not until spring of 1925 that Kon began his collaboration with the designer **Yoshida Kenkichi** on the series of investigations of urban life that would come to be known under the rubric of '**Modernology**' by the late 1920s."

**Modernology defined** (p. 36): "The term 'Modernology' (*Kōgengaku*) was first used by Kon in an exhibition of his research results at Kinokuniya in 1927." He "defined his discipline as an indigenous, unprecedented Japanese study of modern customs and mores. Unlike the Edo period study of customs, but like archaeology, Modernology was to be premised on a scientific method, yet the order of things to be examined was in the present." He acknowledged his project was ethnographic (spelling out "ethnologic and ethnography" in English) but distinguished it from anthropology and folklore because "he was not concerned with noncivilized peoples. The object of his study was the everyday practice (*seikatsu*) of the cultured people of the present."

Design vs folklore (p. 37): "while Yanagita was interested in looking back, **Kon studied the present to aim his findings at the future**" (Tada 1985:111). His position echoed the avant-garde: "**Gendai (the present), he declared, is opening out in front of us!**" And his credo — "**Abandon copying and enter into a creative (*sōzōteki*) everyday practice**" (1931a [KWS I:34]) — the "impulse to borrow from the West … being replaced." His subject-object self-awareness: he "had put himself in a subject-object relationship with an other not unlike that between the civilized and uncivilized or between doctor and patient. At the same time, he found such 'objective' distance necessary: without such loneliness, he explained, his work would be indistinguishable from surveys conducted by bureaucrats."

**Ginza** (pp. 37–39): "Records of Mores on Tokyo Ginza Boulevard." "the ethnographer graphed … combinations of the number of men versus women strolling along Ginza according to differences in time, in space (on the east as opposed to the west sides of the street), and based on occupation … there were twice as many men as women, followed, in a descending rank, by students, office workers, laborers, and children." Illustration 1 (p. 38): **67% of men in Western clothing, all but 1% of women in Japanese dress**, from "observing the dress of 1,180 people on the Ginza" (KWS 1:71; first in *Fujin kōron*, July 1925).

**Why the detail matters** (p. 39): "The pages and pages of drawings and analyses of the specific parts work against a simple dichotomy between East and West … **To focus on the fact that … a certain number of men on the north side of Ginza wore necktie pins and that on the south side only twelve of seventy-eight were so attired, while five as opposed to fifteen (north versus south) were decked out in bow ties … is to shift discussion away from conceptions of alterity into acknowledgment of difference.**" But she is no pure empiricist witness: "The detailed numbering of items in relationship to each other can be read as the obsessions of an empiricist attempt to divide up the world into an infinite numbering of units. Nonetheless, by relating the parts, Kon implied that a new, fluid system was under construction." (n. 8 cites Bourdieu's positivist "who recorded 480 elementary units of behavior in twenty minutes' observation of his wife in the kitchen.")

**Inokashira Park** (p. 39): "Twenty-one practices are recorded as a series of social tableaus of approximately forty groupings of picnickers in motion" — a middle-aged woman eating a tangerine, three children staring at soldiers, someone reading a Bible, eight Boy Scouts. Kon notes "most of the picnic foods consumed are customary, but the doughnuts have appeared recently," and a larger point: "because the picnickers have all come out for the shared purpose of cherry-blossom viewing, **people of different classes are mingling in the park**." (n. 9: two years later he mapped "sites of suicides in the same park between 1924 and 1927.")

**Class** (pp. 39, 42): his early rural work on tenant farmers, the Settlement House, and Ginza — where workers and craftsmen "comprise 7 percent of those on the boulevard." "For Kon the working class landscape of Honjo-Fukagawa is as significant as the middle-class-oriented Ginza or the suburbs." On Honjo: "**The houses are small, the clothes-drying poles many and high, and these rise by each house in counterpoint to the factory chimneys.**" His causal claim: "differences in fuzoku emerge as historical traditions from differences in the natural environment" but they are also "**the difference between rich and poor**." Illustrations 2–3 (pp. 40–41) are paired price-list drawings, "Things Wanted by Men in Honjo-Fukagawa" and "Goods Needed by Women in Honjo-Fukagawa with Prices" — and Silverberg's gendered reading: "**Kon did not comment on his gendered distinction between 'the things wanted by men' and 'the goods needed by women.'**"

**The department store** (p. 42): "Showcase wares were presumably to be treated with the respect owed ancient artifacts in a museum … At another counter, it was as though the lid had been removed from a middle-class household. The department store was supposed to be a place where items were freely bought and sold, and yet **the middle-class customers were anxious, pushing—striving, Kon explained, to imitate the upper class. In the bargain basement, the struggle was intense.**"

**"Survey of Things in a New Household"** (pp. 42–43): a young engineer and his bride; Kon reads the objects "as an archaeologist would survey the partitions of a tomb," but betrays his own judgement — "he is reminded of the grand descriptions of interiors in a Balzac novel … He is saying that these are bourgeois bibelots." Of a bureau (p. 43): "the drawers on the top section are divided so that the husband's are on the left and the wife's on the right," with the note that "the drawers from the top needed to be reversed to preserve this scheme" — a household hierarchy inside the drawing itself. Her verdict on the man: "While the modern preoccupation with buying and displaying objects can be expressive, it is also confined by economically dictated class boundaries and the tyranny of class-related customs" (p. 42).

**Her critique of Kon** (p. 44): "A key question … is whether in his observations he was trapped by his own constructivist (and possibly 'scientific' Marxist) belief in the explanatory power of the diagrammatic." Unlike de Certeau "he did not concern himself with the consciousness of the urban practitioners (whom de Certeau terms blind) … He therefore avoided such ideological framing as de Certeau's argument that practices may be considered as tactical moves and may embody acts of resistance. **Because he presumed the position of an all-observing scientific agent cataloguing the observable social world, he accepted the validity of an all-seeing mapping by Modernology. He could not imagine what the connections of all his itemized lines and dots may have obscured.** … For example, Kon did not examine how state institutions such as the education system helped to organize consumer choices." His Marxism shows nonetheless: "His contention that he wants to leave a record of things on the site of their consumption as they are used and not exchanged is a gloss on Marx's distinction between exchange and use value."

## 4. Gonda Yasunosuke: "Constructing the Identity of Play" (pp. 44–49)

Contrast (p. 44): "a much sharper double edge in his response to mass culture and a much more explicit politics of culture than in the work of Kon Wajirō." He attacked bureaucrats "whom he accused of putting policy before facts," and rejected the tripartite division of life into work/sleep/play, along with "the German theory of surplus energy, and the functionalist American 'Recreation Theory' that gave play the task of rejuvenating the energy lost in labor. In Gonda's view, play was one factor of a social everyday practice."

Bottom line (p. 45): "'construction' was the bottom line: Gonda called for '**play as the creation of everyday practice**' (*seikatsu sōzō to shite no goraku*)." His study covered "the Japanese experience of moviegoing, popular play in the provinces, and the subcultures of traveling entertainers as expressions of practice **generated from below**."

**The state problem** (p. 45): "Unlike Kon, who aimed his data at a nonspecialized popular audience … **Gonda, during the 1920s and 1930s, wanted the state to impose even more censorship and to plan even more cultural facilities.**" The drift: "By 1935, class was no longer an issue for Gonda; he spoke instead of the 'general masses.' By 1941 … he admittedly turned away from 'popular play' to focus on state-organized 'national play'—**from *minshū goraku* to *kokumin goraku***." She calls this "an advocate of a *tenkō*," illustrated by "the transformation of the cultural messages in the cartoons produced by **Okamoto Ippei**."

**Her counter-reading — continuity, not break** (pp. 45–46): "historians have pointed to an abrupt transition in his work in the 1930s … But the career trajectory of Gonda, the bureaucrat-scholar who at the age of twelve came under the influence of the early Japanese socialist organization of intellectuals, the **Heiminsha**, and was then expelled from school for publishing an article protesting the Russo-Japanese War **can also be viewed as characterized by a continuity**. … What needs to be investigated further … is not so much a break in his thought by the 1930s but **his concurrent association with both state policy and left-wing thought and institutions**." Detail: at eighteen he was inspired by **Abe Isoo's** translation of *Capital*; he is said to have "taught socialism" to the Marxist economist **Kushida Tamizō**; he surveyed moving pictures for the state-sponsored **Teikoku Kyōikukai** (1917–20) while working on the **Tsukishima Survey** for the Home Ministry; in 1920 he joined the Marxist-oriented **Ōhara Institute** after resigning from Tokyo Imperial University "in protest over the Morito incident."

Her provisional causal claim (p. 46): "**My current assessment of his so-called shift is that Gonda's awareness of Weimar social policy, linked with his Marxist focus on the practices of the proletariat, prevented him from taking a critical stance toward the intrusion of state into the realm of cultural policy.**" She also insists: "Because of his socialist leanings, Gonda cannot be likened to the Home Ministry 'social bureaucrat' nor was he one of the moralizing, conservative, state affiliated *kyōka* (educating through teaching) ideologues," and "**As late as 1935 … Gonda did not subscribe to an essentialist ideology of 'Japaneseness.'** … even as he was reformulating his earlier premises, Gonda did not turn to a dichotomy that eliminated the West from the Japanese cultural experience."

**Theory of play** (pp. 46–47): "Calling himself a historical materialist during the 1920s, Gonda noted that, just as a series of new practices was being called forth due to the emergence of capitalist economic structures and technologies, new popular play was emerging. **Predating recent analyses of Western anthropologists by half a century, Gonda argued that consuming was not a mere aside to the real business of production.** According to Gonda's phrasing, play was not what was left over when everyday life was cut in half 'horizontally'—it cut through life '**vertically**,' suffusing all aspects." Hence "Asakusa thus was not the equivalent of the early modern pleasure-quarters of Yoshiwara."

Commodity fetishism (p. 47): "Fads … went beyond the cosmetics and other items carried around by women—even the intellectual world was being commodified in a 'race toward thought.'" His gloss on Marx: "people do not determine things; rather, he says, in a gloss on Marx, '**there are things**' and then people appear." And: "**Hats are not made for people's heads; heads are stuffed into hats**" (Gonda 1922b). But "**If Gonda's critique of commodities is much stronger than Kon's muffled barbs, so is his praise for the utopian potential of consumer culture.**" Her best constructivist micro-example: the honorific turns *sushi* into *osushi*, which "was different—it signified a different item, a different practice in the realm of play" (Gonda 1921a). Everyday speech registered the shift: by 1922, "'**Honey, I'm going out for a little popular play**' (*oi kimi, boku wa chotto, minshū goraku ni itte kuru karane*)." On class: "**The *minshū* of the concept 'popular' did not refer to undifferentiated masses: a proletariat wanting for money and free time was at the core of those who desired their own pleasures.**"

**Earthquake** (p. 47): "Before the earthquake, he had thought that 'Asakusa had been there, and then and therefore the Tokyo resident's everyday practices of play had been created.' He realized now that **it was the everyday practices of the residents of Tokyo that had made Asakusa**." Gonda's four stages: "there had been a week of 'absence of play' immediately after the earthquake followed by a two-week-long experience of 'shrinking from play' and two weeks of a 'fervent longing.' Play was then meted out by the authorities as though it were rice for the next four weeks, when the people finally reached a stage of 'pleasurable play' based on their own autonomous, self-motivated actions." New practice: "workers were going to the movies without their families, an unprecedented new practice following the earthquake."

**Massification and the family** (p. 48): "the unification, simplification, and, in Gonda's terms, **massification** (*taishūka*) of play within the city"; "Play was losing a class-based variety and being 'proletarianized' with the emergence of mass forms of play by the early 1920s." On "Play within the household" (*Seikatsu*, 1928): "rich boys, the daughters of the aristocracy, fishmongers, and laborers collected in public to go to the movies, to dance, to engage in sports, and thereby to play outside of family life and in the streets. **Gonda did not bemoan the destruction of an earlier form of family life. A new type of family relationship was being constructed**, he said, as family play was extended, liberated out into the world—now, merchants, salaried workers, wives, and kid apprentices all sported camera bags."

**Ginza vs Dōtonbori** (p. 48): "On Ginza, strolling constituted an activity in and of itself, for its own end, and shoppers at best consumed light food and cold drinks, but never filled their bellies or got roaring drunk. On Ginza, there was nothing in particular to either see or hear." By contrast "the word 'Dōbura' did not make sense, for one went to Dōtonbori not to stroll … but with the specific aim of consuming." Then, against received readings of *ero-guro-nansensu*: "Challenging our common presumptions that the 'erotic-grotesque-nonsense' (*ero-guro-nansensu*) in the sexually explicit nightclub revues of the era were escapism from the horrors of the depression, he contended that **the play of the present was an expression of resistance against material social practices organized under monopoly capital**." His 1930 Asakusa survey "listed each movie theater by name, methods of drawing audiences into theater revues, sizes of business, employees as distinguished by sex and age, and the different sorts of enterprises native only to Asakusa, such as the specialized fortune-tellers."

**Workers, gender, and the end of the culture/other binary** (p. 49): "His 'On Workers' Play' … documented how **play for the worker was not a matter of killing time but an expression of everyday practice as choice. More working women than men chose to go the movies**, and men and women workers considered the consumption of cigarettes and sweets as forms of play. Gonda studied and charted what workers chose to read and how workers made choices to spend their Sundays sleeping, at the movies, or in cafes." And: "while Gonda, like Kon, **preceded Pierre Bourdieu by half a century in relating class to the consumption of differentiated cultural artifacts**, he recognized that *Western* artifacts were being consumed in an everyday Japanese context." The decisive evidence: Chaplin and "Fatty" Arbuckle "had had their day by 1923 and Japanese audiences had gotten used to Western ways … **Western faces had become but faces in a culturally nondifferentiated crowd**," and "the child who did not know the name of the Prime Minister would be sure to identify both Charlie Chaplin and the sword-wielding idol Matsunosuke." Her conclusion: "Such findings call into question any notions of passive or superficial importing of the culture of an other, as argued by the Japanese critics of Americanization."

## 5. "Americanization as Misrepresentation" (pp. 49–50)

Verdict on both men (p. 49): "**Ultimately neither Kon nor Gonda met the goals of their studies during the 1920s.** … **Neither man had a sufficiently ironic stance as participant-observer of early Shōwa culture. Their claims to scientific objectivity were all too easily subverted by state interests** as evidenced, not only in Gonda's celebration of Nazi cultural policy, but in such work by Kon as his *Shūkan asahi* article 'reassessing the beauty of Japan' (Kon 1944)." Yet the payoff: "their study of the constructed culture of the interwar years challenges the vision of Americanism set forth by such critics as **Ōya Sōichi**, and the members of the roundtable discussion on '**Overcoming Modernity**,' who in 1943 were attracted to the 'shape' of Japanese culture as opposed to the process of its construction."

A partial rehabilitation of Ōya (p. 50): "Ōya's essays … were not incorrect, for they accounted for the experience of a **declasse, depoliticized and unemployable group of petit-bourgeois intellectuals** of the late 1920s and early 1930s" — the same group Gonda indicted in his attack on the *modan*, which he equated with the *seikatsu* of the "boulevard" (*burrubuaaru*) or "strasse" (*shutoraase*). Gonda on that street life: such practices were "**constructed in their purest form by a type of people who had no relationship to the everyday practice of labor or of production**." Her rejoinder: "**as Kon and Gonda made clear, the modern was also being made by other classes; the so-called *modan* did not tell all about the constructed differences in the present** by men and women working in factories, shops, and small industries."

On the 1943 *Kindai no chōkoku* group: they "**ignored the class (and gendered) dimensions of mass culture altogether** when they spoke of the 'total life of the Japanese person of the present as he really is.' Moreover, **they introduced racial difference (a distinction absent from the ethnography of Kon and Gonda)** in their discussion of how the world was divided into peoples of color and the white-skinned race (*hakushoku jinshu*)." And: "The Japanese cultural critics of the World War II years spoke in terms of the otherness of Americanism; the ethnographers of the construction of Japanese modern times, like Kon and Gonda, did not."

**Closing thesis** (p. 50): "Their constructivist articulations of the relationship of tradition to change, and of indigenous practice to foreign importation of consumer and material culture **open the way out of our binary crosscultural comparisons**. Of course, the active involvement of the state bureaucracy with the construction and censorship of mass culture by the 1920s, forces ignored by Kon (possibly intentionally) and most probably misunderstood by Gonda, must be taken into account. But as Kon and Gonda make clear, many in Japan during the 1920s recognized that categories of either/or, self or other, would not work. They acted from the premise that **differences were being constructed: differences not between Japan (as an other, in relation to the United States) or between East and West, but differences within the Japanese modern present. Capitalism and the earthquake had irrevocably sundered culture from nature and tradition—those observing and working on the urban landscape could not say that it had always already been there.**"

## Points of contact and disagreement with Gordon (2007), "Consumption, Leisure and the Middle Class in Transwar Japan"

1. **Lineage, not coincidence.** Gordon's thesis turns on Kelly's formulation — "earlier differences … have not been entirely erased; rather, they have been transposed to a new postwar key of differences and tensions" (Kelly 1993:192) — and on "the transposing of difference as much as the diffusing of sameness." That is Silverberg's 1992 argument applied transwar. Her "**differences … within the Japanese modern present**" is the prewar original of Gordon's "transposition."
2. **Shared target: the diffusion/Americanisation frame.** Gordon: not "a simple and happy … story in which the ordinary masses gradually realize a 'Japanese dream.'" Silverberg: "open the way out of our binary crosscultural comparisons." Same enemy, different theatre.
3. **The "reality gap" is Gordon's macro-variable and Silverberg's micro-evidence.** Gordon: only "the 10% or 20% of the population that might fully partake of its pleasures," with the rest in "a world of dreams … however real it was in their cultural imagination." Silverberg supplies the period's own instruments for measuring exactly that gap — Kon's priced Honjo-Fukagawa wish-lists (pp. 40–41), the department store where "the middle-class customers were anxious, pushing" (p. 42).
4. **Parting of the ways on periodisation and on the state.** Gordon: mass consumption and leisure "persisted and even accelerated … both despite, and in some ways because of, depression and war." Silverberg's Gonda runs the opposite direction, from *minshū goraku* to state-organised *kokumin goraku* by 1941, and she stresses a state that "wanted … to impose even more censorship and to plan even more cultural facilities" (p. 45) and whose bureaucracy's "active involvement with the construction and censorship of mass culture by the 1920s" must "be taken into account" (p. 50). **Use Silverberg as the political/censorship counterweight to Gordon's continuity-by-acceleration thesis.**
5. **Both repudiate the wartime-as-aberration "leapfrog."** Gordon attacks Minami Hiroshi's *Shōwa bunka* framing directly; Silverberg uses Minami 1982/1987 but criticises the rationalisation emphasis, and treats *Kindai no chōkoku* (1943) as the ideological terminus of Harootunian's dehistoricisation — "folk was substituted for class."
6. **Two evidentiary registers for the same object.** Gordon: circulation and ownership (80% of Tokyo working-class households with a newspaper in 1919; *Kingu* at 1m by 1926; ~1,300 cinemas and 150m admissions in 1929; ~8m bicycles by 1930). Silverberg: the ethnographers' street census (1,180 Ginza pedestrians; the 67%/1% dress split). A seminar can read them against each other to test the gap thesis.
7. **On the modern girl — complementary but divergent.** Gordon treats her as the media's most-attention-getting figure, and gives the **cafe waitress** (Tanizaki's *Naomi*; waitresses who "outnumbered licensed prostitutes in Tokyo"). **Silverberg's 1992 article contains no cafe-waitress analysis at all**, and puts the modern girl in note 5 as "the ideological construct of the 'modern girl' projected a transgressive femininity onto mass culture." Her substantive modern-girl essay is Silverberg 1991, "The Modern Girl as Militant" (listed p. 54). She also insists the *modan* was a class-limited discourse of "declasse, depoliticized and unemployable petit-bourgeois intellectuals" (p. 50).
8. **Sand's "cultural home" and Kon's "new household."** Gordon cites Jordan Sand on "the beginning of an incessant dialectic between consumers' dreams and their frustrated reality." Kon's "Survey of Things in a New Household" (pp. 42–43) is the period's own version of that dialectic — and Silverberg's reading of the husband/wife drawers is a class-and-gender specification Sand's formulation lacks.

## Course relevance (JAPN 2089, modern Japan)

- **The canonical "constructivist" reading of interwar Japanese modernity** and the strongest available counter-position to "Americanization"/"Westernization" as an explanatory frame — the debate a modern-Japan course exists to teach.
- **Primary-source gateway**: page-precise citations to KWS and GYS for Kon's *Kōgengaku* surveys and Gonda's *minshū goraku* writings — students can follow the footnotes straight into the sources.
- **Method lesson**: how to read a statistic-diagram (67% Western dress for men, 1% for women, n=1,180) as a cultural artefact rather than a fact; *seikatsu*, *fūzoku*, "play of identity."
- **Gender and class tools**: the modern girl as ideological construct (n. 5), the unremarked "wanted by men / needed by women" split, the Honjo-Fukagawa slum surveys, "More working women than men chose to go the movies."
- **The transwar hinge, exactly**: Gonda's *minshū goraku* → *kokumin goraku* shift and the 1943 *Kindai no chōkoku* roundtable link 1920s Tokyo culture to wartime ideology without a hard 1937 break — the joint Gordon's "transwar" argument turns on.
- **Directly pairs with Gordon (2007)**, and with Kelly (1993), Sand (2003), Minami, Harootunian/Najita — put Silverberg's Ginza census beside Gordon's bicycle and cinema-admission figures and the "reality gap" thesis becomes testable.
- **Historiographical literacy**: de Certeau, Bourdieu, Adorno, Huyssen, Clifford, Marcus and Fischer, Gumperz, Bann, Lefebvre, Mohanty — and Silverberg's own hedging ("The application of a notion of code-switching requires further development") as a model of theory held provisionally.
- **Critical, not celebratory**: on one page she praises the method and indicts "their claims to scientific objectivity," Kon's 1944 *Shūkan asahi* "reassessment of the beauty of Japan," and "Gonda's celebration of Nazi cultural policy."
- **Cross-module links**: Kawabata Yasunari, Hayashi Fumiko, Kishida Ryūsei and Murayama Tomoyoshi are named as part of the circle (p. 32), connecting the social-history unit to the literature and art units — though the article analyses none of them in depth, which is itself a good seminar question.
- **Useful gap to assign on**: the article's gender analysis is thinner than its class analysis, and its German/Soviet/Weimar comparisons (Weimar social policy, Soviet constructivism, Nazi KdF) are asserted more than demonstrated — fair targets for student critique.
