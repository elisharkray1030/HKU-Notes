## 1. Thesis and framing (pp. 295–99)

Abstract (p. 295): the disaster was “interpreted and used for political, ideological, and social ends”; commentators read it “as an act of divine punishment to admonish Japan’s subjects for leading what many claimed were self-centered, immoral, and extravagant lifestyles.” The result was “a culture of catastrophe and reconstruction that amplified discourses of moral degeneracy and national renovation in interwar Japan.”

Key concept (p. 296): the quake “fostered what I consider to be a culture of catastrophe in Japan: a mindset, discourse, and set of actions intimately shaped by the disaster and its aftermath,” defined by “an intense sense of reflection, opportunism, and eventually contestation.” It legitimated “the discourse of perceived national regress and moral decline and the discourse of national reconstruction” (p. 296).

Three claims (p. 297): commentators across the spectrum pressed the quake “as a moral wake-up call if not an outright act of divine punishment (tenken, tenbatsu) and as a golden opportunity (kōki)”; they saw “the chance to arrest the perceived moral and ideological regress of Japan,” though prescriptions differed; and reconstruction “became mired in contestation” that “foreshadowed the larger ideological debates that erupted with increased regularity in late Taisho and early Showa Japan between liberals and what James Scott referred to as ‘authoritarian high modernists’.”

Theory (p. 297): Bankoff — disasters “are embedded in the political structures, economic systems and social orders of the societies in which they take place”; Bloch — “the progress of a great calamity yields valuable information about the nature of a society.” Positioning (pp. 298–99): some English-language works cover “the massacre of Koreans and suppression of anarchists and socialists in early September 1923”; urban-planning scholarship (Ishida, Koshizawa, Sorensen, Hein, Nakamura, Mochida) is treated separately.

## 2. Loss at the epicenter (pp. 299–302)

Scale (p. 296): “the magnitude 7.9 earthquake, fires, and aftershocks destroyed over 45 per cent of structures in Tokyo and over 90 percent of structures in Yokohama. Economic costs of the calamity surpassed ¥6.5 billion, a figure four times larger than Japan’s national budget for 1923. In human terms, the disaster rendered just over 2.5 million persons homeless and killed over 110,000 residents.” Shimamoto Ainosuke said it “overturned Japan’s culture from its very foundation”; Ugaki Kazushige called post-quake Japan “a watershed between prosperity and decline of the nation.”

The event began (p. 299) “At two minutes to noon on Saturday, September 1, 1923,” epicenter “60 kilometers south-southwest of Tokyo,” releasing “energy equal to the detonation of 400 Hiroshima-size atomic bombs.” Fire killed most: “Within 30 minutes of the first tremor, more than 130 major fires broke out across Tokyo alone” (p. 300). At the former Honjo Clothing Depot over “30,000 individuals” sheltered; air hit “46 degrees Celsius” and “five whirlwind firestorms erupted,” the last engulfing the site (p. 301). World War I supplied the benchmark by which elites quantified the loss (p. 302).

## 3. Warning, divine punishment, necessary evil (pp. 302–307)

Core claim (p. 303): disasters “are, apart from being natural phenomena, also imaginative constructions of reality influenced by the social, political, cultural, religious, and ideological environments in which they take place.” Pre-quake Tokyo “had become a center of hedonistic consumer culture, entertainment, decadence, political unrest, and labor agitation” (p. 303). The finding: “numerous commentators and bureaucratic elites from diverse social, professional, and ideological backgrounds all found a significant degree of common ground” — sometimes “a necessary evil that destroyed the most corrupt part of the nation.” Exponents were “military men, intellectuals, social welfare advocates, city planners, priests, liberal economists, government bureaucrats, and university professors”; Colonel Shitennō Nobutaka reported that “the divine punishment theory was felt acutely.”

**Fukasaku Yasubumi** supplied the central diagnosis (p. 304): “Everyone must remember that many of us ran to flippancy, lacked steadiness, lapsed into luxury, and forgot diligence and frugality. Particularly, the tendency of sexual slackness [seiteki shikan] was deplorable and there were so many scandalous events. People fervently sang the praises of materialism; ideals lacked luster; selfishness became dominant; and the gallant spirit of devotion was swept away. At such a time, the heavens [ten] cracked down a great hammer for the sake of our race, waking us up from idleness, urging us to reflect on our past deeds.”

The same reading recurred across the spectrum. **Suematsu Kaiichirō** called it “a divine warning,” hoping people would “turn this misfortune into a blessing” (p. 304). **Tawara Magoichi** wanted it to “start a revolution in people’s minds.” **Ugaki** termed it “divine punishment against us who had aspired to a culture of materialism and degrading thoughts” (p. 305). **Okutani Fumitomo** told readers “to simply look at a map”: districts that “served to satisfy people’s greed and desire” burned, and Japan “must thank god sincerely for his infinite kindness” (pp. 305–306). **Horie Kiichi** found “truth to the theory that this earthquake is heaven’s [ten] punishment” (p. 306). **Fukuda Tokuzō**: as the London Fire of 1666 “eradicated the Black Plague,” so the “total destruction of Tokyo was a form of purification” (pp. 306–307). **Abe Iso** greeted the “baptism by fire”: “we cannot help but feel inspired” (p. 307). **Shimamoto** called the quake “the ‘mother of all happiness’” (p. 307; the printed text misprints the inner quotation mark) — read by Schencking as “a great but overly optimistic expectation.”

## 4. Reconstructing the people and the “popular mind” (pp. 307–312)

“The discourses of moral regress and national reconstruction had been ingrained in society prior to the Great Kanto Earthquake. The disaster, however, amplified both discourses” (p. 308); “the earthquake compelled intense introspection,” and Privy Councilor Ichiki Kitokurō said it “provided a rare opportunity to reflect upon our attitudes,” enabling the “reinvigoration of the popular mind.” Yet “while various reformers shared the same overall objective—reinvigoration of the popular mind and reconstruction of society—prescriptions for obtaining these ends were markedly different” (pp. 308–9; the quotation spans the page break).

**Takashima Heizaburō** preached household gradualism — “to eat [only] brown rice on the first of each month to commemorate the earthquake” (p. 309). **Fukasaku** wanted state media and exploited shared suffering: “aristocrats and common people had all suffered a common misfortune” (p. 310).

The **Imperial Rescript Enjoining Sincere and Strenuous Life of November 11, 1923** (Yamamoto Gonnohyōe cabinet) is the centrepiece (p. 310), tied by association with the Imperial Rescript on Education of 1890 and the Boshin Rescript of 1908. It “lamented the frivolous and extravagant habits,” warning that otherwise “the fate of the nation might well be sealed”; Fukasaku called it the “national bible.” **Arima Sukemasa** offered General Nogi Maresuke as the “embodiment of true, fortitudinous national spirit” (p. 311). **Miyake Setsurei** pressed continuous training, holding that “our brain grows stronger and stronger if we train it at a time of upheaval” (pp. 311–12; the quotation spans the page break). **Abe** backed Japan Women’s University’s ban on “extravagant silks” and urged outlawing alcohol and abolishing prostitution (p. 312).

## 5. Reconstructing the capital (pp. 312–318)

“A great reconstruction plan,” in Nagai Tōru’s phrase, would let the state “respond to the needs of the new era materially and spiritually in order to renovate society” (p. 313). Planners asked whether “public lecture halls could replace dance halls and popular cinemas,” and whether “brothels could be turned into maternity hospitals and daycare facilities for working mothers” (p. 313) — Schencking’s gloss is Scott’s “high modernist city.”

**Gotō Shinpei** “embodied the opportunistic spirit of reconstruction”: his colonial service in Taiwan and Manchuria gave him “ample experience in shaping built environments with little effective opposition or input from colonial subjects” (p. 314). His 1921 “¥800 million proposal” was “rejected by the finance ministers of three successive cabinets.” Mizuno Rentarō recalls him on September 2: “The very first thing Gotō said to me was, Tokyo has been reduced to its former state of Musashino. … This is our best chance” (p. 314). The September 6 plan — buying “all of the burned-out and rubble-laden land of Tokyo, roughly 33 million square meters” at an estimated “¥4 billion, a figure that was more than double the national budget of 1923” — was rejected; Schencking calls it “equal parts opportunism and fantasy.” Gotō then pressed for a Reconstruction Ministry (Teito Fukkōshō) “answerable directly to the emperor” (pp. 314–15). **Abe** wanted a “fundamental project for the construction of a new city,” and, for “spiritual hygiene,” “turning brothels and drinking establishments into centers for public welfare” (p. 316). **Fukuda** called restoring old Tokyo “akin to Japan turning its back on a progressive future,” because pre-quake Tokyo was “nothing more than an extension of Edo” (pp. 316–17).

Planners anticipated landowner resistance and stressed “the virtues and ideology of sacrifice”: landowners must “forsake their personal interest and display a spirit of public-mindedness” (p. 317). **Yamakawa Hitoshi** “wanted the state to capitalize on the catastrophe and seize all privately held land in the disaster-stricken regions,” while **Ugaki** sought “aggressive new taxes and forced savings measures targeting the upper classes” (pp. 317–18).

## 6. The culture of contestation (pp. 318–323)

“Along with reflection and opportunism, contestation became a fundamental component of the culture of catastrophe”; the disaster “exposed and widened many of the political and ideological fissures” of post–World War I Japan and “exacerbated urban-rural tensions as well as bureaucratic rivalries” (p. 318).

- **Finance and restraint.** Minister of Finance Inoue Junnosuke called smaller plans (¥1.3–3 billion) “reckless” or “problematic” and “refused to raise new taxes” (p. 319). **Wakatsuki Reijirō** advised “Begin with what is absolutely necessary” and “then gradually improve upon them.” The Teito Fukkō Shingikai “became dominated by voices of financial conservatism and liberal political restraint” (p. 320); **Itō Miyoji** held that rezoning or confiscation “went against the spirit of the constitution.”
- **Rural versus urban.** Speaker Matsuda Genji: “It would be improper … for the whole nation if the government spent a large amount of national expenditures on Tokyo’s city plan” (p. 320); many argued “the countryside, not Tokyo, was the true heart of Japan” (p. 321).
- **Legal ceiling and outcome.** The state “could take up to ten per cent of all privately held land without paying compensation” (p. 322). The Diet cut the budget to “a paltry ¥468 million in December 1923”; total expended “was ¥649 million”; social welfare was “¥4,525,000, or an equivalent of 0.69 per cent of the overall reconstruction budget.” Of 31,166,264 square metres earmarked for land readjustment, only 2,938,050 (9.4 per cent) went to nonresidential purposes.

## 7. Reflections and recriminations (pp. 323–328)

Some planners preferred “what they considered to be ‘blank pages’ in colonial Manchukuo” (p. 323). **Gotō’s** December 1923 memo blamed “the ignorance and selfishness of the citizens of Tokyo,” who “did not thoroughly understand the meaning of reconstruction” (p. 324); he later took the NHK governorship to “reach, guide, and educate the masses.” **Fukuda** concluded “the first task must be to secure complete universal suffrage” (p. 324).

**Charles Beard**, invited by Gotō, “arrived on October 6” and “left Japan on November 16” (p. 325). His verdict: “The day when a dictator could by the wave of a wand order a new city to spring up … is past: hydra-headed democracy or near-democracy is not ready for the task.” Schencking notes this reveals “his high authoritarian impulses rather than the progressivism for which he is better known.”

Spiritual reconstruction also failed. **Nose Yoritoshi** (1925) invoked “Danger past—god forgotten,” likening old habits to “a snake coiling around our necks” (p. 326). Prime Minister **Katō Takaaki** “virtually apologized to the nation,” conceding the “evil habits of extravagance and self indulgence” still overwhelmed the “beautiful customs of hard-working frugality” (p. 327). Schencking’s caveat: luxuries were “viewed merely as modern conveniences or hard-earned rewards by others” (p. 327).

## 8. Conclusion (pp. 328–331)

The disaster became “a defining cultural moment that was pregnant with meaning and thus ripe for interpretive use and manipulation,” helping Japan’s leaders “proselytize an anticonsumption, antiluxury, and antiextravagance discourse across the nation” that “remained ever-present throughout the interwar and wartime periods in Japan” (p. 328). It also “strengthened an emergent discourse associated with the moral and ideological regress of the nation,” warranting the claim that urban consumer culture “had become increasingly immoral and degenerate and threatened both individuals and the nation” (pp. 328–29).

Physical reconstruction “proved to be more illusionary than real”: “However desolate the imperial capital looked to observers in September 1923, it was not a blank slate” (p. 329). “Reconstruction became a hotbed of contestation” and “foreshadowed many of the intense struggles between those who espoused the expansion of liberal parliamentary democracy and economy and those who championed—sometimes violently—an authoritarian high modernist and social management agenda in 1930s Japan.” City rebuilding “would end and be officially commemorated in March 1930,” but “the reconstruction of the popular mind and the Japanese people would have no clearly defined or celebrated end” (p. 329).

Uno Kōji supplies the closing irony: two weeks on he missed “this café and that café in the Ginza” (p. 330). Finally, “other crises—imagined, constructed, and real—would be employed to secure larger ideological, political, economic, and social ends,” culminating in the Greater East Asian War, when the state would “condemn private property to construct air defense firebreaks” (p. 331).

## Course relevance

- **Disaster as cultural text.** Schencking supplies a worked method — read a natural event as a construction revealing “the political structures, economic systems and social orders” of its society (p. 297) — transferable to 1855 Edo, 1891 Nōbi, 1995 Kobe, 2011 Tōhoku.
- **The hinge between Taishō democracy and the 1930s.** The reconstruction fight is framed as a rehearsal for the later conflict between “liberal parliamentary democracy” and an “authoritarian high modernist and social management agenda” (p. 329).
- **Direct point of contact with Andrew Gordon.** Schencking cites Gordon’s *Labor and Imperial Democracy in Prewar Japan* (p. 298 n. 11) on the post-quake massacre and suppression, and independently reaches a Gordon-style conclusion about urban consumer culture.
- **Urban planning and land politics.** Gotō’s ¥800 million (1921) and ~¥4 billion (1923) plans, the 10 per cent uncompensated-take rule and the 9.4 per cent actual readjustment (pp. 314, 322) — evidence of why Japanese urban modernisation fell short.
- **Spiritual reconstruction as a through-line.** The 1923 Imperial Rescript, the “three ken” song, thrift campaigns and eventual wartime consumption controls (pp. 310, 327, 331) bridge Taishō moral reform to wartime national mobilization.
- **Rural–urban tension.** Diet members insisting “the countryside, not Tokyo, was the true heart of Japan” (p. 321) link this reading to the rural-distress literature and the 1930s agrarian crisis.
- **Complicates Taishō “democracy.”** Universal suffrage appears as a means of creating stakeholders and overcoming selfishness rather than as liberal triumph (p. 324). Beard’s authoritarian aside (p. 325) unsettles the standard progress narrative.
