## Section 1 — chapter opening to the first subhead (PDF pp. 1-9 of 24; ebook 84-92)

**Thesis.** The war “became a global moment of reflection on the legitimacy and structures of the imperialist world order” (p. 1); it “was interpreted throughout the world as the first victory of an Asian nation belonging to the yellow race over a major white and Christian Western empire.” (p. 1). The frame is the global debate on race, civilization and progress, “the three legitimizing pillars of the imperialist world order” (p. 1).

**The paradox.** “Japan fought the war with Russia over control of Korea and Manchuria. It achieved its military victory partly as a result of the support it received from the Western superpower of the time, Great Britain.” (p. 1) Hence the question: why did nationalists who saw the victory as “strengthening the pax Britannica in the Far East” still treat it as a turning point for “decolonization from British and French imperial rule”? (p. 1)

**Global public sphere.** Its reach: “the scope of the responses to the war was great proof that the circulation of news and ideas had already created a global public sphere” (p. 2). Effect: “It propelled all anticolonial nationalists to be more assertive and confident, strengthened the constitutional movements, and invalidated several key legitimacy discourses of the Eurocentric world order.” (p. 2) Evidence: “Chinese nationalist leader Sun Yat-sen was amazed and pleased to receive congratulatory gestures and messages from Egyptians during his passage through the Suez Canal during the war.” (p. 3), and Zimmern told his Oxford students the war was ‘the victory of a non-white people over a white people’ (p. 3). Race and civilizational language therefore “became a transnational force, utilized in legitimizing and delegitimizing structures of international politics” (p. 3)

**How 'East' became a shared identity.** “Until the 1880s, most Ottoman intellectuals did not see Japan and the Ottoman world as part of the same general identity” (p. 3); after the 1880s Ottomans read the world through *Şark ve Garp* (East and West), and “nevertheless began to identify themselves with the destiny and situation of the colored People's” (p. 4, 'People's' [sic]). In Japan 'tôyô' began as a term “originally adopted from the Chinese concept of the eastern seas” (p. 4) and by 1905 “compelled even the most pro-Western Japanese intellectuals never to doubt that Japan was an Asian nation belonging to the yellow race and Eastern civilization” (p. 5). The 1890 test case is the *Ertuğrul*: “it did not lead to any discourse of Asian solidarity or any discussion of shared Eastern identity in the Japanese press” (p. 4) — 'sixty-nine Ottoman survivors' were repatriated by two Japanese battleships, yet “the civilized image of Japan in the eyes of assumed Western observers was a major motive in the large-scale governmental and nongovernmental assistance to the victims of the sunk ship” (p. 4). The mechanism was not affinity but “the shared engagement with the European center led to the emergence of a common Eastern identity between Ottoman Muslims and Japanese” (p. 5), because every Asian readership was wired into the same European-centred sphere.

**Wars as proofs.** They served as “test cases and proofs of the validity of the ideals and moral values of the conflicting parties” (p. 5); Japan showed that “a non-Western nation could achieve parity in military power with (and even a superiority over) a European empire after only three decades of successful reform” (p. 5).

**Delegitimising racial hierarchy.** The effect was “shattering the established European discourse on racial hierarchies once and for all” (p. 5); Abdullah Cevdet asked Gustave Le Bon “where European thinkers had erred in their placement of the Japanese at the bottom of the racial hierarchy” (p. 5). Aydin's target is “the trinity of Eurocentric world order at the end of the nineteenth century—namely, imperialism, modernity, and Orientalism/racism” (p. 6), and the war “clearly contradicted the racial arguments and the moral universe that had justified the world order from 1882 to 1905, and thus it could be utilized to invalidate the idea of Western invincibility” (p. 6)

**Ahmed Riza, block quote (p. 6):** “The splendid victory of the Japanese has proved the Christian world arrogant; that it is not indispensable for a people to embrace Christianity in order to acquire morality, civilization, and an aptitude for progress.”

**Civilisation as legal gate.** Each state held “full legal rights in the international society if it was deemed” ‘civilized’ by the core European countries (p. 6), so Iran, Siam, China and Ethiopia were “not qualified to earn full legal equality in international law” (p. 7); even Ottoman membership since 1856 did not make it equal (p. 7). The nationalist inference: “Thus some Asian nationalists maintained a parallel conviction that only military power and industrial modernization could save the weak Asian nations from the so-called mercy of the powerful Western nations” (p. 7)

**Constitutional wave and the ambivalence.** The model “inspired a wave of constitutional revolutions in Asia from 1906 to 1911” (pp. 7-8), yet “demonstrates the ambivalence of Asian admiration for Japan” (p. 8). Periodisation: the 'awakening of the East' “coincided, however, with the ultimate victory of Westernization and the idea of universal modernity” (p. 9)

## Section 2 — 'AN ALTERNATIVE TO THE WEST? ASIAN OBSERVATIONS ON THE JAPANESE MODEL' (PDF pp. 9-13 of 24; ebook 92-96)

“The rise of Japan as the only nonwhite power spurred an increase in Asian interest in the Japanese experience of modernization.” (p. 9) The 'secrets' of Japanese progress were “commonly attributed to Meiji state policies regarding compulsory public education, participatory politics, and the constitution, as well as policies of industrial development” (p. 10). Against the reading that “Japan could only be the exception that proved the rule of Asia's permanent backwardness” (p. 10), the 1911 Istanbul conference shows the stakes: “the emphasis on Japan's uniqueness could be translated into an argument of fatalism and predetermination” (p. 11)

**No alternative modernity.** Asian writing on Japan “did not offer any alternative to the view of modernity symbolized by Western societies” (p. 11); for “the more than ten thousand Chinese students who studied in Japan until WWI” (p. 12) it was the cheaper, nearer road to European modernity, and admirers saw it “not as an alternative to the West but rather as its most successful application” (p. 12), with Europe still 'the ultimate model of modernity and reform' (p. 12).

**Three reasons the model mattered.** (1) Shortcut: thirty years of selective state-led reform could reach the Western level (p. 12). (2) Tradition need not obstruct modernity: “the Japanese example showed that non-Western cultural and religious traditions did not necessarily have to be regarded as obstacles to modern progress” (p. 13) — though “the heritage that was preserved was more an invented image of traditional Japan than a reflection of the actual continuity of pre-Meiji Japanese culture.” (p. 13). (3) Anti-fatalism: “the rise of Japan engendered optimism that it was not too late for Ottoman, Egyptian, and Indian reformers to bring their own societies to modernity and international equality” (p. 13), down to the claim that “the Japanese could succeed, the Javanese could do it, too” (p. 14)

## Section 3 — 'DEFINING AN ANTI-WESTERN INTERNATIONALISM: PAN-ISLAMIC AND PAN-ASIAN VISIONS OF SOLIDARITY' (PDF pp. 14-20 of 24; ebook 97-103)

**Two features.** Deeper internationalisation of non-Western solidarity, and “new realist formulations of pan-Islamic and pan-Asianist solidarity” (p. 14); “Tokyo became a center in the network of pan-Asianist and pan-Islamic thought” (p. 14).

**Ajia Gi Kai.** Abdurreşid İbrahim and “several prominent Japanese Asianists, including Toyama Mitsuru, Inukai Tsuyoshi, and Uchida Ryohei, formed Ajia Gi Kai (Association for the Defense of Asia) in 1909” (p. 15); it was tied to Konoe Atsumaro's *Tōa Dōbunkai*, *Kokuryūkai* and *Gen'yōsha* (p. 15), aimed at branches in China, India, Persia, Afghanistan and Turkey for “Asia for Asians” (p. 15), and “succeeded in registering nearly forty members from different parts of the Muslim world in addition to more than one hundred Japanese members” (p. 15). İbrahim, Russian-born and “educated in Kazan, Mecca, Medina, and Istanbul” (p. 15), “stayed in Japan for seven months” (p. 16)

**Muslim modernists.** Mustafa Kamil (1874-1908) produced the “most notable Muslim publication on Japan”, the book “Shams al-Mushriqa (The rising sun)”, “written just before the Russo-Japanese War” (p. 16); his article on Europe and Islam was “published in the French daily Le Figaro in 1901” (p. 16). Gaspıralı's Cairo congress shows fragility: “the planning for future conferences became the victim of intergroup rivalries among Egyptian intellectuals. The most salient aspect of the first pan-Islamic congress was the unsupportive and cautious approach of the Ottoman government of Abdulhamid II” (p. 17). Tokyo's journals: Barakatullah “started the publication of Islamic Fraternity in the English language” (p. 17), and Hatano Uho's book appeared in Turkish “under the title Asya Tehlikede (Asia in danger)” (p. 17).

**Justifying transreligious solidarity.** Aydin's claim: “A primordial conception of civilizational legacy cannot explain the emergence of a shared identity exhibited by the members of Ajia Gi Kai.” (p. 18) Okakura Tenshin and Tagore had built pan-Asianism on a shared Buddhist legacy (p. 18), and an earlier 1907 body “had classified Muslims as one of the outsider occupiers of Asia, whose boundaries were defined by Buddhist legacy and the yellow race” (p. 18). Aydin's substitute basis: “The basis of Eastern-Asian identity was defined not by religion but by the historical experience with Western expansionism that each Asian society had shared since the mid-nineteenth century.” (p. 19) Shahbandar: “We do not want to unite with Easterners just for aggression against the Europeans but rather to save humanity” (p. 19).

**Programme and its thinness.** Ajia Gi Kai's founding text: “Because we Asians have common customs and manners, common spirit and character, we must make a hard struggle ourselves for the betterment and development of Asia.” (p. 20) İbrahim to a French traveller: “When there is might, there is no question of rights and truth.” (p. 20) Barakatullah on Dutch Java: “Our object in giving publicity to this account [of the barbarity of Dutch rulers in Indonesia] is simply to appeal to the conscience of the Dutch people” (pp. 20-21). On substance they agreed only “more in formulation than in detail, because what each considered genuine Asian values and Eastern spirituality relied on different cultural traditions” (p. 21)

## Section 4 — 'JAPANESE PAN-ASIANISM AFTER THE RUSSO-JAPANESE WAR' (PDF pp. 21-23 of 24; ebook 104-106)

Konoe Atsumaro's Asian Monroe Doctrine was defensive, “without making any grand claim to Japanese leadership in liberating Asia from colonialism” (p. 21), but 1905 “allowed the defensive concerns that motivated Konoe Atsumaro's Asian Monroe Doctrine to evolve into a confident assertion of Japan's regional hegemony and its overarching mission in the world” (p. 21).

**Discourse vs policy.** “Japanese policies toward Asia remained in harmony with the Anglo-Japanese Alliance” (p. 22). In Gotō Shinpei's September 1907 report to Itō Hirobumi, “helping Chinese leaders to create Tôyôjin no Tôyô (Asia for Asians) represented the true aim” of 'Great Asianism' (*Dai Ajia Shugi*), and “Itô also warned that such references to Asianism would cause a misunderstanding in the eyes of Westerners, leading them to associate Japanese power and policies with their prejudiced concept of the” ‘yellow peril’ (p. 22); Japanese leaders “cautioned that any kind of overt Asianist emphasis would threaten Japan's relationship with the Western powers and might provoke anti-Japanese views” (p. 22), demonstrating pro-Western diplomacy by “complying in 1909 with a request from the French embassy to expel a group of Vietnamese students” (p. 23), while “in 1910 Prime Minister Ôkuma Shigenobu wrote a preface to a translation of Lord Cromer's Modern Egypt” (p. 23).

**Two Asianisms, one slogan.** “Between these two perspectives, the idea of a dual Japanese mission was gradually born.” (p. 23) The missions were fused in “a popular formulation making reference to the” ‘harmony between East and West’ (*Tôzai Bunmei no Chôwa*), which “became both an explanation for the rise of Japan and its new national mission” (p. 23) — either “only Japan out of all Asian nations could achieve this synthesis, thus endorsing a civilizing mission toward Japan's colonies in Asia” (p. 23) or a global anti-colonial alternative (p. 23). Verdict: it stayed one of “the most popular but least clarified slogans of cultural nationalism in Japan” (p. 23)

## Section 5 — 'CONCLUSION' (PDF p. 24 of 24; ebook 107)

“The Russo-Japanese War was a turning point in the history of both modernist ideologies and anti-Western critiques.” (p. 24) Three aspects: “First, the Russo-Japanese War sealed the existing Eurocentric world order's loss of legitimacy. Anticolonial nationalists and intellectuals successfully utilized the Japanese victory to counter the earlier discourse of white race supremacy and the backwardness of Oriental cultures.” (p. 24) “Second, pan-Islamic and pan-Asianist visions became part of the realpolitik discourse of world politics.” (p. 24) The war and the constitutional revolutions also “established a consciousness of the era as the” ‘awakening of the East’, redefining 'Asia' and 'East' “in a reverse Orientalist strategy” (p. 24) — “the precondition for the interpretation of WWI as the” ‘decline of the West’, so that “recognizing the moment of 1905 can help us to rethink the subjectivity of the non-Western world in the transformation of imperial world order in the first two decades of the twentieth century” (p. 24)

## Course relevance (modern-Japan history, JAPN 2089)

- Reframes 1905 as a global intellectual event rather than a bilateral war — 'a global moment of reflection on the legitimacy and structures of the imperialist world order' (p. 1).
- Gives the Japanese institutional map of pan-Asianism (Ajia Gi Kai, Tōa Dōbunkai, Kokuryūkai, Gen'yōsha; Tōyama, Inukai, Uchida, Konoe Atsumaro) plus its Ottoman, Egyptian, Tatar and Indian interlocutors.
- Exposes the gap between Asianist discourse and Meiji policy: Itō on 'Great Asianism' and the 'yellow peril' (p. 22), the 1909 expulsion of Vietnamese students (p. 23), Ōkuma's 1910 preface to Cromer (p. 23).
- Tracks the semantic shift of 東洋 / 'tôyô' from a China-centred cultural zone to a racial-civilisational 'East' (pp. 4-5) — core vocabulary for teaching pan-Asianism.
- Supplies the reception history of Meiji as model: the 'secrets' of Japanese progress, 'Japanese selectivity', the 'good wife, wise mother' reading (pp. 10, 13), 10,000+ Chinese students in Japan (p. 12).
- Links the war to the constitutional revolutions (Iran 1906, Ottoman 1908, China 1911) and to the post-WWI 'decline of the West' (pp. 7-8, 9, 24): a periodisation spine for 1868-1945.
- Its central irony is a ready essay prompt: the 'awakening of the East' coincided 'with the ultimate victory of Westernization and the idea of universal modernity' (p. 9).
