## Part III. REVOLUTIONS (pp. 203–276)

### Ch. 6 — Neocolonial Revolution (pp. 203–224)

Dower's frame is that Japan already had a long experience of "revolution
from above," so American reformism was legible to a defeated populace. He
writes that "the prospect of experiencing a 'revolution from above' was
hardly unprecedented" (p. 203), and that MacArthur slotted himself into an
established political pageant as "the new sovereign, the blue-eyed
shogun, the paternalistic military dictator, the grandiloquent but
excruciatingly sincere Kabuki hero" (p. 203).

The chapter's core claim is the *neocolonial* character of the occupation:
administrators saw themselves as "neocolonial overlords, beyond challenge
or criticism" (p. 203), and the Supreme Commander himself never actually
saw the Japan he governed (p. 204). According to "one intimate observer,"
"only sixteen Japanese ever spoke with him more than twice, and none of
these was under the rank, say, of Premier, Chief Justice, president of the
largest university" (p. 205). Dower notes the occupation's openly
hierarchical texture — "the most redundant phrase in the defeated land" was
"*Shinchugun no meirei ni yoru*—By order of the Occupation Forces"
(p. 207) — and quotes Bowers's confession that "I and nearly all the
Occupation people I knew … were extremely conceited and extremely
arrogant and used our power every inch of the way" (p. 207).

**"Reevaluating the Monkey-Men" (pp. 213–217).** Reeducation had to begin
with the American public, since wartime propaganda had depicted Japanese
as "apes, 'jaundiced baboons,' or, most often, plain 'monkey-men'" (p. 213).
Dower paraphrases the instructional film *Our Job in Japan*, whose narrator
declares: "Our problem's in the brain inside of the Japanese head. … There
are seventy million of these in Japan, physically no different than any other
brains in the world, actually all made of exactly the same stuff as ours.
These brains, like our brains, can do good things … or bad things" (p. 215);
the film's refrain is that the Japanese must be made to read, speak and hear
"the truth" (p. 216). This, Dower argues, is "a near-perfect expression of
an American sense of righteous mission and manifest destiny, not to speak of
secular saintliness" (p. 216).

**"The Experts and the Obedient Herd" (pp. 217–224).** A parallel claim:
GHQ systematically excluded Japan specialists. MacArthur himself "was, in
many ways, the most ethnocentric of men," who told a Truman envoy that
"Oriental peoples suffer from an inferiority complex" leading to "childish
brutality" when they conquer and "slavish dependence when they lose"
(p. 223). Colonel Charles Kades — architect of the constitution — admitted
"I had no knowledge whatsoever about Japan's history or culture or myths.
… I was blank on Japan" (p. 223), and when Oppler confessed he had "no
knowledge of things Japanese," a colonel replied: "Oh, that is quite all
right. … If you knew too much about Japan, you might be prejudiced. We do
not like old Japan hands" (pp. 223–224).

### Ch. 7 — Embracing Revolution (pp. 225–253)

Dower opens with two sceptics of the democratic revolution: the filmmaker
Kobayashi Masaki, who found that "Japan had become extremely
democratic," but worried that "the conformism seemed just the same as
before the war, only then everyone had jumped on the militarist band
wagon" (p. 225); and Yoshida Shigeru, whose joke about GHQ was "Go
Home Quickly" (p. 225). Yoshida later regretted acquiescence: "There was
this idea at the back of my mind … that whatever needed to be revised after
we regained our independence could be revised then. But once a thing has
been decided on, it is not so easy to have it altered" (p. 226).

The "obedient herd" was glossed in Japan as *junpū bizoku*, "good morals
and manners." Asked whether such folkways would impede reform, a GHQ
section chief "imperiously retorted, 'Make them change their folkways'"
(p. 226). Dower stresses the popular *embrace* of MacArthur — a flood of
letters to GHQ (441,161 letters and cards processed by ATIS between
September 1946 and May 1951) and a preoccupation that even gave rise to a
genre analysts dubbed the "I-want-to-have-your-baby" letters (p. 233).

**"Intellectuals and the Community of Remorse" (pp. 233–239).** The
intelligentsia "assumed the mantle of being 'progressive men of letters'
(*shinpoteki bunkajin*)" (p. 233) — a turnabout that repelled many as
hypocrisy, since "precious few of whom had opposed the war" (p. 233).
Maruyama Masao named the mood a "community of remorse" (p. 234); Dower
describes the "joyous feeling of hope for the future mixed with deep regret
for the past" and the resolve to turn the "rationed-out freedom" of the
occupation into a spontaneous embrace of reform (pp. 234–235). Marxism's
appeal, the hero-status of released Communists such as Tokuda and Nosaka,
and the quest for a "modern self" all follow (pp. 235–237).

**"Democratizing Everyday Language" (pp. 251–253).** The Americanisation
of schooling produced loanwords — "*karikyuramu, gaidansu, hommu
rummu*" ("curriculum," "guidance," "home room") (p. 251) — and a wider
lexical revolution. Dower's emblematic image (p. 253) is the 1948
lexicographers who reported that the old slogan-terms had been obliterated
by the atomic bombs "along with the residents of Hiroshima and Nagasaki,"
and that the most popular new term was "democratization," to the point that
"one can't even receive rations without wearing a democratization suit."

### Ch. 8 — Making Revolution (pp. 254–276)

The most radical chapter of Part III. Dower notes that radical politics
"caught the victors by surprise" and that "Food May Day" (19 May 1946) and
the *seisan kanri* ("production control") movement confounded them
(pp. 254–255). His structural argument: "the victors contributed
unwittingly to the circumstances in which such radical activities
flourished by deciding to promote political freedom and social reform
without taking an active role in rehabilitating the economy" (p. 255).

Nosaka Sanzō's return produced the famous "lovable Communist Party"
and the claim that "we Communists are the true patriots and the true
service brigade for democracy" (pp. 255–256). Unionisation exploded:
organized workers "rose to around 5.6 million by the end of 1946 and
peaked at some 6.7 million in mid-1948, comprising by that date more than
half of the nonagricultural work force" (p. 257). Production control spread
from "Thirteen incidents … in January 1946, twenty in February,
thirty-nine in March, fifty-three in April, fifty-six in May" (p. 258).

**Turning against the left.** On 20 May 1946 MacArthur warned "that the
growing tendency towards mass violence and physical processes of
intimidation, under organized leadership, present a grave menace to the
future development of Japan," and condemned "excesses by disorderly
minorities" — rendered *bomin demo* (p. 265). The "placard incident"
captured the contradictions: Matsushima Shōtarō's mock-imperial placard
read "The national polity has been maintained. / I am eating my fill. / You
people, starve and die." with, on the other side, "Why are we starving no
matter how much we work? Answer, Emperor Hirohito!" (p. 266); he was
prosecuted for lèse majesté (pp. 266–267). MacArthur's cancellation of the
planned 1 February 1947 general strike ("the use of so deadly a social
weapon," p. 269) is the pivot; labour leader Ii Yashirō asked "What kind of
democracy is this?" and shouted "Japanese workers are not American
slaves!" (p. 269), later charging the Americans with "deceiving the Japanese
people with democracy only at the tip of their tongues" (p. 270). The
"reverse course" then runs through the Red purge — "some eleven thousand
activist union members in the public sector were fired between the end of
1949 and the outbreak of the Korean War on June 25, 1950," plus "an
additional ten to eleven thousand leftist employees by the end of 1950"
(p. 272). Yet Dower concludes the left left durable marks: enterprise
unionism, state interventionism, and the left's later role as "the staunchest
defenders, for decades to come, of the initial occupation ideals of
demilitarization and democratization" (p. 273).

## Part IV. DEMOCRACIES (pp. 277–442)

### Ch. 9 — Imperial Democracy: Driving the Wedge (pp. 277–301)

Dower's central paradox — "imperial democracy" — is stated flatly: the
occupation "chose not merely to detach the emperor from his holy war, but
to resituate him as the center of their new democracy" (p. 278). He argues
this "magical transmutation" vitiated accountability: "if the nation's
supreme secular and spiritual authority bore no responsibility for recent
events, why should his ordinary subjects be expected to engage in
self-reflection?" (p. 278). The new charter's "symbol of the State and of the
unity of the people" (p. 278) is read as a re-phrasing of the old "family
nation," leaving Hirohito "the supreme icon of genetic separateness and
blood nationalism" (pp. 278–279). Yoshida called MacArthur his country's
"great benefactor" precisely for protecting the throne (p. 279). The
mechanics of "driving the wedge" come from psychological-warfare
specialists, notably Brigadier General Bonner F. Fellers, whose "Psychology
of the Japanese Soldier" held that "the Japanese and the Americans are
today as different as if each had always lived on different worlds, separated
by hundreds of light years" (p. 280).

### Ch. 10 — Imperial Democracy: Descending Partway from Heaven (pp. 302–318)

Dower's most revisionist popular-opinion argument: polls showing
overwhelming support for retaining the throne "misl[e]ad if interpreted …
as revealing widespread enthusiasm or deep awe," because throughout most
Japanese the throne was "a facade" (*tatemae*): "the emperor was
invisible, a non-presence" (pp. 302, 304). Police dossiers are his evidence
— children singing a jingle about the palace burning (p. 303), and a
December 1945 intelligence assessment that "informed sources claim that
many people have reached a state where it is almost immaterial to them
whether the Emperor is retained or not" (p. 305).

### Ch. 11 — Imperial Democracy: Evading Responsibility (pp. 319–345)

The abdication debate and the "manifest human" tours. Dower documents
that abdication was seriously canvassed — Higashikuni recommended it,
Konoe raised it publicly, the poet Miyoshi Tatsuji published "The Emperor
Should Abdicate Quickly," accusing Hirohito of being "extremely negligent
in the performance of his duties" and "responsible for betraying the loyal
soldiers who laid down their lives in battle for him" (pp. 320–322) — and
that SCAP "opposed this emphatically" (p. 278; cf. p. 322). The imperial
tours (from 1946) recast Hirohito as a "manifest human," in Dower's coinage
(p. 330); the British expatriate Reginald Blyth drafted the rationale —
"The Emperor must reign, not rule" (p. 331) — and the tours consumed "165
days" and some "33,000 kilometers," reaching every prefecture except
Okinawa (p. 330). The chapter closes on the ex-sailor Watanabe Kiyoshi's
diary-memoir *Shattered God*, whose portrait of betrayed emperor-worship
("The emperor threw away his divinity and authority by himself and bowed
his head like a dog … the emperor died on this day," p. 340) Dower uses to
show the emotional wreckage the staged "humanity" left behind.

### Ch. 12 — Constitutional Democracy: GHQ Writes a New National Charter (pp. 346–373)

Dower calls this "probably the single most important accomplishment of the
occupation" (p. 346). The old Meiji charter was derided as an "unholy
hybrid" — "with Prussian tyranny as its father, and British representative
government as its mother … a hermaphroditic creature" (p. 346). The
government's stonewalling pushed SCAP to act: on 4 February 1946
Whitney told his staff that "in the next week the Government Section will sit
as a Constitutional Convention" (p. 360), converting a Daiichi Building
ballroom into a "great big bullpen" that produced a draft against
MacArthur's "three principles" — the emperor as dynastic head of state
answerable to the people; abolition of war and "No Japanese Army, Navy,
or Air Force will ever be authorized"; and abolition of the feudal system
(pp. 360–361). Dower's verdict: "The line between Supreme Commander and
Supreme Being was always a fine one in MacArthur's mind" (p. 361). Beate
Sirota, the 22-year-old Vienna-born Japan specialist, is singled out as the
drafting group's rare Japan expert (p. 365), and the secrecy is captured in
the aside that had Matsumoto's team glimpsed the ballroom "they would
have sobbed their hearts out" (p. 365).

### Ch. 13 — Constitutional Democracy: Japanizing the American Draft (pp. 374–404)

The 13 February 1946 confrontation. Whitney told Matsumoto and Yoshida
the government draft "is wholly unacceptable to the Supreme Commander as
a document of freedom and democracy," then chillingly greeted Shirasu with
"We have been enjoying your atomic sunshine" (pp. 374–375). His threat —
that acceptance was "your only hope of survival," framed as "the last
opportunity for the conservative group … to remain in power" (p. 375) —
forced capitulation. Matsumoto's rearguard defences ("a tablet sugar-coated
for the benefit of the masses," p. 376) and Kades's "we were vaccinated
against the threat of communism" (p. 376) illustrate the standoff; Dower
marks the moment "Matsumoto finally realized that, although others might
have lost the war, he had just lost the Meiji Constitution" (p. 377).
The Diet's pretense that nothing changed — Kanamori: "the water flows, but
the river stays" (p. 388) — coexists with genuine deliberation.
On **Article 9** Dower is precise about the ambiguity: the original text (as
submitted) renounced war "as a sovereign right of the nation" and declared
"land, sea, and air forces … will never be authorized"; the final Ashida
amendment introduced "In order to accomplish the aim of the preceding
paragraph," leaving "a miasma of ambiguity" over whether self-defence
armament is permitted (pp. 394–396). Yoshida insisted the article renounced
even the right of self-defence — "I think that the very recognition of such a
thing is harmful" (p. 395) — while government spokesmen confirmed that
"even a defensive war cannot be conducted" (p. 396).

### Ch. 14 — Censored Democracy: Policing the New Taboos (pp. 405–442)

Censorship as the structural underside of "democracy." Dower opens with
the banned song lyric "Everybody is talking about democracy, but how can
we have democracy with two emperors?" (p. 405). The "hero worship"
editorial of October 1946 was approved in Japanese but its English version
seized by Willoughby's military police as "not in good taste" (p. 406).
The Civil Censorship Detachment is quantified: "over six thousand
individuals nationwide" at peak; "330 million pieces of mail" spot-checked
and "some 800,000 private phone conversations" monitored (p. 407).
Censorship's own existence was itself taboo — publishers were warned that
"no publicity regarding censorship procedure is desired" (p. 407) and that
titles such as "Passed by censorship" "must not be made" (p. 408) — and it
ended as it began, under the order that "there will be no press release on the
termination of civil censorship" (p. 408). After 1947 the target shifted:
"the major target of censorship now was left-wing rather than right-wing
thought" (pp. 434–435); of the twenty-eight periodicals still under
prepublication censorship in December 1947, "only two were 'ultra-rightist'"
(p. 435). The absurd Thompson striptease case (editor sentenced to a year at
hard labour, paper suspended, 75,000-yen fine) shows the arbitrariness
(p. 434).

## Part V. GUILTS (pp. 443–550)

### Ch. 15 — Victor's Justice, Loser's Justice (pp. 443–484)

The Tokyo Tribunal is weighed against Nuremberg. Dower notes the Allies'
"hatred and hope" (p. 443), the invented categories of "crimes against
peace" (Webb: "there has been no more important criminal trial in all
history," p. 444; Röling: "international law was en route to banning war and
rendering it a criminal offense," p. 444), and the revenge behind them
(Hull's wish to bring the archcriminals "before a drumhead court-martial,"
pp. 444–445). The scale is documented: prisoner death rates of "4 percent"
under the Germans and Italians versus "27 percent" under the Japanese
(p. 446); around fifty local tribunals; and "a total of 5,700 individuals were
indicted for 'Class B' and 'Class C' war crimes. Of this number, 984
initially were condemned to death" (p. 447). The Tokyo charter's Article 5
defines the three crime categories (p. 456), and the majority judgment
upheld a "conspiracy to wage wars of aggression" retrojected to 1 January
1928, with 756 separate acts charged (p. 457).

**"Race, Power, and Powerlessness" (pp. 469–474).** Dower's most pointed
critique: "The trial was fundamentally a white man's tribunal" (p. 469) —
"only three of the eleven judges were Asians" (p. 469), and "It was
especially perverse that no Korean served as judge or prosecutor" though
hundreds of thousands had been brutalised as "comfort women," forced
labourers and conscripts (p. 470). Justice Pal's dissent is quoted at length:
the Western powers' interests in Asia "were acquired by such aggressive
methods"; "As a program of aggrandizement of a nation we do not like, we
may deny to it the terms like 'manifest destiny', 'the protection of vital
interests', 'national honour' … and may give it the name of 'aggressive
aggrandizement' pure and simple" (p. 471). Keenan's claim that Japan was
"determined to destroy democracy" is called "light-headed American
effusiveness" (p. 471).

### Ch. 16 — What Do You Tell the Dead When You Lose? (pp. 485–524)

Dower's pivot from victor's guilt to the Japanese experience of grief. The
chapter's organising question — "What do you tell the dead when you lose?"
— is answered by the contrast: "where the victors focused on Japan's guilt
vis-a-vis other countries and peoples, the Japanese were overwhelmed by
grief and guilt toward their own dead countrymen" (p. 486). Osaragi Jirō's
"apology to departed heroes" and its question "What can we do to ease your
souls?" (pp. 485–486) frame the theme; the non-Japanese dead "remained
faceless" (p. 487).

**"Buddhism as Repentance and Repentance as Nationalism" (pp. 496–504).**
Higashikuni's doctrine — "the collective repentance of the hundred million
(*ichioku sōzange*) is the first step in the resurrection of our country"
(p. 496) — is dismissed as evasion; Maruyama "compared the government's
'collective repentance' campaign to the cloud of black ink a squid squirts
out in its desperate attempt to escape a threatening situation" (pp. 496–497),
and a farmer objected: "There is no need to do repentance for something we
weren't in on. Repentance is necessary for those who betrayed and deceived
the people" (p. 496). The philosopher Tanabe Hajime's *Philosophy as the
Way of Repentance* (*Zangedō to shite no tetsugaku*, 1946) is Dower's test
case of a "repentance" that is "intensely nationalistic" (pp. 497–498);
Tanabe's confession — "Weak-willed as I was, I found myself unable to
resist [wartime thought control] … I can only lower my head and earnestly
lament my sin" (p. 497) — is set beside his claim that redemption lay in a
uniquely Japanese wisdom.

**"Responding to Atrocity" (pp. 504–514).** Victim consciousness "never was
transcended" (p. 504). Dower catalogues dissent from the innocent-bystander
stance: Tsuda Sōkichi's charge that the people bore responsibility for
"the fact that their intellectual ability was so weak as to be deceived"
(p. 505); a Nagano woman's "that stupidity, I think, is also a kind of crime"
(p. 505); the *Asahi* column on Nanjing — "it is shameful that not one line of
truth was reported in the papers" (p. 506). The revelation of Manila
atrocities produced extreme responses, including a mother's letter: "even if
such an atrocious soldier were my son, I could not accept him back home.
Let him be shot to death there" (p. 506). The collection *Testaments of the
Century* turned condemned war criminals into martyrs — "Monstrous
criminals to their enemies, they now became philosophers and poets to many
of their countryfolk" (p. 521) — which Dower reads simultaneously as an
antiwar gesture and "a smoke screen obscuring the horrendous reality of
Japanese war crimes and atrocities" (p. 521).

### Ch. 17 — Engineering Growth (pp. 525–550)

The "reverse course," presented as the collapse of the reform ideal under
Cold War pressure: "The notion of a genuinely democratic revolution — from
above, below, or anywhere else — seemed more and more … a dream within
a dream. Before the occupation ended, the Japanese media had dubbed this
dramatic turn of policy the 'reverse course'" (pp. 525–526). Polling
evidence: 1948 majorities still positive, 1949 negative, with majorities
fearing renewed war (p. 526). The "Oh, mistake!" incident (a thief's
catchphrase) becomes the chapter's emblem of a society teaching itself to
dismiss the recent past "as just a mistake" (p. 527). Dower shows the
cultural counterrevolution (facetious lyrics, return of *chambara*, *Gone
With the Wind*, pp. 527–528) and the policy reversal — SCAP ordered to
"not assume any responsibility for the economic rehabilitation of Japan"
(p. 529), yet consolidating control through the Economic and Scientific
Section and preserving wartime controls (p. 529). Big business's naivety is
documented ("Our friend is coming," p. 530; "the Americans and British
won't treat us badly and everything will go well," p. 530). The chapter
ends with remilitarisation under the Korean War — the "National Police
Reserve" as a "little American Army," with tanks written into manuals as
"special vehicles" (p. 547) — Yoshida's "twilight zone of rearmament"
(p. 548), MacArthur's recall on 11 April 1951 (p. 548), the *Asahi*'s
"Lament for General MacArthur" (pp. 548–549), and MacArthur's testimony
that the Japanese "were in a very tuitionary condition … they would be like
a boy of twelve as compared with our development of 45 years" (p. 550).

  p. 551) is likewise unlisted and is out of scope.
- **Page ±1.** Chapter start pages are from the Contents; most within-chapter
  page citations are taken from the running heads that survive in the
  extraction. Some running heads are missing or OCR-mangled, so a few page
  numbers may be off by one (e.g. the Fellers quote, the Blyth memorandum,
  the *Testaments* material). No citation here is invented; each is anchored
  to a visible head or to the chapter's page range.
- **OCR quality.** The extraction is imperfect. Systematic substitutions
  (`\y`→`w`, `y`→`v`, long-`s` artefacts) were normalised inside quotations;
  genuinely garbled place/name strings were checked against context only.
  Specific spans I could **not** fully verify: some endnote text (irrelevant
  here) and a handful of photo captions.
- **Numbers cross-checked vs. single-sourced.** The following figures are
  quoted as Dower gives them and were not independently corroborated against
  a second source: union membership (5.6m/6.7m), Red-purge totals (≈11,000 +
  10–11,000), ATIS letters (441,161), CCD staffing (6,000+), mail/phone
  surveillance (330m / 800,000), war-crimes indictments (5,700 B/C; 984 death
  sentences), POW death rates (4% vs 27%), imperial-tour totals (165 days /
  33,000 km).
- **Attribution.** Passages where Dower is *reporting* another's words are
  quoted as his sentences (e.g. Maruyama's squid metaphor, the *Our Job in
  Japan* narration); these are Dower's paraphrase-framed quotations, not
  necessarily the speaker's literal words.
- Publisher/title confirmed from the title page ("EMBRACING DEFEAT / Japan
  in the Wake of World War II", "W. W. Norton & Company / The New Press");
  the extraction shows a "PULITZER PRIZE" cover sticker but no ISBN or
  publication-year line on the visible pages.

## Course relevance

- **Defines the pivot of the course.** Parts III–V take the story from
  collapse (Chs. 3–5, sibling digest) to the institutional settlement of the
  occupation — reforms, emperor, constitution, censorship — a natural hinge
  for any JAPN 2089 week on the occupation.
- **"Revolution from above" as a course-long theme.** Dower's argument that
  the Americans *imposed* democracy autocratically is the standard scholarly
  counterweight to triumphalist accounts, and pairs directly with the Meiji
  "civilization and enlightenment" top-down reforms.
- **Emperor Hirohito and war responsibility.** Chs. 9–11 and 16 supply
  evidence and quotations (Fellers, Blyth, Miyoshi, Watanabe) for the
  contested question of the emperor's accountability — a perennial exam
  topic.
- **The constitution and Article 9.** Chs. 12–13 give the documentary
  chronology (Whitney/Matsumoto, the "constitutional convention," the
  Ashida amendment) behind Japan's pacifist clause and its later
  reinterpretations.
- **Democratisation vs. censorship — the paradox.** Ch. 14 is the key reading
  for teaching that occupation "democracy" coexisted with a vast, unacknow-
  ledged censorship apparatus and the Red purges.
- **Law, race and memory.** Ch. 15 links Tokyo to Nuremberg and foregrounds
  Justice Pal's critique of victor's justice; it is essential for
  comparative war-crimes and postcolonial discussions.
- **Grief and national memory.** Ch. 16 (Osaragi, Tanabe, *Testaments of the
  Century*) is the best single treatment of how Japan mourned its dead while
  deflecting guilt — foundational for later debates on Yasukuni and
  "victim consciousness."
- **Cold War and the "reverse course."** Ch. 17 explains why reform stalled,
  the zaibatsu/union settlements, and the remilitarisation that produced the
  Self-Defense Forces, tying the occupation to Japan's high-growth era.
- **Method.** Dower models the use of polls, police dossiers, letters to GHQ
  and censorship files — a template students can imitate when writing
  source-based essays.
