## Part One: Growing Up in Wartime Tokyo (pp. 81–87)

**Identity & family.** "I was born in October 1936, and my first clear recollections are those of the Nishi-Ōkubo neighborhood of Tokyo around 1939... Akira and I lived with our grandparents in their big house set in by a granite gate. I knew that my parents were in Paris, but never missed them" (p. 81). Footnote 1 names them: "Their parents Keishirō, a journalist, and Naoko, had left for Paris in 1939"; "Their maternal grandmother Tsukamoto Man raised Kyoko and Akira while their parents were in Paris"; "Their maternal grandfather Tsukamoto Tetsuzo (1881–1953) was a leading teacher of Chinese and Japanese classics and a prolific author" (p. 102).

**A multi-ethnic street.** "On the right was the Kibōsha (a nice-looking building used by Korean rights activists) and then the home of Yatchan" (p. 81).

**Militarised childhood language.** A neighbour boy's dictation test required the children "to write 'bu-un chōkyū o inoru' (I pray for your good martial luck)" (p. 81).

**Parents' return, July 1941.** "our grandparents took us, I think, to Tokyo Station, to meet our parents. It was July 1941" (p. 81). The mother first wooed them — "she said, 'Of course, I was trying my best to win your hearts'" (p. 82) — then became the disciplinarian: "But Mother proved to be stricter once she knew she had won her children's hearts. She liked good manners, decent language, and honesty" (p. 82). In the *kinako* episode, caught lying about eating soy flour, "She caught hold of me and carried me to the veranda, saying she would not have a liar in the house" (p. 82). Adult reflection: "Probably, she was fighting to establish rules for her children, who appeared to her to have been somewhat spoiled by their grandmother" (p. 82).

**Mother's health.** After giving blood and carrying her ill child home: "She had had a heart condition following my birth and was also ailing from beriberi... The doctor had warned that she might not survive, but she had chosen to give birth to me" (p. 82). "Due to rheumatism, she was partially paralyzed for long periods, mostly in winter, and notably between 1944 and 1946" (p. 82).

**Schooling.** At Nishi-Ōkubo Elementary School, "a short block away," she "used to see its flag from the second story of our house" (p. 82). Private tutoring by Maeda-sensei produced a telling mock interview: "One of his mock-interview questions was, 'What countries is Japan fighting against?' I had no idea. I said, 'France, America, China.' I was going to add 'Italy, Germany,' but I wasn't sure, so I didn't (!)" (p. 84). At the entrance exam "I was told to draw a picture of the Japanese flag... I felt dismayed when told to stop after just drawing the bamboo flagpole and an outline of the flag" (p. 84).

**Father.** Rarely home; at a farewell supper before returning to China, "either Shanghai or Nanjing," he told her: "'Promise me one thing. Learn to eat without chewing with your front teeth'" (p. 84).

**Move to Narimune.** "On April 1, 1944, we moved to Narimune in Suginami ward" (p. 84); footnote 2: "Narimune, in the western suburbs of Tokyo, was then mostly rice fields" (p. 102). At Suginami Daini Elementary School she corrected a teacher's spelling of her name: "I told her, 'It's Irie, not Irihe.' I shouldn't have" (p. 85). A classmate was excluded from evacuation: "Wada-kun... wrote with his left hand because his right arm was paralyzed. He did not take part in the student group relocation (*gakudō sokai*)... because, as Mother explained to me, his mother thought he would have a hard time" (p. 85).

**Scarcity & air raids.** "In the fall of 1944, school children were sent out to *jinja* (shrines) and the woods to collect acorns. Food was growing scarce, and acorns would help" (p. 86). "In August 1944, air raids began to strike big cities. By February 1945 we had only two or three morning classes at school and we were sent home whenever the air-raid alarm went off" (p. 86). The shelter: "Our shelter was a simply dug hole where there used to be a little strawberry patch. It was just big enough to seat three people. I was not scared by the place but never liked being there because it was damp and slugs crawled about" (p. 86). "On December 3... we heard bombing noises that shook the ground. I felt uneasy when Mother handed me a toy whistle and told me to blow it if the shelter collapsed and I was being buried alive" (p. 86). "I was also agitated when she said, 'If incendiary bombs come through the roof at night, don't rush to the shelter but cover the fires with quilts and step on them; you are old enough to do this'" (p. 87). Blackout: "we lived in a pitch-dark city; we were not allowed to put on lights at night for fear that enemy airplanes would spot us" (p. 87).

**Key reflection.** "Adults may know how to respond to an abnormal situation, but a child can only accept it as perfectly normal and lasting. I recall the faces of my younger friends at home, faces of acceptance and abandonment. As I later recalled with shock, neither the two-year-old across the street nor the four-year-old next door ever smiled in those days... I never thought of this as abnormal" (p. 87).

## Part Two: Evacuation to the Countryside (pp. 87–97)

**The evacuation.** "In August 1944, Akira, then a fourth grader, left for Osamura Sanada, Chiisagata-gun, Nagano Prefecture. That was when systematic *gakudō sokai*... began" (p. 87); "children in the third grade and higher were sent away, even from Narimune, an area of many rice paddies and bamboo groves" (p. 87).

**Departure, 19 Feb 1945.** "Mother sent me for a haircut, had me rest on the couch..., and gave me a special supper of rice with a slice of fish, a feast the likes of which I had not seen in months" (p. 87). "Schoolmaster Yoshizumi-sensei led a file of fifty children on a fifteen-minute walk to the railroad station... a night train to a station named Ueda in Nagano Prefecture" (p. 87). Arrival at Sanada, where "mulberries were cropped short and kept like bushes for the silkworm industry" (p. 87).

**The dormitory.** "The dormitory, a converted inn... over two hundred children lived with four teachers from the Tokyo school and ten locally hired room mothers, ages sixteen to twenty-two" (p. 88). Roll call was military: the head boy said "*Bangō!*" (Count off), "like soldiers do in the army" (p. 88). She names "*enko sokai* (evacuating to join country relatives)" (p. 88) and notes of one homesick girl, "She was the only child I saw cry" (p. 88).

**Ideology at table.** Children "joined hands and recited a poem to thank the gods of heaven and earth," the tanka "When you pick up chopsticks, taste the grace of heavenly and earthly gods, as well as the favors of parents, teachers, and older students" (p. 88), "followed by a *sokai* song written by the empress... 'You are the ones to shoulder the next era; grow up strongly and correctly by moving to the countryside'" (p. 88).

**Hunger, disease, discipline.** "We had rice mixed with barley or soybeans at first... many children including myself had become too weak to digest soybeans" (p. 89). "Many children suffered from malnutrition and diarrhea" (p. 89). "A fifth grader was scolded in front of everyone at a morning assembly for having soiled the bathroom. He died some time afterward. The teacher who scolded the boy was known to be patriotic and tough" (p. 89). During field labour, "a third grader... complained, 'I hate war. I hope we lose quickly.' A twenty-year-old teacher... gently replied, 'Perhaps you shouldn't say that aloud'" (p. 90).

**Farm labour.** *Mugifumi* (treading barley): "I was afraid of stepping on them but learned that it made them stronger" (p. 90). Of relaying rocks by hand with her friend Hiroko: "I suddenly felt a human sensation: all I touched was the rock that I put in her hands, but I felt the warmth and resiliency of human flesh — with its tenderness, toughness, and thickness — under the rock's weight" (p. 90).

**Songs & propaganda.** She flags the colonial repertoire — "'The Seven Seas' and 'Festival in the Woods'... The latter two songs were colonialistic, meant to excite children about Asia and the South Pacific" (p. 92) — and glosses a lyric: "'Omoshiroku' probably means both 'amusingly to spectators' and 'having fun themselves'; I'm afraid that either way it's insulting" (p. 92). Ishikawa-sensei's non-martial songs "might have" been introduced "because we looked so serious and devoid of humor" (p. 92).

**Ethnicity & discipline.** Satoko-chan, a Korean girl, was slapped repeatedly for returning late: "This was the only time I saw a teacher slap a girl" (p. 95). Selden's adult suspicion: "I wrote it here with some reluctance, fighting a suspicion that occurred to me for the first time that her being Korean might have made it easier for Ishikawa-sensei to slap her" (p. 95). Satoko's whispered parody: "*Satoko Satoko to baka ni suru, onaji kome kutte doko chigau*" — "You say Satoko, Satoko making fun of me; how can we be different, we eat the same rice" — of which "although meant to be a joke, it was too sad to be funny" (p. 95).

**Peer militarism.** "some older students were more militaristic than the teachers" (p. 95). A boy slapped on the face "said, 'I sincerely apologize' (*Mōshiwake arimasen!*). I thought this reflected the army manner" (p. 96); "'sumimasen'... was not good enough because the angry officer might say 'Do you think you can get away with sorry'" (p. 96).

**The war's end from afar.** "My father returned from China in June 1945" (p. 97); the parents visited in late July. Told of her happiness at seeing them, "a sixth grader snapped at me: how could I be happy when everyone was mourning for the Okinawans. She had just learned about Okinawa's final suicide battle" (p. 97). When the parents wanted to take the children home, the head master "yelled at them that it would mean killing their children. 'It's better for the family to die together in Tokyo,' my father said. They prevailed" (p. 97).

**Reflection.** "To children my age, war was a fact that was taken for granted. I never expected the war to end or that I would rejoin my parents... Even if there were little study, little hygiene, little food, and much physical work, children took such things for granted" (p. 97).

## Part Three: Return to Tokyo in the Aftermath of War (pp. 97–100)

**The ruined city.** "Getting off the train in Tokyo, I saw an empty field... the only thing I saw was a burnt bank safe" (p. 97). "Grandmother looked at us, speechless and pale. She thought we were ghosts. She had seen many corpses and ghosts following the March and April air raids; besides, we were ghost-thin from undernourishment" (pp. 97–98). The return feast: "slices of beautiful tomato from the yard and cornmeal rolls, an unbelievable feast after the starvation diet in the countryside" (p. 98).

**Hiroshima.** "My cousin in the first grade, and her family lived in a small town near the city of Hiroshima... Both of her parents became quite ill later from exposure to radiation. With diarrhea, vomiting, and lost hair, they were each bed-ridden for an extended period of time" (p. 98). Her uncle's account: "he saw a girl about thirteen or fourteen, heavily burned, her face swollen like a balloon; she took two or three slow steps toward him, her arms raised, and then collapsed" (p. 98).

**The surrender.** "At noon on August 15, 1945 my grandfather and father were at work. My grandmother, mother, brother, and I crossed the street to a neighbor's house to listen to what we were told would be an 'important radio broadcast.' It was the emperor's address to the nation regarding the surrender. When we returned home after the one-minute announcement, my mother sat us down and told us what it meant" (p. 98). Mother: "'Japan lost the war... The American Occupation will start soon, but you need not worry'" (p. 99); she had "witnessed what happened when the Germans entered" Paris in June 1940 (p. 99). Father: "Japan's defeat was due to a lack of learning and to ignorance of the international situation," and "Americans are gentlemen; there is nothing to worry about" (p. 99).

**School's explanation.** The schoolmaster "withdrew, accepting responsibility for having cooperated with the war effort," and the vice-master, "who seemed to me to have been more nationalistic," became the new schoolmaster (p. 99). He told the children: "'Japan made mistakes. Every one of us was at fault — we adults as well as you children... try our best to reconstruct our country and rejoin the nations of the world, this time as a peaceful country'" (p. 99). The new catchphrase — "'peace and reconstruction'" — superseded the old — "'determination unto death'" (pp. 98–99). Selden's critique: "Now we had to be baby-like, as if we could change overnight... encouraging children to be babies kept us from thinking about what had gone wrong. Anyway, children had been trained as little adults during the war" (p. 99).

**Occupation gestures.** A man named Sekiya Isoroku told them "that Emperor Hirohito presented himself before General MacArthur and told him to do whatever he wished. I reported this to my mother, and she scoffed, 'What's great about that? He did such a terrible thing'" (p. 99). "when teachers fluffed our hair and sprayed DDT in 1947, that too had been ordered by GHQ" (p. 99).

**Textbook blackening.** "My third-year class blackened portions of our *kokugo* (Japanese) and *shūshin* (ethics) textbooks with brush and ink" (p. 99); "pages that had references to Shinto myths, the Imperial Household, the Imperial Army, and the colonies" (p. 100). One banned story was that of Tajimamori: "Apparently, the story had been banned because of the phrase 'although he was Korean'" (p. 100). Reflection: "I now find the blackening of the textbooks much more poignantly symbolic than I did then: it is as if the authorities believed that the act could wipe out the wrong ideas and wrongdoings of the past. Blackening pages neither changed children's mentalities nor obliterated the past; this was not a meaningful substitute for taking responsibility" (p. 100).

**A postwar play.** In March 1946 the *gakugeikai* (arts festival) was held; she played the nighthawk in Miyazawa Kenji's "The Night Hawk Star" (p. 100). Shimamura-sensei, who wrote the class play, gave candy on graduation: "'One is for yourself, one is for your parents, and the remaining one is for your sibling'... I think Shimamura-sensei was really saying, 'None for the country. None for the emperor'" (p. 100).

## Part Four: Reflections (pp. 100–101)

Prompted by "the 1995 Smithsonian exhibit of the Enola Gay and the suppression of the exhibition on the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki" (p. 100; footnote 6, p. 103):

- "These dichotomies are typically intended not for learning about war atrocities on both sides but for justifying, minimizing, or absolving one or the other" (p. 100).
- "A Japanese should not feel less compunction about the rape of Nanjing because of Hiroshima; likewise, no one should feel better about Hiroshima because of Pearl Harbor" (p. 100).
- "It is disrespectful to reduce humanity to numbers, and... many deaths were slow and cruel" (p. 100).
- "'Is it okay to kill only a few? Is it okay to kill slowly?' as poet Ishihara Yoshiro asked"; "The writer Hayashi Kyōko mentions a Nagasaki factory worker who survived the bombing for a day or two. He was in such pain that he tore his flesh" (p. 100).
- "In Tokyo, my grandmother witnessed forty air raids in one and a half years before her neighborhood was finally flattened" (p. 100).
- Thesis: "all city bombings are cruel, and every war is cruel; there is no such thing as a good war. What I see in Tokyo, Hiroshima, and Nagasaki is humans harming fellow humans, involving all types of people: scientists, political and military leaders, weapon makers, bomber pilots, soldiers, POWs, neighbors, relatives, and friends" (p. 100). Last line: "And when children do not smile, there is something wrong" (p. 101).

## Afterword by Akira Iriye (pp. 101–102)

- "Because I was two years her elder, I preceded Kyoko as a resident at the Nagano hotel turned into a refugee camp for school children to escape the anticipated saturation bombing of Tokyo" (p. 101).
- "Neither during our stay at the camp nor later did we discuss our experiences. This may have been because... we were too hungry from lack of anything to eat and too exhausted from daily work outdoors to have the energy... Perhaps such conversations may be possible only in peace" (pp. 101–102).
- "I have forgotten, or have chosen to forget, all such conversations with my parents and teachers" (p. 102).
- Biography: parents returned "in the spring of 1941, after having been away from Japan for four years"; "My father went to China as a correspondent for the Domei Press"; "I spent ten months (September 1944 to the end of July 1945) in a horrible environment of hazing and lice (but no rice)" (p. 102). His own diary was confiscated by roommates (p. 102). "Our father had returned from Nanjing in late May in what would turn out to be the last ship carrying Japanese from China that was not sunk by U.S. submarines" (p. 102).
- Verdict: "the war was an unmitigated disaster from beginning to end, and the wartime relocation experience was the worst phase of my life... I started keeping a diary on August 19, 1945. Its daily entries reflect a sense of relief, even joy, at Japan's defeat" (p. 102).
- Warning: "It comes as a shock that some of the younger generation in contemporary Japan seem to have a more benign image of the long, disastrous war, even to the extent of justifying the nation's aggressive acts against its neighbors... Our task is to connect, not divide, the world" (p. 102).

## Course relevance (JAPN 2089, modern Japan)

- **Primary-source counterweight to the historiography.** Dower and Gordon synthesise the war from above; Selden gives a lived, child's-eye home-front account — shelters, blackout, acorns, toy whistle, *monpe* — that makes "total war" concrete.
- **The imperial subject & everyday propaganda.** The dictation line "I pray for your good martial luck" (p. 81), the empress's *sokai* song (p. 88), and colonial songs "meant to excite children about Asia and the South Pacific" (p. 92) show ideology administered through school and chorus.
- ***Gakudō sokai* and evacuation geography.** Narimune → Sanada, Nagano (pp. 87–88), with a dormitory of "over two hundred children," is a case study of a process usually treated only statistically.
- **Food, scarcity, public health.** "too weak to digest soybeans" (p. 89), malnutrition, lice and skin disease (pp. 89–91) supply social-history texture behind the military narrative.
- **Air war and civilian suffering.** The memoir bridges the Tokyo firebombing (Note 4: "sixty-four Japanese cities... destroyed by bombing and napalm") and Hiroshima/Nagasaki (p. 98), making "strategic bombing" human.
- **The emperor and the surrender.** The "important radio broadcast" and the mother's response to Hirohito before MacArthur (pp. 98–99) dramatise contested meanings of the *Gyokuon-hōsō*.
- **Defeat, textbook blackening, memory.** The blackening of *kokugo*/*shūshin* pages (pp. 99–100) and Selden's charge that "encouraging children to be babies kept us from thinking about what had gone wrong" (p. 99) speak to Occupation censorship and the politics of responsibility.
- **Ethnicity and empire inside Japan.** The Korean-rights Kibōsha (p. 81) and the slapping of Korean Satoko (p. 95) raise the colonial-subject question within the metropole.
- **Memory and the next generation.** Iriye's Afterword (pp. 101–102) — the war as "an unmitigated disaster," alarm at later "benign" images, "Our task is to connect, not divide, the world" — bridges to postwar memory debates and the History Wars.
- **Historiographical reflexivity.** Selden's Part Four critique of "Hiroshima versus Pearl Harbor" dichotomies (p. 100) models the analytic move students must learn: refuse to "justify, minimize, or absolve."
