## 0. What kind of reading this is

Gordon (b. 1951) is Lee and Juliet Folger Fund Professor of History at Harvard and the author
of *A Modern History of Japan* (2002) — the standard survey — and of *Postwar Japan as History*
(1993). This article is **not** an archival monograph chapter. Its own footnote says it is "a
revised version of an interpretative essay based in roughly equal parts on my own research and
synthesis of other recent scholarship", first published in Japanese translation in the Iwanami
volume *Ajia Taiheiyō Sensō* 6 (p. 1 n.*).

That matters for how you use it. It is a **synthesis-plus-thesis** piece: expect a strong
argument, a wide evidentiary base drawn from other people's work (Sand, Young, Sato, Atkins,
Kasza, Dower, Hara/Havens), and a handful of places where Gordon's own primary research
(the sewing-machine advertisements and the consumer-credit reports) carries the weight.

It also makes it the single best *exam-ready* reading in the JAPN 2089 set: it gives you a
framework (transwar) that you can hang the other readings on.

## 1. The 60-second version

Japan's mass consumer society and commercialised leisure did not begin after 1945. They emerged
in the **1920s**, expanded in the **1930s on the road to war**, survived wartime mobilisation in
contradictory ways, and exploded again from **1947 through the 1960s**. Gordon calls this bounded
1920s–1960s span the **transwar era**, and argues two things:

1. **What rose was a middle-class way of life** — `chūryū` 中流, "mainstream" — whose pioneering
   figure was the male *sarariman* (salaryman) but whose defining consumer was **female**: the
   housewife, the working woman, the "modern girl."
2. **The mechanism was not only diffusion (more people getting more goods) but transposition**
   — differences that were not erased but moved "to a new postwar key of differences and
   tensions" (Kelly 1993: 192, quoted p. 2). At the start of the era a privileged 10–20% lived the
   modern life and everyone else consumed it as image; by the end a *majority* tenuously shared it
   and a new *minority* was left outside. Same structure of difference, new key.

His opening sentence states the thesis in one line:

> "This essay argues that the ascendance of middle-class lifeways, consumption and leisure
> prominent among them, took place through a process that involved the transposing of difference
> as much as the diffusing of sameness." (p. 1)

And the punchline about the war:

> "The spread of modern mass consumption and commercialized leisure, that is, persisted and even
> accelerated in Japan both despite, and in some ways because of, depression and war." (p. 4)

## 2. The vocabulary you must be able to define

| --- | --- | --- |

| ***renzoku-setsu*** 連続説 | The "continuity thesis" version of transwar used by Nakamura Masanori (2005, pp. 5–9): continuities across the war, extending to the end of the 20th century. | ***chūryū* 中流** | "mainstream", middle-class. The cultural standard, not just an income bracket. | p. 1 |
| ***sarariman* サラリーマン** | The salaryman; from the 1920s the "most neutral and common" of several rival names for the new middle class, because it just described earning a salary. | **new middle class** | The early-20th-century pioneers of consumer society: education and occupation defined them, urban, employed by a big firm or a government office; at the bottom rungs, policemen and schoolteachers. | p. 1 |
| **reality gap / vicarious participation** | The gap between the modern life as image and as owned reality. Mass media let people "participate" by consuming words and images if not goods. | p. 2 |
| **wartime modernity** | Gordon's borrowing from "colonial modernity" (Shin & Robinson 1999; Miyajima 2004) for the 1930s surge: modern consumer life spreading *under* war mobilisation. | p. 2 |
| **the double life** (*nikijū seikatsu*) | The 1920s debate over Western and Japanese goods and practices coexisting in food, clothing and housing — tatami vs chairs, kimono vs Western dress. Gordon's point: the debate assumed modern values of efficiency and rationality and argued only over which side was "properly Japanese." | pp. 12–13 |
| **rationalization of daily life** | The 1920s/30s argument that consumer demand and consumer credit would *discipline* households (budgeting, planning) *and* raise living standards, strengthening the "national people's economy" (*kokumin keizai*). | pp. 9–10 |
| **installment credit** (*geppu hanbai* 月賦販売) | Buying on time — the article's central mechanism. Singer introduced it; Janome copied it; it was revived in 1948–49 and by the late 1950s was "the method of choice." | pp. 5–6, 9–10, 17–18 |
| **"custom" vs "traditionalism" vs "modernity"** | Gordon's three-way distinction. *Custom* = locally generated, non-commercial, unbranded daily life, especially rural (Embree's Suyemura). *Traditionalism* = the politicised, state-propagated ideology of quintessentially Japanese practices. *Modernity* = the regime of goods, images and mass media. All three coexisted. | p. 14 |
| **"luxury is the enemy"** (*zeitaku wa teki da*) | The 1940 anticonsumption slogan; Tokyo put up 1,500 signboards that August. Gordon's test case for whether propaganda changed behaviour. | p. 15 |
| **"the mass middle class of 100 million"** (*ichioku sōchūryū*) | The 1960s self-image that Japan was one big middle class; Gordon calls it "a chimerical entity" — real as belief, false as fact. | p. 2 |

## 3. The argument, section by section

The article has six numbered sections. Read them as a single arc: *baseline → first surge →
war's contradictions → second surge → what the era was for.*

### §1 Daily Life and the Middle Class in Transwar Perspective (pp. 1–4)

Sets up all the machinery above. Three moves worth noticing:

- **The middle class is defined culturally, not just economically.** The `sarariman` is defined by
  education and occupation, but "Simultaneously and relatedly, a set of behaviors and desires
  centered more on women and on consumer life than on men and working life came to define the
  middle class" (p. 2). Consumption and leisure are how the class is *recognised*.
- **Modernity has a Janus face**: the tension "between values of discipline, investment and
  rationality — seen as necessary for the progress of individual, nation and empire — and values of
  individual liberation and pleasure" (p. 2). This is the article's engine; every historical phase
  is a different balance of the two.
- **Transwar is defined against three rival positions** (pp. 3–4):
  - *the "total mobilisation" school* (Yamanouchi, Koschmann & Narita 1995; Okazaki Tetsuji 1997) —
    the war is the transformative event; Gordon's complaint: it "overlooks the impact of the 1920s
    and early 1930s, in particular the response to the depression" (p. 3);
  - *Noguchi Yukio's "1940 system"* (1995) — same war-to-postwar link from the opposite political
    direction;
  - *the "leapfrog" view* — cosmopolitan interwar culture dies in the "dark valley," revives after
    1945 thanks to occupation reforms (Gordon names the Minami Hiroshi project, *Shōwa bunka
    1925–45*, 1987, and Havens 1978).
  Gordon's counter: the 1930s does not wither the modern life, it *spreads* it, and the 1920s
  baseline is where the pattern is set.

### §2 The Prewar Baseline: Mass Media and Modern Life in the 1920s (pp. 4–6)

The 1920s is where the modern life becomes **visible to everyone** while remaining **owned by
few**. Evidence of saturation: a 1919 Tokyo survey found 80% of working-class households took at
least one daily newspaper; the *Osaka Asahi* and *Mainichi* each passed 1 million daily circulation
by 1924; Kōdansha's *Kingu* (founded 1925) reportedly hit 1 million by 1926; the three big women's
magazines each claimed over 200,000 copies; movie theatres went from about 100 before World War I
to almost 1,300 by the end of the decade, with 1929 attendance over 150 million (p. 4).

The **gendering** is the key analytic move. The media world was populated by *sarariman* husbands
and, drawing equal or greater attention, a range of female roles: the privileged full-time
housewife studying cooking, sewing and department-store fashions, the working woman (typist,
teacher, seamstress, hairdresser), and above all the **"modern girl"** — photographed on the Ginza,
lampooned, celebrated, and fictionalised, e.g. in Tanizaki Jun'ichirō's *Naomi*, whose café-waitress
heroine "flaunted their sexuality and broke convention by choosing their own partners"; by the end
of the 1920s Tokyo's café waitresses "outnumbered licensed prostitutes" (p. 5).

Then the **reality check** — the numbers that make this article teachable:

- The only machine good that was truly mass-consumed by 1930 was the **bicycle**: about 500,000 in
  1910 to nearly 8 million by 1930, in a country of roughly 14 million households (p. 5).
- **Singer** sold roughly 500,000 household sewing machines in Japan 1903–1930 with ~800 shops and
  8,000 employees — but by 1930 "no more than 3% or 4% of Japan's households owned these machines" (pp. 5–6).
- Households owning **radios** in 1930: under 5%. Refrigerators, stoves, washing machines and
  phonographs had still less. Advertisers "actually held back from dangling them in the face of
  consumers" — a Denen-chōfu model house in 1924 had its electric stove, washing machine and vacuum
  cleaner *removed* by the executive in charge once he realised what the electricity would cost
  buyers (p. 6).

Gordon quotes Jordan Sand for the condition this produced: "the brief efflorescence of 'culture'
discourse in the early 1920s … marked the beginning of an incessant dialectic between consumers'
dreams and their frustrated reality that is itself an aspect of the modern condition" (Sand 2003:
226, quoted p. 5).

### §3 The Surge in Consumer Society on the Road to War (pp. 6–13)

The longest and most surprising section. **The 1930s, not the 1920s, is when the modern consumer
life became ordinary** — and the drive to war is part of why.

Numbers of the first surge: radio subscriber households rose tenfold, from 650,000 (1929) to
6.6 million (1941), peaking at 7.5 million in 1944; one in four urban households subscribed in the
early 1930s against one in twenty rural, but by early 1940 it was 56% urban and 21% rural (p. 7).
Movie attendance nearly tripled in the decade after 1930 to over 400 million at 2,363 theatres by
1940 — "an average of nearly six films per year per person" (p. 7). Louise Young's *Japan's Total
Empire* supplies the causal link: the Manchurian Incident of September 1931 launched intense media
competition ("growth spurt") that homogenised a national news market and blurred "news" and
"entertainment" as newsreels of victories preceded Hollywood features (p. 7).

American culture was enjoyed *while* the war was being fought: Chaplin's May 1932 visit drew huge
interest even as PM Inukai was assassinated; Japan's first professional baseball teams formed in
1934, the same year Babe Ruth toured 18 exhibition games in 12 cities with 65,000 at the Tokyo
opener (p. 7). Real modernisation of the streets: first subway (Tokyo 1927, Osaka 1933, the Ginza
Line linking Shibuya–Ginza–Asakusa by 1939) (p. 7).

**Women's dress is the strongest single thread in the article.** Hair salons spread (about 850 in
Tokyo alone by 1939); Western dress accelerated "on grounds of fashion and of purported
rationality (more efficient for factory workers, safer for evacuation in case of fire or
earthquake)"; by 1943 — at the height of the war — Tokyo had **1,282 dress shops, 572 of them owned
or operated by women** (p. 8). Sewing-machine sales spiked hardest between 1935 and 1940: about
910,000 machines sold in the 1930s, with ownership roughly tripling to close to 10% of households
(concentrated in cities) on the eve of the Pacific War (pp. 7–8).

**Consumer credit is the article's key mechanism.** Installment selling spread through the 1930s
(pianos, cars, suburban houses on mortgages along new commuter lines). A 1935 City of Tokyo survey
estimated 10,000 of 130,000 greater-Tokyo retailers offered it, financing 8% of the value of Tokyo
retail sales; the top goods sold on credit — men's dress, bicycles, cars, shoes, radios, sewing
machines, books, cameras, watches, jewellery, Western furniture — were, "with the one exception of
books … every item … a product of Western material and for the most part industrial civilization"
(p. 9).

Gordon then reads the 1929 Tokyo Chamber of Commerce report as *the* transwar ideology of
consumption, which fused two rationalisations: the firm's and the household's. Two consequences
"not only extremely useful in leading a disciplined life with planning for a monthly budget … it
also raises standards of living" (p. 9). The Chamber reportedly took its cue from E. R. A.
Seligman's *The Economics of Installment Selling* (1927), which also argued that credit
"'induce[d] the consumer to look ahead with greater care'"; Gordon notes the Tokyo authors were
even more forthright — they put the disciplining function in the preface where Seligman buried it
mid-book (p. 9). Matsunami Tadayuki's 1930 best-seller (*Installment Selling for Dummies*, loosely)
predicted it would "democratize mass access to commodities, spread human happiness more equally,
and relieve troubling class struggles" (p. 10).

**The sewing-machine advertisements (pp. 10–12)** are Gordon's own evidence and his best example
of the era's contradiction. In the *Asahi* of 9 January 1936 — six weeks before the 26 February
coup attempt — Pine-Janome ran "You! This year's resolution is to earn a ton with your woman's
hand! It's shop 'til you drop, as fast as you can," explaining that "the American woman is the one
who plays more, buys more, and earns more than any in the world," and answering the housework time
problem with "the problem is solved perfectly by bringing in science." Two years later the same firm
sloganeered "for you a bayonet; for me a sewing machine" (5 January 1938), and in 1939, beside an
Eve-and-serpent illustration, "pedal and multiply; save with a sewing machine" — a pun on the
state's "give birth and multiply" (*ume yo fuyase* / *fume yo fuyase*) (pp. 10–11, n. 12). On
8 December 1941, the day of Pearl Harbor, a Janome ad told readers the money saved on clothing
could go to reading and "self-cultivation" (p. 12).

The section closes on **two tensions** that define the era (pp. 12–13):

1. **Class**: a sewing machine cost two to three months' wages for a university graduate starting
   in a *zaibatsu* or government office. Resentment plus hope — the 1924 refusal of the Japan
   Salaryman Union by the labour federation *Sōdōmei*; Sata Ineko's "From a Caramel Factory" and its
   heroine gazing at sunny billboards from a shaded workroom; the labour organiser Tatewaki Sadayo
   recalling that sewing and cooking lessons drew more textile workers than "proletarian economics"
   — "they all said they just wanted to do what human beings do" (pp. 12–13). Gordon ties this to
   1920s social policy: containing the fall of policemen and schoolteachers into the dispossessed,
   quoting Hara Takashi's 1910 diary fear that "[i]f people like teachers and policemen take even
   one false step, they might become socialists" (p. 12).
2. **Identity — "the double life"**: the debate over Western vs Japanese in food, clothing and
   housing, conducted (Gordon stresses) *inside* modern assumptions of efficiency and rationality
   (pp. 12–13).

### §4 The Contradictions of Wartime Modernity and Consumer Society (pp. 13–16)

The state attacked consumption and the West; consumers largely did not stop. Film theatres peaked
in 1941 (2,471 theatres, nearly seven tickets per person, 460 million admissions) even as the
Finance Ministry restricted foreign films from 1937 and the Film Law of 1939 built a censorship
apparatus; in June 1938 the *Yomiuri* jovially announced the relaxation of "'closed-country' film
regulation" — "Western film fans, Rejoice!" (p. 13).

> "It would appear, then, that the Japanese people marched into the so-called 'dark valley' of war
> singing cheery movie theme songs as often as they hummed somber or strident military marches."
> (p. 13)

Gordon then makes his three-way distinction — **modernity and custom and traditionalism** (p. 14).
*Custom* is the rural world of Embree's Suyemura (1939) and Wiswell's women of Suyemura: modern
elements present (farm machinery, schools, reserve associations, occasional films) but daily
rhythm only partly penetrated. *Traditionalism* is the politicised ideology of Meiji-era purity,
crystallised in the Ministry of Education's 1937 *Kokutai no Hongi* — the article gives its English
title, 'Fundamentals of the National Polity', and quotes it as blaming Japan's crisis on Western
beliefs from individualism to communism.
Crucially: "the rise of traditionalism did not mean the death of the modern" (p. 14) — a "tense
dance."

The measures and their fates (pp. 14–16): 96 American films cleared on 4 December 1941, then
rescinded; dance halls banned end-October 1940; a war on jazz as "enemy music" — yet cafés switched
the victrola off "for a few days" and then turned it back up; April 1943, the Ministry of Education
forced the Tokyo Association of University Baseball to disband and banned intercollegiate games —
yet in October 1943 Keiō and Waseda played a farewell match (Waseda 10–1) and professional baseball
only suspended in November 1944; language purification ordered *sutoraiki*/*auto* replaced by
*yoshi*/*hike*, the "Japan Alps" renamed the "central range," and "mama"/"papa" discouraged;
rationing began in 1941 and a black market let those with means keep luxuries. Anti-credit ideology
got shrill — Matsumiya Saburō (1938) called "the American-style system of installment selling …
extremely cruel and completely at odds with the spirit of the Japanese nation," and sewing-machine
makers stopped offering installment credit in 1942 (p. 15). Production of domestic Singer
knock-offs fell from 140,000–150,000 a year in 1940–41 to 25,000 in 1943 and 2,150 in 1945 (p. 15).
"Luxury is the enemy" was on 1,500 Tokyo signboards in August 1940.

And the general law Gordon draws from all of it:

> "Policies and propaganda that were not reinforced by immediate material scarcity or direct
> military necessity had limited effect." (p. 15)

Even the 1939 "ban" on permanent waves was not absolute — a power-conservation order letting
officials "guide" excess; electric hair dryers were banned only in the second half of 1943, after
negotiations over the price the authorities would pay for the dryers' metal for military recycling,
with industry predicting 70–80% of beauty parlours would close (pp. 15–16). Jazz persisted so far
that kamikaze pilots awaiting orders wrote verses about it — "a guy who fights America listens to
jazz" (p. 16). Gordon's summary: "The centrality of calls to 'overcome modernity' were an
indication of how fully modern Japan had become" (p. 16).

### §5 The Surge in Consumer Society on the Road to Recovery (pp. 16–18)

The second surge begins, symbolically, with **words and images**: an insatiable postwar public
consuming books, magazines, films, radio — "dregs literature," political satire, English-conversation
manuals (p. 16, citing **Dower 1999: 87–202** as the evocation of this efflorescence). Then goods.

The **sewing machine** leads again: 1951 sales over 1 million a year (two-thirds exported), 1952
domestic sales over 500,000, exports 19% of machinery export revenue (the single most valuable
machine export); by the mid-1950s up to 500,000 young women were studying at sewing academies that
sprouted "like bamboo after the rain" (p. 17). Household electrification proper follows from the
mid-1950s. The 1960 penetration survey is the number to memorise (p. 17): **radio 89%, sewing
machine 72%, bicycle 66%, television 54%, camera 47%, electric washing machine 45%, electric fan
42%, electric rice cooker 38%, transistor radio 25%, phonograph 20%, electric refrigerator 16%,
motorbike 12%.**

Financing follows the 1930s pattern plus an innovation: manufacturers resumed installment selling
from 1948–49, and new finance companies and shopping districts issued **credit "tickets"** through
company personnel offices and neighbourhood associations — "a form of consumer finance which
anticipated the credit cards of several decades later" (p. 17); in Tokyo alone such ticket credit
reached ¥6.6 billion in 1959. By 1959, installment purchase shares of newly bought goods were:
**bicycles 80%, televisions 75%, cars 70%, motorbikes 68%, refrigerators 66%, sewing machines 60%,
washing machines 59%** (p. 18).

The rhetoric is explicitly *transwar*: the economist Kawauchi Mamoru (1952) called installment
selling "a great weapon for increasing demand and blazing the trail to new markets" — assisting
manufacturers "by bringing latent demand to the surface" while it "brings planning into daily life
and regulates consumption"; the Tokyo Chamber of Commerce in 1957 reproduced its own late-1920s
logic almost word for word (pp. 17–18).

### §6 Epilogue: From Transwar to Postwar (pp. 18–19)

The era ends in the 1960s. Following Yoshimi Shun'ya, the span from the 1960 Anpo struggle through
the 1964 Tokyo Olympics is "a decisive transformation of the era called 'postwar'"; Yoshimi's
example is Yamada Yōji's 1970 film *Family* (*Kazoku*), in which an impoverished Kyushu family is
stranded at the entrance to Expo '70, able only to glimpse the celebrating crowds — an image Gordon
likens to Sata Ineko's caramel-factory heroine, but with a difference: this family is marginal "in a
more profound way" (p. 18).

**Transposition, restated** (pp. 18–19):

| | Transwar (1920s–1950s) | Postwar (1960s–) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Material access | A minority, however growing, has a foothold; the majority shares only the cultural imagination (magazines, open-air radio, window-shopping, billboards) | A majority — "however, partially and tenuously" — incorporates the life of the modern middle-class consumer; a new minority is left out |
| The double life | Problematic, actively debated (one Western room and tatami elsewhere) | No longer problematic: the "Japanese" side is a minority/museum piece (apartment blocks from the late 1950s reverse the balance; since the 1990s typically no tatami) |
| Class/difference | Between potential middle class and dispossessed | Within a mass middle class, argued as "citizens" (*shimin*) or "consumer livelihood" (*seikatsusha*); cooperative unionism, class conflict in retreat |
| Measure | A sewing machine = 2–3 months of a graduate's wage in the 1920s–30s | A week's pay or less by the end of the 1960s |

Those left behind "feel their dispossession all the more acutely for the very fact that so many of
their fellow countrymen and women believe they hold a place among the middle-class millions" (p. 19).

## 5. Six ideas to be able to explain under exam conditions

1. **Transwar is a thesis about *change*, not sameness.** "Continuity" alone is the weak version;
   Gordon's strong version is a bounded era with a recurring causal dynamic — responses to
   depression, mobilisation and recovery improvising the institutions of the 1960s (he parallels
   Chalmers Johnson's MITI story for industrial policy with a consumer-economy equivalent, p. 3).
2. **Diffusion vs transposition.** Diffusion = the reality gap narrows. Transposition = the
   *framework of tension* changes: from minority/majority over access, to majority/minority over
   inclusion. Use Kelly's phrase.
3. **The Janus-faced modernity and consumer credit as its hinge.** Installment selling was sold
   *both* as discipline (budgeting, planning, "rationalization of daily life") *and* as pleasure
   (shop 'til you drop; "self-cultivation"). The Tokyo Chamber of Commerce and Seligman show the
   American lineage of the argument.
4. **Wartime modernity — war as an accelerant.** Three mechanisms (p. 8–9): war-induced recovery
   and deficit spending (Arisawa Hiromi in 1937 already spoke of a "miracle"); war-driven media
   expansion reaching ever further; and a "rationalization" discourse that modernised material
   daily life. Hence consumption rose "both despite, and in some ways because of, depression and
   war" (p. 4).
5. **The limits of cultural control.** Where scarcity did the work, austerity happened; where only
   propaganda did, it did not (p. 15). Permanent waves, jazz, baseball, installments and American
   films all show propaganda failing to beat desire.
6. **Gender is the load-bearing wall.** The middle class is defined through women's consumption:
   housewife magazines, the modern girl and café waitresses, Western dress as "rational," hair
   salons and sewing machines, housewives as credit-using budget managers, war widows as
   seamstresses. Any answer about Gordon that ignores gender is missing his argument.

## 6. How the reading answers likely essay questions

*Prompts the article is built to answer — and the skeleton for each.*

- **"To what extent did the Pacific War mark a break in modern Japanese social history?"**
  Thesis: a break in political economy and in the material conditions of daily life (1944–45
  destroyed consumption: production of goods collapses, rationing, black markets), but the
  *trajectory* of modern consumer life runs 1920s → 1930s → 1947–1960s. Use: two surges (p. 2);
  the 1930s spread (pp. 6–9); wartime contradiction (§4); 1948–49 revival of installment credit
  and the 1960 "bright new life" (pp. 16–18). Conclusion: 1945 is a rupture in *supply*, not in
  *desire* or in the ideological scaffolding of consumption.
- **"Was Japanese modernity Westernisation?"**
  Use the double life (pp. 12–13) and dress (§3, §6): the debate over kimono vs Western dress was
  conducted in terms of efficiency and rationality — i.e. Japanese modernity was argued *inside*
  modern assumptions. Add the lampooning of the modern girl, and the loops: wartime "people's
  dress" pushed a basically Western mode of dress out to the majority (p. 16, citing Inoue 2001).
- **"How did the Japanese state shape everyday life?"**
  Two-sided: social policy from the 1920s (social bureaus, health insurance, unemployment
  benefits) plus wartime mobilisation (Film Law, rationing, anti-luxury campaigns, language
  purification) — and the limits of both (pp. 12, 14–16).
- **"Was Japan a middle-class society by the 1960s?"**
  Gordon's answer: belief yes, tenuous reality yes for a majority, and the "100 million middle
  class" was "chimerical" — with a new excluded minority and a transposed structure of difference
  (pp. 2, 18–19).
- **"What does consumption tell us about gender in modern Japan?"**
  Sections §2–§3 plus §6: women as the archetypal consumers, as targets of state pronatalism
  ("pedal and multiply"), as owners of 572 of Tokyo's 1,282 dress shops, as war widows using
  sewing machines to earn, as "housewives" taught to budget on credit.

**Phrases to quote verbatim** (short, safe, and high-value): "the transposing of difference as much
as the diffusing of sameness" (p. 1); "both despite, and in some ways because of, depression and
war" (p. 4); "the rise of traditionalism did not mean the death of the modern" (p. 14); "Policies
and propaganda that were not reinforced by immediate material scarcity or direct military necessity
had limited effect" (p. 15); "The centrality of calls to 'overcome modernity' were an indication of
how fully modern Japan had become" (p. 16); "earlier differences … have not been entirely erased;
rather, they have been transposed to a new postwar key of differences and tensions" (Kelly 1993:
192, quoted p. 2).

### Silverberg ↔ Gordon: the same argument in a different theatre

This is the tightest pair in the set, and not by accident.

- **Silverberg is the prewar original of Gordon's key formulation.** Gordon's thesis turns on
  William Kelly's claim that "earlier differences … have not been entirely erased; rather, they
  have been transposed to a new postwar key of differences and tensions" (Kelly 1993: 192, quoted
  Gordon p. 2). Silverberg's closing thesis is the same move made for the 1920s: Kon and Gonda "open the
  way out of our binary crosscultural comparisons" and worked from "differences **within** the
  Japanese modern present" — "not between Japan (as an other, in relation to the United States) or
  between East and West" (Silverberg p. 50). Both refuse "Americanization" as an explanation.
- **They measure the same reality gap with different instruments.** Gordon uses circulation and
  ownership: 80% of Tokyo working-class households taking a daily newspaper in 1919; *Kingu* at a
  million by 1926; ~1,300 cinemas and 150 million admissions in 1929; nearly 8 million bicycles by
  1930 against 14 million households. Silverberg's ethnographers built the period's own measuring
  tools: Kon Wajirō's Ginza dress census (1,180 pedestrians counted; **67% of men in Western dress
  against 1% of women**, p. 38), his priced wish-lists of things people wanted but could not buy in
  Honjo-Fukagawa (pp. 40–41), the department store where "the middle-class customers were anxious,
  pushing" (p. 42). Put Kon's census beside Gordon's bicycle figure and the "reality gap" stops
  being a metaphor and becomes a number you can contest.
- **Where they part: the state and the direction of travel.** Gordon's story is desire surviving the
  state ("persisted and even accelerated … both despite, and in some ways because of, depression and
  war", p. 4). Silverberg's Gonda tracks the opposite drift, from *minshū goraku* (people's play) to
  state-organised *kokumin goraku* (national play) by 1941, and she insists that the bureaucracy's
  "active involvement with the construction and censorship of mass culture by the 1920s" must be
  taken into account (p. 50). She also indicts both her subjects: "Neither man had a sufficiently
  ironic stance as participant-observer of early Shōwa culture. Their claims to scientific
  objectivity were all too easily subverted by state interests" (p. 49), noting Gonda's wartime
  celebration of Nazi cultural policy. **Use her as the counterweight to Gordon's
  continuity-by-acceleration thesis** — in an essay, that tension is an argument, not a problem.
- **One precision to get right.** The café-waitress material — Tanizaki's *Naomi*, waitresses who
  "outnumbered licensed prostitutes in Tokyo" — is **Gordon's** (p. 5). Silverberg's 1992 article
  mentions the "modern girl" only as "the ideological construct" in a note (n. 5, p. 35); her
  substantive modern-girl essay is Silverberg 1991, 'The Modern Girl as Militant'. The 1992 gender
  analysis is the 67%/1% census, the "wanted by men"/"needed by women" pairing (pp. 40–41), the
  husband's and wife's bureau drawers (p. 43) and "More working women than men chose to go the
  movies" (p. 49). Do not attribute the café waitress to Silverberg.
- **Shared target:** both attack the "leapfrog" reading that jumps from Taishō modernity over the
  "dark valley" to the postwar. Gordon names Minami Hiroshi's *Shōwa bunka 1925–45* directly;
  Silverberg treats the 1943 *Kindai no chōkoku* ('Overcoming Modernity') roundtable as the
  terminus of the same dehistoricising move — "folk was substituted for class" (Silverberg p. 49,
  quoting Harootunian).

### Schencking ↔ Gordon: the earthquake draws Gordon's baseline

- Schencking's "culture of catastrophe" — the quake read as *tenken*/*tenbatsu* (divine punishment)
  and simultaneously as a *kōki* (golden opportunity), a discourse of "reflection, opportunism, and
  eventually contestation" (pp. 296–97) — is an attack on exactly the "self-centered, immoral, and
  extravagant lifestyles" (p. 295) that Gordon then documents, prices and dates.
- **The numbers line up:** a sewing machine in the 1920s–30s cost two to three months' wages for a
  university graduate entering a *zaibatsu* or government office (Gordon p. 12); that figure is what
  makes the anti-luxury politics of 1923–1940 socially legible.
- **Direct intellectual link:** Schencking cites *Andrew Gordon*, *Labor and Imperial Democracy in
  Prewar Japan*, among the works on the massacre of Koreans and the suppression of socialists and
  anarchists after the quake (Schencking p. 298 n. 11). That is the political violence running
  underneath Gordon's consumer boom — the thing Gordon's article brackets.
- **Silverberg dates her own argument by the quake:** "Capitalism and the earthquake had
  irrevocably sundered culture from nature and tradition" (p. 50). The 1923 disaster is the hinge of
  the whole set: it is where the reconstruction debate (Schencking), the constructivist ethnography
  (Silverberg) and the modern consumer baseline (Gordon) all begin.
- **A through-line of moralising about consumption:** Schencking's post-quake "spiritual
  reconstruction" — the 1923 Imperial Rescript, thrift campaigns, the idea that the "popular mind"
  needed reforming — leads by a straight line to Gordon's 1930s–40s "rationalization of daily life"
  and the 1,500 Tokyo signboards of 1940 declaring "luxury is the enemy" (Gordon pp. 9, 15).

### Aydin ↔ Gordon: where anti-consumption politics came from

1905 matters to this article less as a war than as a language. Aydin shows the victory read
worldwide as "a global moment of reflection on the legitimacy and structures of the imperialist
world order" (p. 1), shattering "the established European discourse on racial hierarchies" (p. 5) —
while, in his central irony, the "awakening of the East" coincided "with the ultimate victory of
Westernization and the idea of universal modernity" (p. 9); pan-Asianist and pan-Islamic visions
entered world politics (p. 24) even as Meiji leaders held to the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, with Itō
warning Gotō about "Great Asianism" and the "yellow peril" (p. 22) and the 1909 expulsion of
Vietnamese students (p. 23). That is the available vocabulary of the 1930s. Gordon shows what
happened when it met the department store: Matsumiya Saburō in 1938 denouncing "the American-style
system of installment selling … extremely cruel and completely at odds with the spirit of the
Japanese nation," the enemy-music campaign against jazz, "mama"/"papa" discouraged, "Japan Alps"
renamed (Gordon pp. 14–15). Aydin gives the ideological grammar; Gordon measures its purchase on
behaviour — which, in his account, was slight wherever scarcity was not already doing the work
(p. 15).

### Gordon ↔ Selden: the same years, measured and remembered

Gordon writes the sentence; Selden is the sentence lived. Gordon: the contradiction of wartime
consumption was "decisively resolved in 1944 and 1945 in a nightmare of scarcity, deprivation and
then physical destruction, which indeed sacrificed virtually all consumption and leisure" (p. 13).
Selden, born 1936: acorn-gathering for food in autumn 1944, air raids on the cities from August
1944, only two or three morning classes by February 1945, a dug-hole shelter with a toy whistle to
blow if buried alive, quilts to beat out incendiaries on the roof (pp. 86–87); evacuation (*gakudō
sokai*) to Sanada, Nagano in February 1945 with "over two hundred children" in a converted inn
(pp. 87–88); return to a burnt-out Tokyo and the surrender broadcast of 15 August 1945 (pp. 97–99);
the blackening of *kokugo* and *shūshin* textbook pages (pp. 99–100). Her most useful line for
exam purposes is the claim about what a wartime child's normality consists of — "a child can only
accept it as perfectly normal and lasting" (p. 87) — which is precisely the "reality" pole of
Gordon's reality gap. Her brother's Afterword (pp. 101–102), calling the war "an unmitigated
disaster" and warning against later "benign" images, then hands the argument to Dower.

### Gordon ↔ Dower: two halves of one transwar argument

- **Gordon cites Dower twice, and the citations tell you how to pair them.** First: postwar
  efflorescence — "Dower (1999: 87–202) superbly evokes this cultural efflorescence" (Gordon p. 17
  n. 21) — i.e. Dower's Part II, *Transcending Despair*, the chapters on *kyodatsu* exhaustion and
  the cultures of defeat. Second: Dower 2005 on "grass-roots satire" as evidence of the popular
  sense of betrayal by the emperor and the military (Gordon p. 15 n. 18).
- **The two scholars use the same formula at different levels.** Dower on continuity: "'Change,' in
  a word, was itself a continuity" (Dower p. 178), and catchphrases crossed 1945 as "valises" whose
  contents had changed (Dower pp. 164, 177). Gordon on the same phenomenon, at the level of goods
  and desires: diffusion plus transposition. **Dower = institutions, censorship and moral
  reckoning (occupied Japan's 'imperial democracy', the constitution, the Tokyo trial, war guilt);
  Gordon = the long material curve underneath.**
- **What each supplies to an essay on the occupation.** Use Dower for why reform "took root … as a
  lived experience and a seized opportunity" yet was also "the last immodest exercise in the
  colonial conceit known as 'the white man's burden'" (Dower pp. 23), and for the paradox that
  "democracy" arrived with an unacknowledged censorship apparatus (Ch. 14). Use Gordon for the
  desire side: the sewing machine's second boom (over 1 million a year by 1951), installment credit
  revived from 1948–49, credit "tickets" as proto-cards, and the 1960 ownership table (radio 89%,
  television 54%, refrigerator 16%). The occupation did not create consumer desire; it finally
  supplied the means to satisfy one built since the 1920s.

### The connective thread

All six readings treat **everyday life** as the place where state power, gender, class and empire
are negotiated — Schencking's reconstruction slogans and thrift campaigns, Silverberg's
street-census ethnographers, Selden's rations and blackened textbooks, Dower's censored democracy
and *panpan* street life, Gordon's sewing-machine advertisements and credit tickets. And all six
distrust the idea that Japan's modernity was simply borrowed: Aydin's "awakening of the East" that
was really Westernisation's victory; Silverberg's constructivists refusing the Japan/United States
binary; Gordon's domestically-made consumer modernity (Janome against Singer, Edo-era credit
practices reinvented as "uniquely Japanese" prepaid plans, wartime "Japanese dress" policy that
spread Western dress to the majority); Dower's Japanese making American reform their own.

**Three question-frames that use the whole set**

1. *Was Japan's twentieth century a story of rupture or continuity?* — Gordon and Dower give you the
   transwar answer (continuity across 1945, with the configuration transposed); Schencking and
   Silverberg anchor its beginning in 1923; Aydin and Selden supply 1905 and 1945 as the emotional
   bookends.
2. *Who defines Japanese-ness — the state or the street?* — Schencking's moral reformers, Aydin's
   pan-Asianists, Gordon's anti-luxury campaigns and language purifiers all lose, partly, to
   shoppers, cinema-goers and jazz fans; Silverberg explains why the ordinary was the right place to
   look; Selden shows what it cost when the state finally won, in 1944–45.
3. *Consumption as politics.* — Gordon is the reading that authorises you to argue that a bicycle,
   a sewing machine or a radio is as historical as a cabinet or a treaty.

## 8. Self-test (answer before you re-read; model answers below)

1. Define "transwar" in Gordon's sense, and state how it differs from the plain "continuity" claim.
2. What is the difference between *diffusion* and *transposition*, and what changes between the
   1920s and the 1960s under each?
3. Which single consumer durable was the only true good of mass consumption by 1930, and which
   class of goods mattered most for women in the 1930s?
4. Give three mechanisms by which war and depression *accelerated* modern consumer life.
5. Why is the sewing machine the best single object for describing the transwar era?
6. What did the Tokyo Chamber of Commerce (1929) and E. R. A. Seligman (1927) both argue about
   installment selling? Why did that argument suit both business and the state?
7. Explain Gordon's three-way distinction between modernity, custom and traditionalism.
8. What is his rule about when state propaganda *did* change behaviour, and what are the four or
   five examples that support it?
9. How does Gordon use Yoshimi Shun'ya and Yamada Yōji's *Family* to date the end of the transwar
   era?
10. Why does Gordon call the "mass middle class of 100 million" chimerical, and what does that
    imply about class in the 1960s?

**Model answers (condensed)**

1. A bounded era (1920s–1960s) defined by a recurring dynamic of change with identifiable causal
   elements — depression, war mobilisation, recovery (p. 3). Plain continuity simply says features
   persisted across 1945; Gordon says the *configuration* changed while the structure of
   difference persisted in a new key (p. 3).
2. Diffusion = the reality gap between image and ownership narrows (a minority → a majority).
   Transposition = the tension's framework shifts: from minority-inside/majority-dreaming to
   majority-inside/new-minority-excluded, and from the contested "double life" to a museum-piece
   Japanese side (pp. 2, 18–19).
3. The **bicycle** (500,000 in 1910 → nearly 8 million by 1930). For women, the **sewing machine**
   (about 910,000 sold in the 1930s; ~10% ownership by 1941), with Western dress, hair salons and
   dress shops as the surrounding consumer world (pp. 5, 7–8).
4. (i) war-induced recovery/deficit spending (Arisawa's 1937 "miracle") giving people the means;
   (ii) state-plus-commercial media expansion reaching further as it beat the war drums (radio
   subscriptions rising with war news, p. 9 n. 9); (iii) the "rationalization of daily life"
   discourse that legitimised modern goods as efficient and disciplined (pp. 8–9).
5. Because it was simultaneously a **tool of production** (home dressmaking, contract piecework,
   war widows' income) and an **object of consumer desire** marketed as science, glamour and
   American modernity — and it is the object whose price curve (2–3 months' wages → a week's pay)
   tracks the closing of the reality gap (pp. 10–12, 17, 19).
6. Both argued that installment credit *disciplined* consumers (budgeting, planning) while
   *expanding demand* and raising living standards. It suited business (markets, steadier demand)
   and the state (the "national people's economy," social stability, a bulwark against class
   struggle — Matsunami's "relieve troubling class struggles", p. 10).
7. *Custom*: locally generated, non-commercial, unbranded daily life, especially rural (Suyemura).
   *Traditionalism*: politicised, state-propagated ideology of quintessentially Japanese practices
   and morality (Kokutai no Hongi, 1937). *Modernity*: the regime of goods, images, media and
   credit. All three coexisted and interpenetrated; traditionalism's rise did not kill the modern
   (p. 14).
8. Propaganda worked where material scarcity or military necessity already enforced the behaviour;
   otherwise, "limited effect" (p. 15). Examples: permanent waves continuing after the 1939
   "ban"; hair dryers banned only in late 1943 and only to save electricity (with a negotiated
   buy-out of the metal); Keiō–Waseda baseball in October 1943 after the April ban, pro baseball
   only suspended in November 1944; cafés restoring jazz; kamikaze pilots' jazz verses; continued
   demand for American films (pp. 13–16).
9. Yoshimi treats the span from the 1960 Anpo struggle to the 1964 Tokyo Olympics as the decisive
   transformation of "postwar"; Yamada's *Family* (1970) shows a family *outside* the gates of
   Expo '70, able only to watch the celebration — which Gordon reads as the moment the difference
   structure is transposed: the excluded are now a minority watching a majority (pp. 18–19).
10. Because access was "tenuous and partial"; belief in the middle-class mainstream became the norm
    while some still stood outside it, so the chimerical quality *was itself* the era's defining
    anxiety (p. 2) and the dispossessed minority felt exclusion more acutely (p. 19).

## 10. Misreadings to avoid

- **Gordon is not saying consumer demand caused Japan's growth.** He carefully frames consumer
  demand as a *claim made by contemporaries* about the "national people's economy," and he notes
  that Japan's consumer economy "has not typically been seen as driving the nation's economic
  growth" (p. 9).
- **"Transwar" does not mean "nothing changed at 1945."** Materially, 1944–45 destroyed
  consumption (pp. 13, 15); the argument is about the trajectory and the structure of difference.
- **The modern life was never simply American.** Gordon stresses the domestic producers
  (Pine-Janome against Singer), local credit practices (Edo-era rotating credit, prepaid "layaway"
  plans), and the fact that wartime "Japanese" dress policy ended up *spreading* Western dress
  (pp. 9 n. 8, 16).
- **Don't treat the "dark valley" as darkness only for culture.** The article's sharpest point is
  that the 1930s, not the 1920s, is the decade when ordinary people first got the goods.
