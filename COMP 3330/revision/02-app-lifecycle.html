The app lifecycle

Chapter 2 uses "lifecycle" for the journey from app idea to running product to next version. This page builds that picture, then untangles it from the runtime lifecycle you will meet in Chapter 6, because mixing the two up is the most common way to lose marks on this topic.

Two different things called "lifecycle"

Fix this distinction first, because the course uses one word for two stories:

  • The development lifecycle (Chapter 2): how an app comes into existence and keeps improving: inception, design, development, stabilization, deployment, then tracking and updates. This is a project story. Timescale: months.
  • The runtime lifecycle (Chapter 6): the sequence of callback methods the Android system invokes on your activity as the user opens, switches away from and returns to your screen. This is a code story. Timescale: seconds.

Both are "managed" by someone other than you: the store manages your release flow, and the Android system manages your component's callbacks. That is the shared idea underneath the word: a lifecycle is a process you plug into rather than drive.

Why it matters

An exam question like "describe the app lifecycle" expects Chapter 2's phases unless it names activities or callbacks. If it names onCreate/onPause, it wants Chapter 6. Read the question's vocabulary before choosing a story.

Why a development lifecycle exists at all

Two facts force structure onto mobile projects. First, mobile software is slow to ship: the lecture quotes a Facebook release manager that "shipping mobile software is inherently different than shipping web software – the stakes are higher", with even small changes taking around 7 days of Apple review and users then needing to download the update. Second, mobile software is slow to fix: a bad release cannot be patched instantly; it goes through review again while users keep the broken version. So teams work in ordered phases with checkpoints, and they test early precisely because late corrections are expensive.

Source: COMP3330_Ch02_Lifecycle.pdf, slide 12; COMP3330_Ch01_Introduction.pdf, slide 43.

The five phases

Inception (strategy, then analysis and planning)

Inception defines and refines the app idea. Strategy questions: competitive advantage (are there similar apps? how does this one differentiate?), infrastructure integration (for enterprise distribution, what existing infrastructure does it extend?), value (what does it bring users? how will they use it?) and form / mobility (how does it work in mobile form? where do location awareness and the camera add value?). To shape functionality, define actors (roles within the application, often users) and use cases (actions or intents).

Analysis and planning then demands clear objectives (a specific purpose and marketing campaign; expected outcome; you cannot ship every service in one app, so identify the exact scope) and the target audience and their requirements (where you can reach them, their implicit needs, the on-the-go features they might demand: functionality and UI must line up with customer expectations).

Design: UX first, then UI

UX design is done with wireframes or mockups, so the experience can be designed before the real UI exists. Paper prototyping: hand drawings of interfaces: is used to communicate ideas between designers, developers, users and stakeholders at the start of user-centred design, and as a usability testing technique to observe interaction before interfaces are designed and developed. Platform guidelines matter (Apple Human Interface Guidelines, Android Design Guidelines): the app should "feel at home" on each platform. Hardware shapes UX too: iOS devices have no physical back button (hence the Navigation Controller metaphor), and a tablet's larger screen compresses what takes several phone screens into one.

UI design comes once the UX is settled: UX mockups are typically black and white; UI design adds colours and graphics. Popular apps have professional design, and a well-designed application may still look different on each platform.

Development

Development starts very early. Once the idea matures, build a working prototype: not an animation: to validate functionality and assumptions and to expose the true scope of the work.

Stabilization / testing

Stabilization works out the bugs from a functional standpoint, plus usability and performance, and should start early so corrections are cheap. The lecture's maturity ladder:

  • Prototype: proof of concept; only core functionality or specific parts work; major bugs present. Audience: key stakeholders.
  • Alpha: core functionality is generally code-complete (built, not fully tested); major bugs remain, outlying functionality may be missing.
  • Beta: most functionality complete, at least light testing and bug fixing done; major known issues may still be present.
  • Release candidate: all functionality complete and tested; barring new bugs, it is a candidate for release. May be distributed to customers who signed up for early access.

Notice the ladder is also an audience ladder: the app opens to more testers and feedback as it moves along.

Source: COMP3330_Ch02_Lifecycle.pdf, slides 3-12.

Deployment (distribution)

Once stabilized, publish. iOS options: ad-hoc deployment for development and testing on a limited number of provisioned devices (what happens when you deploy from Xcode), in-house deployment for internal corporate apps not on the App Store, and the Apple App Store itself. Certificates prove authenticity under public key infrastructure: a development certificate runs apps on devices during development, a distribution certificate signs apps for designated devices or store submission.

Android: all applications must be signed before distribution, using the developer's own certificate protected by a private key. Certificates can chain back to a certificate authority, but most developers self-sign; the main purpose is to differentiate developers and applications. Distribution is open: devices are not locked to one approved store, and third-party stores are generally allowed.

Performance tracking and enhancement

Judge success with analysis: number of downloads, total minutes spent per user, total revenue. The lecture's remark is the closing idea of the whole chapter: release is the first step of continuous evolution. Ship the minimum, then listen to what customers love, hate, desire, and say in critical reviews: all of it is scope for improvement.

Source: COMP3330_Ch02_Lifecycle.pdf, slides 13-16.

The runtime side: process state and "background"

Chapter 2's slides barely touch runtime, but the scope of the course expects you to hold a simple picture of it here, and page 6 develops it fully.

  • Each application runs in its own process (Android is multi-process; isolation uses Linux user and group IDs). Your code executes inside a process the system owns, not one you created.
  • The system manages app life cycles: different callback functions are invoked at different stages (onCreate, onStart, onResume, onPause, onStop, onDestroy: detailed on page 6).
  • In the lecture's component vocabulary, "background" first appears as a component label: an activity is "user interface / foreground" (buttons, lists), while a service is "no user interface / background" (music, download). So "background work" means work with no screen of its own.
  • The lifecycle note on slide 7 of Chapter 6 adds a second sense: an activity can be visible but not in focus when the screen shows multiple activities at once. Focus and visibility are separate axes, which is why the callbacks come in pairs (onStart = visible, onResume = focus).
Pitfall

"Background" is overloaded: no UI (a service), or not the thing the user is interacting with (the plain-English sense). The slides define the first. When answering, say which sense you mean.

Discuss

Take a position: the release-candidate stage is the first point at which real users should touch an app, and prototypes should be shown only to stakeholders. Evidence that would change your mind: cases where usability testing on paper prototypes with real end users (not stakeholders) at the design stage prevented a wrong feature set: the lecture itself suggests paper prototypes are a usability testing technique.

Worked mini-example: one app through the phases

Take a "class deadline reminder" app for HKU students. Inception: competitive advantage against calendar apps is HKU course-code awareness; value is fewer missed deadlines; form: push notifications and timetable import; actors: student, and (later) course coordinator; use cases: import courses, set reminders, snooze. Planning: scope to one faculty first. Design: paper prototype of the reminder screen tested with three classmates; on Android no back-button problem, but tablet layout would compress two screens into one. Development: a working prototype that imports one real timetable format. Stabilization: alpha when import works but notifications misfire; beta after two weeks of light testing; release candidate after all functionality is tested. Deployment: self-signed APK for testers, then Google Play. Tracking: downloads, minutes per user, reviews complaining about import: next version fixes import. That loop is the whole chapter in one story.

Check yourself

Q1

Your app is code-complete and built, but not fully tested, and major bugs remain. Which stage of the lecture's ladder are you in?

Alpha is defined as core functionality generally code-complete but not fully tested, with major bugs still present. Beta has had at least light testing and bug fixing.

Q2

Which pair of questions belongs to the inception phase?

Inception covers competitive advantage, value, form/mobility, and then audience and requirements. Certificates belong to deployment; callbacks are Chapter 6 runtime material.

Q3

In the lecture's component vocabulary, what does "background" describe?

Chapter 6 lists Service as "no user interface / background" (music, download) versus Activity as "user interface / foreground". The second option is everyday English, not the course's definition.
