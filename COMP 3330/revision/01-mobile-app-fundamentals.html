Mobile app fundamentals

What actually separates an app from a web page, why phones force design compromises, and how the app business works. Everything later in the course assumes this picture, and the exam expects you to reason from it, not recite it.

Start with the right mental model

A mobile app is a program you install once and that then lives on the device. A web app is a mobile version of a site that loads inside a browser like Safari or Chrome on every visit. That single difference explains almost every other distinction the lecture draws.

Because the app is already on the device, the pictures and code are already there too. The lecture's own example: transmitting a sunny weather icon costs about 2.9 KB, but transmitting the word "sunny" costs 5 bytes. A browser re-downloads the icon every time; an app sends only the text. And the lecture notes you can compress even further: there are only 39 possible weather icons, so each one can be named in 6 bits (since 25 = 32 is too few but 26 = 64 covers them).

The same reasoning covers everything else the lecture lists as an advantage of apps: personalization (based on interests, location, usage behaviour), notifications (push and in-app), access to device features (camera, contact list, GPS, phone calls, accelerometer, compass), offline working, and freedom in interaction design (tap, swipe, drag, pinch, hold). A cited 2024 article claims mobile users spend 88% of their time in apps and 12% on mobile websites, and that apps feel faster than websites.

Why it matters

"Convenient" here has a technical meaning: no URL typing, a UI sized for the screen, and a small download. When an exam asks why apps beat browsers, answer with mechanism, not adjectives.

The platforms under your app

Android is a software platform and operating system for mobile devices, built on the Linux kernel (Linux handles device drivers, memory management, process management, and networking). It was developed by the Open Handset Alliance (OHA), a consortium of 80+ technology and mobile companies led by Google, established in 2007 (Google, Intel, Samsung, Sony, HTC, Qualcomm, T-Mobile and friends).

Two runtimes matter. Dalvik was the original managed runtime, using interpretation and trace-based JIT compilation. ART (Android Runtime) arrived as a preview in Android 4.4 (KitKat) and fully replaced Dalvik in Android 5.0 (Lollipop). ART compiles whole apps to native machine code at installation (ahead-of-time compilation), which improves execution efficiency, lowers power use, and gives faster execution, better memory allocation and garbage collection, and more accurate profiling.

Security is enforced at the process level: Android is a multi-process system where each application (and parts of the system) runs in its own process, protected by standard Linux user and group IDs assigned to applications. A finer-grained permission mechanism restricts what a process may do, plus per-URI permissions for ad-hoc access to specific data. Official languages: Java and Kotlin; C/C++ runs as a native library packaged into the APK by Gradle (built with CMake) and called through the Java Native Interface (JNI).

iOS (iPhone OS before June 2010) is Apple's mobile OS, released with the first iPhone on 29 June 2007, used on iPhone, iPad, iPod touch, Apple Watch and Apple TV. Languages: Objective-C ("most common" on the slides) and Swift; C/C++ possible but not supported. HarmonyOS (Huawei, first released 10 September 2019) is a distributed OS that is not Android-based: its ArkCompiler compiles code directly to native machine instructions, and HUAWEI AppGallery is its app distribution platform.

Then there is the Android-derived crowd: Samsung One UI, Vivo OriginOS, OPPO ColorOS, Xiaomi HyperOS, OnePlus OxygenOS and Honor MagicOS are all Android-based vendor skins with their own ecosystem features. Historical platforms in the lecture: BlackBerry OS (QNX-based BlackBerry 10 in 2013; the 2015 Priv was Android-based), Symbian (C++ native, mostly Nokia, final release Nokia Belle Feature Pack 2 in October 2012), Windows Mobile (2000-2013) and Windows Phone (2010-2015), and KaiOS for keypad phones with HTML5-based apps.

Market share (August 2026, StatCounter): worldwide Android 67.61%, iOS 32.36%. China: Android 71.34%, iOS 28.58%. United States: iOS 60.68%, Android 39.29%. Read that as the reason "which platform?" is a real business question and not a coin flip: the answer flips by market.

Exam angle

Be able to say why Dalvik lost and ART won (AOT at install beats JIT traces for battery and startup), and why Android's process-level security exists (apps are isolated by Linux user/group IDs before permissions even apply).

The constraints that shape every mobile design

Four constraints come up again and again whenever the lecture discusses why an app looks or behaves the way it does.

  1. Client-side storage. App data sits on the device rather than on a server. Installing your app costs the user storage, and when storage runs out the user decides between your app and a family video. The lecture's blunt summary: on mobile, frequency of use matters more than perceived utility.
  2. Small screens and clunky controls. Nielsen is quoted: reading through a peephole roughly doubles the difficulty of understanding. Controls and keyboards are awkward; 56% of users in a cited study abandoned sign-up because registration took too long.
  3. Distraction. Phones are used in non-work settings, users drop off far more easily than on the web, and often never return, deleting the app after weeks unused. You need a strategy for bringing users back.
  4. Hard updates. Shipping a tiny change takes around 7 days of Apple review on average, users must download updates manually, and a bad change cannot be fixed instantly. Release management is therefore a first-class concern.

The lecture's conclusion is worth remembering as a stance: constraints can be catalysts. Small screens, limited storage, distraction and app stores force teams to build tighter products.

Eight UX principles, compressed

The lecture gives eight principles of mobile user experience design. Learn them as decisions, not slogans.

  1. Remove clutter. Allocate the user's attention; one primary action per screen. Many single-action screens beat one overloaded screen.
  2. Intuitive navigation. Good navigation is invisible and consistent, and always communicates where the user is right now.
  3. Consistent experience across devices. No dramatic difference between phone, tablet and desktop versions of the same app.
  4. Finger-friendly tap targets. Not less than 7-10 mm, with clear visual feedback.
  5. Legible content. Text at least 11 points at typical viewing distance; breathing room; standard fonts (Helvetica Neue for iOS, Roboto for Android).
  6. Visible elements. Colour and contrast carry meaning, and matter most for low-vision users. W3C contrast ratios: at least 4.5:1 for small text, 3:1 for large text (14 pt bold or 18 pt regular and up), computed as (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05) where L1 is the lighter and L2 the darker relative luminance.
  7. Design for hand position. Hoober's research: about 49% of users operate one-handed. Put top-level menus and frequent controls in one-thumb comfort zones.
  8. Minimize typing. Short forms, autocomplete, personalised defaults.
Discuss

Take a position: for an HKU society events app, Principle 1 (one primary action per screen) should win whenever it conflicts with the desire to show a full timetable on one screen. Defend your answer with the constraints section above. Evidence that would change your mind: usage data showing users abandon the flow when screens multiply.

Types of mobile apps

By category the lecture lists game, weather, social networking, navigation/map, music, news, entertainment, sports, banking, shopping, dining, search, digital books and more.

By nature there are three, and the exam loves this distinction:

  • Native apps are what "app" usually means: downloaded from the App Store or Google Play, sitting among the device's applications, launched by tapping an icon.
  • Web apps are the mobile version of a site, loaded in a mobile browser. Nothing to install, no storage cost. Ideal when you mainly want content or functionality on mobile and a real app is a bad fit or too expensive (the lecture's example: http://www.cs.hku.hk/).
  • Hybrid apps are built mostly with cross-compatible web technologies (HTML5, CSS, JavaScript) plus some native code for device functionality and a more refined user experience. They wrap "web views" inside native code. The payoff: only a portion of native code must be rewritten per platform.

By business model: free apps with in-app purchases, paid apps without in-app purchases, paid apps with in-app purchases. Chapter 3 adds the build-technology view (React Native, Flutter, Cordova, game engines): that is the next page's neighbour, so keep "how it is built" (native / web / hybrid / cross-platform) separate from "how it earns" (the three business models).

Compare

Web app: no install, browser-bound, re-downloads assets. Hybrid: installed, web code inside native wrappers, device access through API bindings. Native: installed, one platform's language and SDK only. Cross-platform toolkits (page 3) aim at native results from one codebase.

Where the lecture says apps are heading

Trends covered in Chapter 1: the sharing economy (Airbnb as a broker taking percentage service fees from both sides of each booking; Uber matching riders to the nearest driver and computing the fare, with the legality of the model challenged by governments and taxi companies; UberEATS on top); Web3 / decentralised apps using smart contracts and blockchain data, many monetising through NFT sales (Web3 games sell in-game assets as NFTs); AI (chatbots); VR and AR (Pokémon Go is the worked example: about $1.2 billion revenue and 752 million downloads per the cited reports); instant apps (run part of a native Android app without installing, taking no storage, supported by Android Studio from version 3.0); and mHealth (real-time and flexible service, more patients served; key feature requirements: time efficiency, user-friendly, simple, plus fun elements to sustain long-term use).

mHealth for the elderly is the lecture's open challenge: Hong Kong is ageing, the elderly are least likely to adopt new technology (only about two-thirds of adults aged 65+ own a smartphone), and the question is how to get them to pick up and keep using these apps.

Discuss

Take a position: the elderly mHealth adoption problem is a design problem (principles 1, 4, 5, 8 above) rather than a marketing problem. Evidence that would change your mind: a study showing well-rated, simple apps still fail to retain elderly users once distribution effort stops.

Check yourself

Q1

A weather app and its website show the same forecast. Why does the app transfer so much less data per update?

The lecture's argument is about what crosses the wire: components like images are stored in the app, only essential text is downloaded (2.9 KB icon vs. 5 bytes "sunny", and 6 bits for an icon code).

Q2

Which statement matches the lecture's definition of a hybrid app?

That is the "By Nature" definition: web technologies plus a portion of native code, so only that portion is rewritten per platform. The third option describes a web app.

Q3

According to the lecture, what replaced Dalvik as Android's runtime?

ART previewed in Android 4.4 (KitKat) and fully replaced Dalvik in Android 5.0 (Lollipop), trading interpretation plus JIT traces for AOT compilation at install.
